## 3. Corrections, tensions and methodological implications

R1 — Attribution heterogeneity, not a categorical null. Malle Table 2 (extracted): all studies random-effects I–E d=.095, 95% CI [.032,.159], I=.062, E=.023; standard subset I–E=-.001, I=-.093, E=.007. Overall 173 studies in 113 articles; nested samples and different operationalizations are explicit limits. Valence-adjusted I–E random-effects .042 and fixed-effects -.021; negative/positive event effects .241/-.158 under random effects. The aggregate can be small and nonzero while the classic universal cross-valence hypothesis lacks support. Walker's probabilistic critique addresses a different question (normative interpretation of constrained behavior), not proof that Malle's estimates are wrong. Compare likelihood of the same observation under preference and actual situational constraints; do not assume constraints eliminate all diagnosticity, or invent personal likelihoods.

R2 — Cognitive load and situational correction. Gilbert experiment 1: 47 female introductory psychology students; randomly assigned task conditions, additional topic-memory instruction. Mean recall 11.79 vs 10.30, while perceived trait-anxiety task-by-topic interaction F(1,43)=4.07, p=.05. Better recall did not imply greater use of the information for this judgment. Experiment 2 involved 63 students and preparation for a later speech; do not generalize a small experimental sample and specific load manipulation to universal automatic processing, high ability or emotional involvement. No individual mechanism is identified by these studies.

R3 — Attribution and satisfaction: model-dependence and marginal path. Fincham results p.278: manifest model causal paths satisfaction-to-attribution and attribution-to-satisfaction respectively husbands beta=-.23/-.20 (p<.05), wives -.12/-.19 (p<.10/.05). The first wives' path is not significant at .05 despite broad wording in the article. Responsibility cross-lags not significant in this specification. Latent attribution model reports different directional pattern. Another reported alternative model describes a husbands' coefficient -.23 with p<.10 as significant (p.276; not conventional .05). Do not silently repair p-values or conflate models. Association over time, mediation model and actual causal direction are distinct. Complete-case sample of 98 couples, observational design and potential stable/shared causes limit transfer; absence of significance does not establish no effect.

R4 — Perspective taking versus information acquisition. Eyal experiment 25 Table 14 absolute opinion-rating discrepancy means (control, perspective taking, getting even, odd, all): 1.46,1.71,1.15,1.21,.88. Control minus all=.58; taking minus control=.25; these are task-scale differences, not percentage increases in general accuracy. Partial information improved accuracy on discussed items, not undisclosed items; 20 opinion items deliberately independent. Confidence ratings did not differ conventionally (F(4,97)=1.01,p=.41); no significant correlations with the reported accuracy indices. Table 14 estimated correct counts and actual correct counts are separately measured; do not infer exact mean overconfidence by subtracting rounded condition means when denominators may differ. Distinguish instructed imagination from sophisticated longitudinal inference, voluntary opinion reports from verified latent motives, and accuracy gain from generalization across propositions. No obligation to disclose or repeated questioning implied.

R5 — Empathic accuracy is task- and target-dependent. Zikic 2025 reports weak correspondence between personal and standardized tasks; their comparison bundles familiarity, target and channel. Additional Zikic et al. Express yourself, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41556896/ (indexed original abstract) reports target variance and expressive accuracy contributions in 137 same-gender friend dyads. Both records describe 137 dyads; possible overlapping sample, not counted as independent replication without provenance verification. Murphy's approximately 1% task-variance result concerns self-report versus behavioral cognitive empathy, not all social skills; Sened's small correlation with satisfaction is not evidence that greater accuracy causes improved relationship quality.

R6 — Tracking versus directional bias. Directional bias original indexed Study 1 negative-emotion overestimation d=.52 [.36,.67]; positive-emotion contrast p=.066, not a proof of exact zero. Study 6A/6B are separate diary reanalyses with different populations/instruments; Study 6B methods report 80 couples. Prior ledger's 6A/6B contrast and nonlinear association with perceived partner responsiveness are retained as bounded author-reported results, not fully reproduced model estimates. Do not infer intent from intensity ratings or recommend deliberate misperception. Carry REL-X04 R2 unresolved sign/label contradictions in messaging-frequency moderation; no directional conclusion or prescription to increase messaging. Self-report criterion is not infallible. Daily reports and shared samples may be dependent.

R7 — Familiarity, consensus and criterion validity. Kenny's author synthesis https://davidakenny.net/ip/soagree.htm reports Kenny & West 24 round-robin studies, 118 variables, 2992 participants, self–other agreement .21 vs consensus .27; familiarity moderator negative in this relatively low-familiarity set. Connelly & Ones report higher validity with interaction/intimacy; author synthesis notes mixed findings, restricted range and trait visibility. These are heterogeneous estimands, not contradictory universal laws. Blackman & Funder's consensus finding is separate from self–other accuracy; Kenny's author synthesis reports increases for at least visible traits. Additional information may be redundant or non-diagnostic. A target effect is shared perception, not verified true trait; a unique relationship effect can include valid relationship-specific information and bias. No SRM decomposition from one isolated dyad; repeated reports alone do not identify it. Koehler's similarity-based inference findings show that confidence can be misleading in a particular task, not a universal inverse relation.

## 4. Revised operational rules

1. Separate observable behavior, opportunity, voluntary report, explanatory inference, criterion and future outcome. Evaluate person–situation alternatives relative to exact evidence, not universal attribution-bias assumptions.
2. Separate descriptive prevalence, perceived prevalence, actual/perceived approval, individual endorsement, sanctions and explicit agreement. Norm-consistent behavior cannot identify endorsement or motive.
3. Distinguish mental-state tracking, mean signed error, magnitude of error, agreement, confidence and predictive validity. Example mathematical distinction: estimates (3,4,5) against reports (1,2,3) track perfectly but have mean signed error +2; errors (-2,0,+2) average zero but mean absolute error 4/3. These are constructed metric examples, not empirical data.
4. Assess diagnosticity of cues, criterion adequacy, task/context, observer/target effects, dependence and disconfirming observations; familiarity may improve information and introduce expectations. Neither subjective confidence nor logical coherence establishes accuracy.
5. Prefer ordinary new information and voluntary clarification of the specific unresolved proposition; a self-report verifies expressed position but not all motives. Do not use intrusive tests, surveillance, pressure or manufactured situations.
6. Distinguish prediction from causal explanation; if rival hypotheses predict the same outcome, correct prediction does not discriminate them. Keep missingness and forecast failures in evaluation; avoid uncalibrated numerical personal predictions.
