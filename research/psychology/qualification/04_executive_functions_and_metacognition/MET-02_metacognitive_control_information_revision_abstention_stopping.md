# MET-02 — Metacognitive control: information seeking, revision, abstention, strategy selection, and stopping rules

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Establish how metacognitive monitoring is converted into control actions.

The block separates:

- uncertainty/confidence from the policy used to act on it;
- information seeking from information-search quality;
- sampling quality from search-termination quality;
- seeking information from habitually externalizing memory/search;
- confidence from expected value of information;
- change-of-mind from simple response instability;
- corrective revision from noncorrective revision;
- abstention from inability to answer;
- opting out from random giving up;
- stopping rules from objective problem solvability;
- help/advice seeking from helplessness;
- using others' confidence from blindly following others;
- strategy selection from first-order capacity;
- study allocation from memory monitoring alone;
- monitoring reactivity from beneficial metacognitive control;
- local control decisions from stable person-level self-regulation traits.

## Core rule

**Metacognitive control is the policy that maps monitoring signals such as confidence, uncertainty, error likelihood, and expected learning state onto actions such as seeking information, revising, abstaining, asking for help, allocating study time, changing strategy, or stopping. Good monitoring does not guarantee good control, and apparently good control does not prove accurate introspection.**

A generic control architecture is:

`first-order state / decision`
-> `confidence / uncertainty / error estimate`
-> `expected value of further cognition or information`
minus
`time / effort / opportunity / social / error costs`
-> `control action`
-> `new evidence / revised state`
-> `continue, commit, revise, abstain, outsource, or stop`.

## 1. Monitoring and control are distinct levels

### Monitoring

Estimate:

- confidence;
- uncertainty;
- error probability;
- learning state;
- need for more evidence.

### Control

Use the estimate to choose:

- continue;
- search;
- re-check;
- revise;
- ask;
- defer;
- abstain;
- stop.

Canonical rule:

**monitoring produces a signal; control chooses what to do with it.**

## 2. Accurate monitoring is neither necessary nor sufficient for optimal control

Good monitoring can fail to improve outcomes if:

- information is costly;
- the control policy uses the wrong threshold;
- revision introduces noise;
- the available extra information is low quality;
- action costs outweigh expected benefit.

Conversely, useful control can sometimes emerge from:

- learned rules;
- external feedback;
- environmental structure;
- habits

even when explicit confidence estimates are noisy.

## 3. Metacognitive control is value-sensitive

The same confidence level can rationally lead to different actions depending on:

- stakes;
- time pressure;
- information cost;
- error penalty;
- opportunity cost;
- availability/quality of further evidence.

Therefore:

`low confidence -> always seek more information`

is false.

Control requires a policy, not confidence alone.

## 4. Information seeking

Information seeking is an active attempt to reduce uncertainty or improve a decision before commitment.

Examples:

- viewing evidence again;
- sampling another option;
- searching online;
- asking a question;
- requesting feedback;
- consulting another person.

It is a metacognitive-control action only when linked to monitoring or uncertainty regulation; information seeking can also arise from habit, curiosity, or external instruction.

## 5. Confidence often guides information seeking, but context changes the mapping

Van Marcke and Desender (2025) causally manipulated confidence and found that trial-level confidence guided re-sampling, but task-level information seeking also depended on how under/overconfidence had been induced.

Thus:

**confidence is one driver of information seeking, not a sufficient policy variable.**

Prior beliefs about task difficulty/history can independently alter search.

## 6. Low confidence can drive search even when confidence is poorly calibrated

In ambiguous-news judgments, lower confidence predicted greater demand for more information even though confidence itself did not reliably track objective accuracy.

This produces an important distinction:

`confidence-sensitive control`
can coexist with
`poor metacognitive calibration`.

Canonical rule:

**a control policy can faithfully use a bad monitoring signal.**

## 7. Information seeking can therefore amplify error

If confidence is miscalibrated:

- high-confidence errors may terminate search too early;
- low-confidence correct judgments may trigger unnecessary search;
- search may preferentially expose the person to misleading evidence.

Thus:

**more information seeking is not inherently better metacognitive control.**

## 8. Information search quality has at least two components

Hu et al. (2026) distinguish:

### Sampling quality

Choosing **which** information to inspect.

### Termination quality

Knowing **when** enough information has been collected.

Metacognitive sensitivity predicted both in value-based decision making.

When working-memory demands were reduced, the relationship with termination quality persisted more strongly than the relationship with sampling quality.

Canonical rule:

**knowing what to sample and knowing when to stop are separable control problems.**

## 9. Search termination is not simply high confidence

Search can stop because:

- confidence reaches a criterion;
- expected information gain falls;
- time runs out;
- additional information is costly;
- decision stakes are low;
- alternatives become sufficiently discriminable.

Therefore:

**termination threshold != confidence level alone.**

## 10. Information-search policy should optimize expected information value

Useful search asks:

> Which next observation is expected to reduce decision-relevant uncertainty enough to justify its cost?

This links metacognitive control to:

- expected information gain;
- value of information;
- cost-sensitive sampling.

The exact computational implementation remains model-dependent.

## 11. Human search reduces multiple kinds of uncertainty

Large-scale analysis of more than one million search choices found that predecisional search reflected multiple drivers interpretable as reducing:

- structural uncertainty;
- estimation uncertainty;
- computational uncertainty.

Their use was adaptive and sequential, shaped by:

- expectations;
- instructions;
- experience.

Canonical rule:

**information seeking should not be modeled as one undifferentiated "need to know."**

## 12. Question asking is a special form of active information seeking

The 2026 Annual Review introduces the QuInS framework, emphasizing question asking as an iterative process for identifying and filling knowledge gaps.

Question quality depends on:

- identifying what is unknown;
- selecting an informative query;
- evaluating the answer;
- deciding whether further inquiry is needed.

Thus asking many questions is not automatically good metacognitive control.

## 13. Help seeking is not helplessness

Seeking help can be adaptive when:

- confidence is low;
- another agent has relevant expertise;
- information has positive expected value;
- cost is acceptable.

Avoiding help can reflect:

- overconfidence;
- social cost;
- low trust;
- autonomy preference;
- poor access.

Canonical rule:

**help seeking is a control policy, not direct evidence of low ability or dependence.**

## 14. Social information use depends jointly on own and others' confidence

A 2026 study found that people weighted others' confidence more strongly when their own confidence was low.

This can improve decisions when:

- the other source is competent;
- confidence is informative.

It can increase vulnerability when:

- others are confidently wrong;
- source calibration is unknown.

Canonical rule:

**social metacognitive control requires estimating both self-uncertainty and source reliability.**

## 15. Confidence can regulate whether feedback is sought or processed

Experimental probabilistic-learning work found that higher confidence was associated with:

- less willingness to pay for feedback;
- reduced neural/behavioral feedback processing;
- less belief updating.

The proposed mechanism was reduced expected informativeness of feedback.

Canonical rule:

**high confidence can rationally reduce feedback processing, but becomes harmful when confidence is miscalibrated.**

## 16. Search behavior can become habitual rather than confidence-driven

Bittner and Storm (2026) found that prior online searching increased future online searching without evidence that reduced internal confidence caused the effect.

They interpret this as possible habitual reliance on an external/transactive memory partner.

Canonical rule:

**external information seeking != uncertainty-driven metacognitive control by default.**

## 17. Externalizing cognition can be adaptive

Using:

- notes;
- search;
- reminders;
- calculators;
- other people

can reduce internal memory/control demands.

Externalization should not be treated as cognitive failure when it improves goal achievement.

The relevant question is:

**is the external support selected appropriately and used effectively?**

## 18. Change of mind

A change of mind occurs when post-decision information or monitoring leads to revision of an initial choice.

It can be driven by:

- continued evidence accumulation;
- detected error;
- external feedback;
- social information;
- reconsideration.

Canonical rule:

**change of mind is a control outcome, not evidence that the first decision process was irrational.**

## 19. Post-decision evidence can update confidence before changing the answer

Current evidence-accumulation models extend processing beyond the first response.

Possible sequence:

`initial decision -> continued evidence accumulation -> confidence update -> error signal -> possible revision`.

Thus initial choice and initial confidence are not necessarily terminal cognitive states.

## 20. Change of mind can be corrective or harmful

Revision can:

- correct an initial mistake;
- replace a correct answer with an error.

Therefore:

`more revision = better metacognition`

is false.

Useful control selectively revises decisions when expected accuracy gain exceeds revision cost/risk.

## 21. Confidence can create post-decision confirmation bias

The 2026 post-decisional review describes evidence that confidence can alter how later evidence is accumulated/interpreted.

High initial confidence may reduce openness to disconfirming evidence.

Thus confidence can influence both:

- whether further evidence is sought;
- how further evidence is processed.

Canonical rule:

**information exposure does not guarantee unbiased belief updating.**

## 22. Explicit confidence reporting is not always beneficial

A 2025 study found that requiring confidence reports in a perceptual task did not improve regulatory performance and could reduce final accuracy/change-of-mind correction under some conditions.

Therefore:

**forcing deliberate metacognitive reporting can itself alter first-order cognition and control.**

Measurement is not always passive.

## 23. Judgments of learning are also reactive

A 2025 meta-analysis of:

- 344 effect sizes;
- 175 independent experiments;
- 15,079 adults

found that immediate judgments of learning (JOLs) changed later memory performance on average, with substantial moderator dependence.

Canonical rule:

**eliciting a metacognitive judgment can change the object-level process being measured.**

## 24. Metacognitive-control research must therefore model reactivity

When asking participants to:

- rate confidence;
- make JOLs;
- explain reasoning;
- re-check decisions,

the measurement procedure may change:

- attention;
- encoding;
- evidence accumulation;
- strategy;
- revision.

Do not assume pure observation.

## 25. Abstention

Abstention means declining to commit to a first-order answer.

Examples:

- "I don't know";
- skip;
- opt out;
- defer decision.

Abstention can be rational when:

- confidence is low;
- error cost is high;
- abstention cost is lower.

Canonical rule:

**abstention is a meta-decision, not automatically a failure to decide.**

## 26. Abstention threshold

An abstention policy can be modeled as a criterion:

`answer if expected value/confidence exceeds threshold; otherwise abstain`.

The threshold can shift with:

- error penalty;
- reward;
- time;
- task difficulty;
- information availability.

Therefore abstention rate alone does not measure metacognitive ability.

## 27. More abstention can fail to improve accuracy

A 2025 perceptual decision study found that risk and difficulty increased opt-out behavior, but increased opting out did not improve overall error performance.

This demonstrates:

**monitoring uncertainty sufficiently to opt out does not guarantee strategically beneficial control.**

## 28. Opting out can itself impose cognitive cost

Choosing whether to:

- answer;
- skip

adds another control decision.

Under some conditions this additional demand can offset expected gains from selective responding.

Canonical rule:

**more control options do not automatically improve performance.**

## 29. Giving up is a regulated control problem

Ackerman (2026) emphasizes that efficient problem solving requires not only knowing when to submit an answer but also when to stop investing time.

Her 3-Stopping-Rule Model separates:

- confidence threshold for answering;
- confidence threshold for opting out;
- time limit.

Canonical rule:

**stopping/giving up is not simply exhausted effort or failure.**

## 30. Opt-out confidence can be distinct from opt-out frequency

Across Ackerman's experiments, opt-out frequency varied with:

- task;
- manipulation;
- individual/context characteristics.

Yet the confidence criterion for opting out was comparatively stable across several manipulations.

This illustrates that:

**behavioral frequency and underlying decision threshold are not the same construct.**

## 31. Stopping rules regulate global performance

Persisting indefinitely on one hard problem can reduce total performance when time/resources are limited.

A rational stopping policy may sacrifice one item to improve:

- total solved problems;
- expected reward;
- deadline performance.

Thus:

**persistence on each item != optimal self-regulation.**

## 32. Time pressure changes metacognitive control

When time is scarce, control may favor:

- early opt-out;
- easier items;
- lower information-search depth;
- different confidence thresholds.

A policy that appears "avoidant" under unlimited time may be globally adaptive under a deadline, or vice versa.

## 33. Strategy selection

Metacognitive control also selects **how** to perform a task.

Possible strategies include:

- retrieve from memory;
- calculate;
- search externally;
- use a mnemonic;
- simplify;
- switch representations;
- seek advice.

Strategy selection is distinct from first-order ability.

## 34. Strategy adjustment can be predicted by metacognitive ability

A 2025 adolescent study found that better metacognitive ability predicted more adaptive spontaneous task-strategy adjustment.

This supports a monitoring-to-control link.

It does not establish that metacognition is the only mechanism of strategy change.

Learning, task knowledge, and executive control also contribute.

## 35. Strategy choice is cost-sensitive

The best strategy is not always the most accurate possible strategy.

It can depend on:

- time;
- effort;
- stakes;
- reversibility;
- tool availability;
- skill.

Canonical rule:

**adaptive strategy selection optimizes goals under constraints, not abstract accuracy alone.**

## 36. Study-time allocation

Learning contexts make monitoring-control coupling explicit.

A learner can use judgments such as:

- difficulty;
- JOL;
- familiarity

to decide:

- what to restudy;
- for how long;
- when to stop studying.

But allocation depends on time pressure and learning goals, not monitoring alone.

## 37. "Study the hardest item most" is not universally optimal

Classic study-time research shows that when time is abundant, learners often allocate more time to difficult items.

Under severe time pressure, allocating time to easier/high-yield items can be more adaptive.

Canonical rule:

**metacognitive control should be evaluated against the global learning objective and resource constraint.**

## 38. Information seeking versus information avoidance

People can avoid information because it is:

- costly;
- emotionally aversive;
- decision-irrelevant;
- unlikely to change action;
- threatening to preferred beliefs.

Information avoidance is not automatically poor metacognition.

The normative question is whether avoided information had positive expected decision value.

## 39. Uncertainty reduction is not the only value of information

Information can provide:

- instrumental value;
- cognitive/uncertainty reduction;
- affective value;
- social value;
- curiosity/interest.

A 2025 study of information seeking found expected uncertainty-reduction utility predicted search in its context more strongly than several affective factors.

This should not be universalized to all information contexts.

## 40. More information is not always better

Additional evidence can be:

- redundant;
- noisy;
- biased;
- misleading;
- expensive.

Optimal control therefore requires both:

- starting search when useful;
- stopping before marginal information value becomes negative.

Canonical rule:

**information quantity != information quality or decision quality.**

## 41. Search quality depends on source selection

Good metacognitive control asks:

- Is the source reliable?
- Is it independent?
- Does it add new evidence?
- Is it diagnostic for the uncertainty?

Repeatedly sampling correlated sources can create false certainty.

This links metacognition to source monitoring and evidence methodology.

## 42. Confidence thresholds are context-dependent policies

A threshold appropriate for:

- low-stakes trivia

may be inappropriate for:

- irreversible/high-cost decisions.

Therefore:

**"confidence threshold" is not necessarily one stable personal constant.**

Even if one threshold shows stability within a paradigm, cross-domain generalization must be tested.

## 43. Metacognitive control and cognitive control are distinct

Cognitive control regulates task processing.

Metacognitive control uses estimates about cognitive states to choose:

- whether/how to regulate;
- whether to seek more evidence;
- whether to stop/revise.

They interact, but are not identical.

Example:

- cognitive control can maintain a task rule;
- metacognitive control can decide the rule/strategy is not working and initiate a change.

## 44. Metacognitive control and motivation are distinct

A person may accurately know that:

- more study would improve performance

yet choose not to study because:

- reward is low;
- effort/opportunity cost is high.

Therefore:

**good metacognitive knowledge != motivation to act on it.**

## 45. Metacognitive control and executive capacity are distinct

A person may choose an appropriate control action but be unable to implement it because:

- WM capacity is exceeded;
- inhibition fails;
- task switching is costly.

Conversely, strong executive capacity can implement a poor metacognitive policy efficiently.

Canonical rule:

**choosing the right correction and executing it are separate stages.**

## 46. Metacognitive Control Inference Gate

Before interpreting a metacognitive-control behavior, ask:

### A. Monitoring input

1. What monitoring variable is available: confidence, uncertainty, error signal, JOL, familiarity, feedback?
2. Is that signal calibrated/sensitive enough to be useful?
3. Is the signal local or global?
4. Is it prospective or retrospective?

### B. Control target

5. Is the action search, revise, abstain, ask, study, switch strategy, continue, or stop?
6. What is the intended benefit of that action?
7. Is the control action directly measured or only inferred?

### C. Information seeking

8. What uncertainty is being reduced?
9. Which information source is selected?
10. Is source quality/reliability known?
11. Is sampling quality separated from termination quality?
12. Could search reflect habit/curiosity rather than monitoring?

### D. Value/cost

13. What is the expected value of further information?
14. What are time/effort/opportunity costs?
15. What are error stakes?
16. Does the same confidence rationally imply different action under different costs?

### E. Revision

17. Is additional post-decision evidence available?
18. Does confidence update before revision?
19. Are changes of mind corrective or noncorrective?
20. Could confidence-dependent confirmation bias distort later evidence?
21. Does revision improve expected accuracy?

### F. Abstention/opt-out

22. Is abstention available?
23. What is its reward/cost?
24. Is opt-out frequency separated from opt-out threshold?
25. Does abstention actually improve global performance?
26. Could the extra meta-decision itself add cognitive burden?

### G. Stopping

27. What stopping criterion is used?
28. Is there an answer-confidence threshold?
29. Is there an opt-out threshold?
30. Is there a time/deadline threshold?
31. Is persistence evaluated globally rather than item-by-item?

### H. Help/social information

32. Is another source likely to be more accurate?
33. Is own confidence considered?
34. Is source confidence/reliability considered?
35. Could confident misinformation dominate when own confidence is low?

### I. Strategy selection

36. What alternative strategies exist?
37. Which strategy is predicted to optimize the current goal?
38. Are time/effort costs included?
39. Is strategy change being attributed uniquely to metacognition rather than learning/control?

### J. Learning/study allocation

40. What is the learning objective?
41. Is time constrained?
42. Are easy versus difficult items differently valuable?
43. Could JOL elicitation itself alter memory?
44. Is study allocation evaluated by final learning rather than allocation pattern alone?

### K. Measurement reactivity

45. Does requiring confidence/JOL/explanation alter the first-order task?
46. Is the no-monitoring control condition available?
47. Could reporting demand reduce or improve subsequent performance?

### L. Individual inference

48. Is the control policy repeated across occasions?
49. Across domains?
50. Is monitoring quality measured separately from control quality?
51. Are motivation and executive implementation separately measured?
52. What rival explanation remains?
53. What second manipulation would discriminate it?
54. What is the strongest warranted conclusion?

## 47. High-value failure modes

### Failure A — low confidence = information seeking

Incorrect as a universal rule.

Correct:

Confidence influences search, but task beliefs, difficulty, expected information value, and costs also matter.

### Failure B — more search = better metacognitive control

Incorrect.

Correct:

Search quality depends on source selection and stopping; miscalibrated confidence can produce ineffective search.

### Failure C — good confidence calibration = good information search

Incorrect.

Correct:

Sampling, termination, cost, and source quality are additional control problems.

### Failure D — information seeking = uncertainty-driven metacognition

Incorrect.

Correct:

Searching can also be habitual, curiosity-driven, externally instructed, or socially motivated.

### Failure E — change of mind = indecision

Incorrect.

Correct:

Revision can be adaptive post-decision evidence use.

### Failure F — more changes of mind = better metacognition

Incorrect.

Correct:

Revision can correct or introduce errors.

### Failure G — confidence reporting improves self-regulation

Incorrect as a universal claim.

Correct:

Requiring confidence can sometimes impair first-order/revision performance.

### Failure H — metacognitive measurement is passive

Incorrect.

Correct:

Confidence/JOL elicitation can change the process being measured.

### Failure I — abstention = inability

Incorrect.

Correct:

Abstention can be a rational meta-decision under uncertainty/high error cost.

### Failure J — more abstention = better control

Incorrect.

Correct:

Opting out may fail to improve accuracy and can impose an additional decision cost.

### Failure K — giving up = weak persistence

Incorrect.

Correct:

Stopping can optimize global performance under limited time/resources.

### Failure L — help seeking = low ability/dependence

Incorrect.

Correct:

It can be rational delegation/information acquisition.

### Failure M — following a confident other = good social metacognition

Incorrect.

Correct:

Usefulness depends on source calibration/reliability and one's own uncertainty.

### Failure N — strategy change = metacognition alone

Incorrect.

Correct:

Learning, executive control, task knowledge, and incentives also contribute.

### Failure O — hardest items should always receive most study time

Incorrect.

Correct:

Optimal study allocation changes with time pressure and global learning objectives.

### Failure P — good monitoring = good control

Incorrect.

Correct:

The monitoring signal can be accurate but used with a poor threshold/policy.

### Failure Q — good control action = accurate introspection

Incorrect.

Correct:

Useful control can be learned/habitual or externally scaffolded.

### Failure R — one search/abstention/help-seeking tendency = stable self-regulation trait

Incorrect.

Correct:

Control policies are context-, cost-, domain-, and measurement-dependent.

## Canonical working rule

**Measure the monitoring signal first, then identify the control action, model the expected benefit and cost of further cognition/information, distinguish sampling from stopping and revision from correction, test whether the action actually improves the global objective, and only then infer a metacognitive-control mechanism or stable tendency.**

Never:

**uncertain -> searched/revised/asked/stopped -> good or bad metacognition by definition.**

## Current-standard note

As of 2026-11-19:

- the newest integrative metacognition review explicitly frames metacognition as supporting control across timescales, from rapid error correction to strategic task arbitration and social communication;
- current information-seeking evidence shows that confidence influences search but does not uniquely determine it; prior beliefs, perceived difficulty, source value, cost, and task context can change the confidence-to-search mapping;
- current 2026 evidence distinguishes information-search sampling quality from termination quality and links both to metacognitive sensitivity under value-based decision conditions;
- opting out is increasingly modeled as a threshold-based control decision with its own stopping criterion, yet greater opt-out behavior does not automatically improve accuracy;
- post-decision processing continues after initial commitment, enabling confidence revision and changes of mind, but confidence can also bias interpretation of later evidence;
- confidence/JOL reports can alter first-order processing, so metacognitive measurement itself can be reactive;
- help seeking, social information use, external search, strategy switching, and stopping are best treated as cost-sensitive control policies rather than direct readouts of ability, dependence, or "good/bad" metacognition.
