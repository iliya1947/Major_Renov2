## 3. Bone remodeling

Bone is continuously renewed through coordinated cellular activity.

Canonical remodeling sequence:

`activation → osteoclast-mediated resorption → reversal/coupling → osteoblast-mediated formation → mineralization`.

Osteocytes help coordinate local remodeling and mechanical adaptation.

Bone remodeling serves:

- replacement of old/damaged tissue;
- mineral homeostasis;
- adaptation to mechanical environment.

Project rule:

**bone remodeling is a coupled cellular process, not simply “building bone.”**

Net skeletal change depends on the balance and spatial organization of formation and resorption over time.

---

## 4. Osteoblast / osteoclast balance

### Osteoclasts
Resorb mineralized bone.

### Osteoblasts
Produce osteoid and support mineralization; osteoblast-lineage cells also regulate osteoclastogenesis.

### Osteocytes
Coordinate remodeling and mechanosensing through signals including:

- RANKL/OPG axis;
- sclerostin/SOST;
- Wnt/β-catenin-related signaling;
- other load-sensitive pathways.

Important boundary:

Serum markers such as P1NP, osteocalcin/BALP or CTX are **turnover proxies**.

They do not directly report:

- cell number;
- local skeletal-site balance;
- net whole-bone strength change.

Project rule:

**formation/resorption markers are mechanistic/monitoring evidence, not direct equivalents of osteoblast/osteoclast “balance.”**

---

## 5. Mechanotransduction in bone

Bone is highly mechanosensitive.

Osteocytes are central mechanosensors within the lacunar-canalicular network.

Candidate/established mechanotransduction components include:

- fluid shear and matrix deformation around osteocytes;
- integrin/cytoskeletal signaling;
- mechanosensitive ion channels such as Piezo1;
- Wnt/β-catenin signaling;
- sclerostin regulation;
- RANKL/OPG-mediated coupling;
- YAP/TAZ-related pathways.

Primary 2025 mouse evidence supports a Piezo1–YAP–CCN–Src axis in osteocyte maturation/dendrite biology.

Boundary:

**molecular mechanotransduction evidence establishes plausibility/causal biology but does not directly define the optimal human exercise dose.**

---

## 6. Bone mineral density

BMD is a major clinically and physiologically useful skeletal measure.

Exercise RCT syntheses show:

- small-to-moderate site-specific BMD benefits across adulthood;
- effects differ by skeletal site, age/sex and modality;
- resistance, impact and combined exercise can be osteogenic;
- many exercise-dose rankings remain uncertain because of heterogeneity and sparse direct comparisons.

The 2026 meta-analysis of 162 RCTs found significant pooled improvements at several sites, but site-specific NMA rankings and dose-response optima were often imprecise/exploratory.

Project rule:

**BMD is important but should not be interpreted as a complete readout of bone adaptation or fracture resistance.**

---

## 7. Bone geometry / microarchitecture / strength

Bone strength depends on more than mineral density.

Relevant contributors include:

- cortical thickness;
- cortical area/porosity;
- periosteal/endosteal geometry;
- section modulus;
- trabecular thickness/number/connectivity;
- material quality;
- mineralization;
- collagen/matrix properties.

Human exercise studies using HR-pQCT/QCT and finite-element models show that exercise can alter:

- microarchitecture;
- geometry;
- estimated stiffness/failure load

even when DXA BMD changes are small or absent.

Examples:

- 12-week power training in very old mobility-limited adults assessed geometry/microarchitecture and estimated bone strength rather than relying on BMD alone;
- 6-month high-impact + resistance exercise produced regional changes in estimated vertebral strength despite mixed/regional BMD changes.

Project rule:

**BMD ≠ bone strength; geometry, microarchitecture and material properties also matter.**

---

## 8. Mechanical stimulus: magnitude

Higher mechanical strain/load magnitude can be osteogenic, but the relationship is not simply:

`more external load → more bone adaptation`.

Important distinctions:

- external ground-reaction force/load is not local bone strain;
- muscle forces can create large local skeletal loads;
- the same external exercise can load skeletal sites differently;
- individual geometry and technique change local strain.

Human 2026 drop-jump RCT showed that greater prescribed jump height did not produce a simple monotonic skeletal dose-response.

Project rule:

**local skeletal mechanical stimulus matters more directly than the exercise label or external load alone.**

---

## 9. Mechanical stimulus: rate

Bone response is influenced not only by strain magnitude but also by how quickly loading changes.

Higher strain/load rates are generally considered osteogenic features, especially in impact/ballistic loading.

Current exercise guideline/synthesis literature therefore often includes:

- dynamic resistance;
- power training;
- impact loading

when targeting bone strength.

But exact human thresholds are not established across bones/populations.

Project rule:

**loading rate matters, but there is no universal minimum rate that guarantees adaptation.**

---

## 10. Mechanical stimulus: novelty / distribution / cycles

Bone mechanosensitivity is shaped by more than total work.

Relevant loading features include:

- novelty/unusual strain distribution;
- directionality;
- number of cycles;
- frequency;
- rest insertion;
- waveform;
- site-specific strain environment.

Mechanistic loading literature suggests osteogenic response can saturate with repeated identical cycles and may be enhanced by unusual/distributed loading or rest insertion.

However much of the precise parameter evidence comes from animal loading models.

Project rule:

**novelty and loading pattern are credible determinants, but exact animal-derived strain thresholds/cycle prescriptions are not transferred directly to human RT.**

---

## 11. Resistance training and bone

Resistance training can support skeletal adaptation through:

- direct external loading;
- high muscle-generated forces;
- repeated site-specific strain.

Current human meta-analyses show resistance exercise can maintain or modestly improve BMD, with effect dependent on:

- site;
- age/sex;
- baseline bone status;
- duration;
- intensity/frequency;
- comparator.

A 2026 older-adult meta-analysis found high-intensity RT clearly superior for muscle strength, but **not significantly superior** to lower-intensity RT for lumbar-spine or femoral-neck BMD.

Project rule:

**stronger muscle-strength benefit from heavier RT does not automatically imply proportionally larger BMD benefit.**

---

## 12. Impact / high-rate loading

Impact exercise can provide high-rate, multi-directional skeletal loading.

Current evidence supports impact/high-intensity/strength approaches as potentially osteogenic, especially at loaded sites.

In postmenopausal women, 2026 synthesis found small significant pooled femoral-neck and lumbar-spine effects for high-intensity/impact/strength training.

But:

- impact is not universally required;
- exact osteogenic dose varies;
- jumping height/external impact magnitude is not a simple proxy for local skeletal adaptation;
- safety/medical appropriateness depends on bone status and clinical context.

Project rule:

**impact loading is a useful osteogenic modality, not a universal requirement or “more impact is better” rule.**

---

## 13. Bone-turnover markers and acute response

Exercise can acutely alter:

- P1NP;
- osteocalcin/BALP;
- CTX;
- sclerostin and related signaling markers.

A 2025/2026 human crossover study in young men found acute impact exercise increased P1NP, while resistance exercise did not produce the same response under that protocol.

Other acute resistance studies show changes in P1NP, osteocalcin, CTX and sclerostin.

Canonical boundary:

**acute bone-turnover/signaling response ≠ proven chronic BMD/geometry/strength adaptation.**

Markers are useful for mechanism and short-term turnover, not program ranking by themselves.

---

## 14. Time course of bone adaptation

Bone adaptation is generally slower to become measurable structurally than acute biochemical responses.

Current evidence shows:

- turnover markers can change after a single bout or within weeks;
- meaningful BMD/geometry/microarchitecture trials generally operate over months;
- some structural/estimated-strength changes can be detectable over ~12–24 weeks in selected populations/sites;
- longer interventions can produce more consistent BMD effects in some meta-analyses.

There is no universal onset week because:

- bone site differs;
- cortical/trabecular compartments differ;
- age/sex/hormonal state differ;
- measurement sensitivity differs;
- baseline loading history differs.

Project rule:

**bone adaptation has multiple clocks: turnover markers, density, geometry and mechanical strength need not change simultaneously.**

---

## 15. Age-related changes

Aging affects bone through multiple mechanisms:

- altered remodeling balance;
- hormonal changes;
- reduced osteoblast-lineage function;
- osteocyte senescence/dysfunction;
- matrix glycation/crosslink changes;
- changes in mineralization/microdamage;
- altered mechanosensitivity;
- cortical porosity and trabecular deterioration.

Current 2025 review emphasizes age-related changes in bone matrix composition and osteocyte mechanical microenvironment that may reduce mechanosensitivity.

Exercise remains beneficial in older adults, but the response may differ from younger bone.

Project rule:

**aging changes both baseline bone state and mechanotransduction; it is not merely “lower BMD.”**

---

## 16. Energy availability and bone

Adequate energy availability supports skeletal remodeling and endocrine conditions compatible with bone formation.

Low energy availability (LEA) is associated with impaired bone health and increased bone-stress-injury risk in athlete evidence.

Fresh randomized crossover evidence (2026) shows that only 5 days of marked LEA:

- suppressed fasting P1NP;
- maintained lower formation-marker response through recovery;
- in one condition increased postexercise CTX.

Preserving protein intake did not eliminate the bone-turnover disturbance.

Boundary:

**short-term biomarker changes do not quantify chronic BMD loss, but they strengthen causal plausibility that energy deficiency can rapidly shift bone remodeling in an unfavorable direction.**

Exact energy prescription → NUT/IND.

---

## 17. Osteoblast/osteoclast balance and energy availability

Energy deficiency can influence bone indirectly through:

- reproductive/endocrine signaling;
- leptin/IGF-1/thyroid-related pathways;
- stress hormones;
- nutrient availability;
- altered exercise recovery and remodeling.

Current LEA systematic evidence indicates impaired bone health in athletes, while bone effects vary with:

- sex;
- severity/duration;
- hormonal state;
- sport/loading type;
- calcium/vitamin D and other nutrition.

Project rule:

**mechanical loading cannot be assumed to fully compensate for sustained low energy availability.**

---

## 18. Bone adaptation vs age / sex / hormonal status

Exercise effects are context dependent.

Examples:

- postmenopausal women can show BMD benefit from high-intensity/impact/strength approaches;
- older-adult meta-analyses show exercise can attenuate age-related BMD decline;
- premenopausal adults may have smaller detectable BMD changes when baseline bone mass/loading is already high.

Project rule:

**group-specific baseline biology modifies the detectable adaptation; one population's effect size is not a universal response.**

---

## 19. Medical boundary: osteopenia / osteoporosis

PHY-13 covers normal/adaptive bone physiology.

It does **not** diagnose or manage:

- osteopenia;
- osteoporosis;
- fragility fracture;
- bone stress injury;
- secondary metabolic bone disease.

Clinical decisions involving:

- DXA T/Z scores;
- fracture risk;
- pharmacotherapy;
- contraindications to impact/heavy loading;
- recurrent bone stress injury workup

belong to MED.

Project rule:

**physiological exercise evidence cannot be substituted for individual clinical fracture-risk management.**

## 21. Established project-level model

`site-specific repeated mechanical loading`
`→ osteocyte mechanosensing / mechanotransduction`
`→ altered osteoblast–osteoclast coupling and local modeling/remodeling`
`→ density + geometry + microarchitecture + material adaptation`
`→ altered skeletal strength`.

This process is modified by:

- magnitude/rate/distribution/novelty of local loading;
- age/sex/hormonal state;
- energy availability;
- baseline bone state;
- adaptation time;
- measurement method.

No single outcome captures the whole chain.

## 22. Training-relevant implications

1. **BMD is important but not the whole of bone strength.**
2. **Geometry/microarchitecture/material properties matter.**
3. **Bone-turnover markers are not direct chronic-strength surrogates.**
4. **Mechanical stimulus is site-specific.**
5. **External load/impact is not identical to local bone strain.**
6. **Magnitude, loading rate and novelty/distribution can all matter.**
7. **More impact height or more %1RM is not guaranteed to produce more bone adaptation.**
8. **Resistance and impact loading can both be osteogenic.**
9. **Heavier RT is not automatically superior for BMD even when superior for muscle strength.**
10. **Bone adaptation is measured over months, while markers can change acutely.**
11. **Age modifies remodeling and mechanosensitivity.**
12. **Low energy availability can impair bone remodeling despite exercise.**
13. **Clinical low bone mass/osteoporosis decisions belong to MED.**
