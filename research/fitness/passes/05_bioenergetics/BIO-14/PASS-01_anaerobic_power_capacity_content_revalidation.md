## 2. Qualification purpose / Question Card

A trainer needs BIO-14 to avoid:

- calling every short maximal performance result “anaerobic capacity”;
- treating peak power and mean power as interchangeable;
- interpreting peak power as a direct PCr measurement;
- interpreting mean power as a direct glycolytic-capacity measurement;
- treating a Wingate score as a pure biochemical readout;
- using blood lactate as a quantitative anaerobic-energy measure;
- treating power decline as one single fatigue mechanism;
- assuming repeated-sprint performance is only an anaerobic property;
- assuming longer rest only changes “freshness” rather than energetic state;
- confusing total mechanical work with total anaerobic energy;
- assuming one high-power test transfers equally to every sport/task;
- using “anaerobic” as shorthand for “no aerobic contribution.”

Primary later decisions supported:

- distinguish maximal rate/output from finite high-intensity capacity;
- distinguish first-bout ability from repeated-bout ability;
- interpret peak, mean and decrement metrics as different constructs;
- understand why oxidative recovery affects repeated “anaerobic” performance;
- select plausible testing constructs without overclaiming physiology;
- preserve task/mode specificity before programming.

Question type:

- **construct definition + output interpretation + energetic boundary + repeated-bout integration**.

## 3. Core model — anaerobic is a contribution class, not oxygen absence

Project terminology:

“anaerobic” describes ATP supply not directly dependent on oxidative phosphorylation over the relevant accounting interval.

It does **not** mean:

- oxygen is absent;
- oxidative ATP production is zero;
- glycolysis only;
- phosphagen only.

BIO-08/09 remain authoritative:

**anaerobic-dominant ≠ aerobic silence.**

Canonical rule:

**anaerobic power/capacity are performance/bioenergetic constructs within mixed metabolism, not isolated oxygen-free physiology.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 4. Mechanical power vs metabolic power

Mechanical power is a performance output:

`power = work / time`

At the whole-body/external level it reflects:

- force;
- velocity;
- technique;
- mechanical efficiency;
- active muscle mass;
- neuromuscular output.

Metabolic ATP-production rate is an internal energetic process.

These are related but non-equivalent.

Canonical rule:

**external mechanical power ≠ ATP-production rate.**

A high peak mechanical power does not directly report:

- PCr concentration;
- CK flux;
- glycolytic ATP flux;
- total anaerobic ATP production.

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 5. Anaerobic power — content PASS

Trainer-level definition:

**anaerobic power = ability to generate a very high rate of work/power during a task where non-oxidative ATP provision is a major contributor.**

Important boundaries:

- it is task/mode specific;
- it is a performance construct, not a direct biochemical assay;
- it depends on neuromuscular/mechanical factors as well as bioenergetics;
- oxidative metabolism is still active;
- exact pathway shares depend on duration, mode and method.

Canonical rule:

**anaerobic power emphasizes rate/output, not total sustainable non-oxidative work.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 6. Anaerobic power ≠ phosphagen power only

Very short maximal work often has a major PCr contribution.

But BIO-02/08 establish:

- glycolytic ATP production begins early;
- oxidative ATP production also begins early;
- no universal phosphagen-only time window exists.

Therefore:

**anaerobic power cannot be reduced to PCr alone.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 7. Peak power as an anaerobic-power-related output

Peak power usually captures:

- the highest externally measured power over a defined averaging window;
- the best brief output achieved in a particular test/mode.

It is influenced by:

- force;
- velocity;
- acceleration/inertia;
- test resistance/load;
- sampling frequency;
- averaging window;
- technique;
- body size;
- familiarization;
- neuromuscular state.

Canonical rule:

**peak power is an operational test output, not a universal biological trait.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 8. Peak power ≠ pure energy-system measurement

Peak power may be strongly supported by phosphagen ATP turnover in brief maximal work.

But it is not equivalent to:

- PCr concentration;
- phosphagen ATP-production rate;
- “ATP-PC capacity”;
- total anaerobic capacity.

Project rule:

**peak power is performance output, not direct phosphagen biochemistry.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 9. Anaerobic capacity — content PASS

Trainer-level definition:

**anaerobic capacity = finite amount of high-intensity work/energy that can be supported by non-oxidative ATP provision over a specified task interval.**

Important:

- “capacity” emphasizes total amount, not instantaneous rate;
- the measured value is protocol and method dependent;
- the construct is not one literal anatomical tank;
- oxidative contribution remains non-zero;
- task performance also depends on mechanics and fatigue tolerance.

Canonical rule:

**anaerobic capacity is a finite integrated high-intensity capability, not a directly visible single store.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 10. Capacity ≠ power

A person can have:

- high peak power but poor maintenance;
- moderate peak power but better maintenance;
- similar mean power with different peak/decrement profiles.

Therefore:

**power = rate dimension; capacity = amount/sustainability dimension.**

Neither is a complete substitute for the other.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 11. Anaerobic capacity is not one biochemical reservoir

Relevant non-oxidative contributors include:

- stored ATP;
- PCr/CK;
- glycolytic substrate-level phosphorylation;
- secondary adenylate-kinase contribution.

But total task capacity also depends on:

- recruitment;
- mechanical efficiency;
- metabolite/ion tolerance;
- E–C coupling;
- pacing/test constraints;
- oxidative support.

Canonical rule:

**anaerobic capacity ≠ PCr store + glycogen store treated as one fixed tank.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 12. Peak vs mean power — content PASS

### Peak power

Represents the highest brief external output in the test.

### Mean power

Represents average external power over the defined test interval.

These differ because mean power is influenced by:

- peak reached;
- ability to sustain output;
- fatigue/decrement;
- pacing;
- duration;
- resistance/load;
- cadence/technique.

Canonical rule:

**peak power and mean power are related but distinct performance constructs.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 13. Mean power ≠ anaerobic capacity by definition

In a short maximal test, mean power can be informative about sustained high-rate performance.

But:

- oxidative contribution increases with duration;
- mechanical efficiency matters;
- pacing/fatigue matter;
- mean external power does not directly equal total non-oxidative energy.

Therefore:

**mean power may correlate with an anaerobic-capacity-related task but is not a direct biochemical measurement of anaerobic capacity.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 14. Peak power ≠ “alactic”; mean power ≠ “lactic”

Reject simplistic mapping:

- peak power = phosphagen/alactic;
- mean power = glycolytic/lactic.

Why:

- phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative pathways overlap;
- pathway contribution changes continuously;
- lactate is not glycolytic ATP flux;
- mean power integrates mechanical output, not metabolism.

Canonical rule:

**peak/mean power cannot be assigned one exclusive energy system.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 15. Fatigue index / decrement boundary

Power decline during a maximal test can reflect:

- PCr depletion;
- Pi/ionic changes;
- E–C changes;
- declining glycolytic flux;
- oxidative contribution;
- neuromuscular fatigue;
- pacing/technique.

Therefore:

**a fatigue index is not a single-mechanism biomarker.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 16. Absolute vs relative power

Absolute power:

- W or equivalent external output.

Relative/scaled power can normalize to:

- body mass;
- lean mass;
- allometric body-size terms;
- task-specific mass.

Different scaling choices answer different questions.

Canonical rule:

**absolute peak/mean power and mass-normalized power are different constructs.**

Detailed scaling/normalization belongs TST/RES.

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 17. Mode specificity of power

Peak/mean power differs by:

- cycle ergometry;
- running sprint;
- rowing;
- jump-based tasks;
- resisted sprint;
- other mode.

Mechanical constraints differ.

Canonical rule:

**anaerobic power is mode specific; one modality score does not automatically generalize to another.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 18. Repeated-bout performance — content PASS

Repeated-bout performance is the ability to reproduce high output across multiple efforts.

It depends on:

- first-bout peak/mean performance;
- work-bout duration;
- recovery duration/mode;
- PCr resynthesis;
- residual Pi/pH/ion state;
- oxidative recovery;
- glycolytic redistribution;
- neuromuscular fatigue;
- pacing/motivation;
- mode/technique.

Canonical rule:

**repeated-bout ability is not simply single-bout anaerobic power repeated.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 19. First bout ≠ later bouts

BIO-08 establishes that later repeated sprints commonly begin with:

- incomplete PCr recovery;
- altered pH/Pi;
- elevated VO2;
- changed glycolytic state.

BIO-13 retained current repeated-sprint evidence showing shorter recovery:

- increased VO2/internal load;
- reduced sprint performance.

Canonical rule:

**same maximal intent ≠ same internal metabolic state or output across bouts.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 20. Repeated-sprint performance is mixed-metabolic

Repeated maximal sprints may be “anaerobic-dominant” per bout.

But across a series:

- oxidative contribution becomes increasingly important for recovery and ongoing ATP support;
- glycolytic contribution can change across repetitions;
- PCr restoration depends on oxidative ATP.

Canonical rule:

**repeated-sprint ability is not a purely anaerobic capacity measure.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 21. Current retained repeated-sprint evidence

BIO-13 retained:

- PMID **42712884** — 10 × 6-s maximal cycle sprints with 1:4, 1:3, 1:2 active recovery; shorter rest increased VO2 but reduced sprint performance.
- PMID **40833321** — elite cyclist hypoxic repeated-sprint protocol; shorter/better-recovered sprints preserved higher peak/mean power and torque, while longer/denser bouts produced greater decrement/metabolic accumulation; exact transfer is restricted.
- PMID **41983005** — field-based repeated-sprint training in soccer improved sprint/repeated-sprint/high-intensity-running outcomes without clear pooled aerobic-capacity gain.

These support task specificity and recovery dependence.

No new external interpretation beyond the retained upstream evidence is added in PASS-01.

## 22. Repeated-sprint ability ≠ one score

Possible outputs include:

- best sprint;
- mean sprint;
- total work;
- absolute decrement;
- percentage decrement;
- time-based decrement;
- ability to maintain a threshold output.

These are not interchangeable.

Canonical rule:

**single-bout maximum, average repeated performance and decrement quantify different features.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 23. Decrement metrics can behave differently

A large decrement can result from:

- very high first-bout power;
- poor recovery;
- both.

A smaller decrement can result from:

- strong repeatability;
- or simply lower first-bout output.

Canonical rule:

**low percentage decrement ≠ automatically superior anaerobic fitness.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 24. Best sprint vs repeatability

Example logic:

Athlete A:
- very high first sprint;
- large decline.

Athlete B:
- lower first sprint;
- stable outputs.

Depending on sport/task:

- A may have superior peak power;
- B may have superior repeatability;
- neither is globally “more anaerobically fit” without target definition.

Canonical rule:

**performance profile matters more than one composite label.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 25. Recovery between efforts — content PASS

Recovery after high-intensity work is metabolically active.

Oxidative ATP production supports:

- PCr resynthesis;
- ATP-dependent ion transport;
- restoration of energetic state;
- continued lactate oxidation/redistribution;
- other recovery processes.

Canonical rule:

**recovery is part of repeated-bout performance, not dead time.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 26. PCr recovery

BIO-02 establishes:

- PCr resynthesis is predominantly oxidative;
- O2 availability can alter recovery;
- pH/depletion/protocol influence kinetics;
- no universal recovery time exists.

2025 retained systematic review/meta-analysis:

- PMID **40189235** — 31P-MRS PCr recovery kinetics across 128 studies, demonstrating substantial biological/methodological heterogeneity.

Canonical rule:

**rest duration changes next-bout phosphagen state, but no fixed rest→%PCr formula is retained.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 27. Recovery ≠ complete readiness

Even with substantial PCr restoration, performance can remain limited by:

- Pi;
- ions;
- pH;
- E–C function;
- neuromuscular fatigue;
- thermal state;
- motivation.

Canonical rule:

**PCr recovery ≠ complete repeated-bout recovery.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 28. Longer rest and repeated-bout quality

Longer rest generally allows:

- more PCr restoration;
- greater metabolite/ion normalization;
- better opportunity to reproduce high output.

But:

- exact relationship is task specific;
- long rest may change the intended density/stimulus;
- “full recovery” has no single universal time.

Canonical rule:

**longer rest generally supports output quality, not guaranteed complete restoration.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

BIO-14 does not retain one universal active-vs-passive recovery prescription.

Canonical rule:

**recovery mode is part of the protocol and cannot be ignored when comparing repeated-bout outcomes.**

## 30. Relationship to phosphagen and glycolytic systems — content PASS

Anaerobic power/capacity are not synonymous with one pathway.

### Phosphagen contribution

Especially important for:

- abrupt high ATP demand;
- early brief maximal output;
- rapid repeated high-power work when PCr is available.

### Glycolytic contribution

Can rise during:

- sustained high ATP demand;
- longer intense bouts;
- repeated incomplete-recovery work.

But:

- both overlap;
- oxidative ATP remains active;
- contribution changes during the task.

Canonical rule:

**phosphagen and glycolytic pathways jointly support anaerobic-dominant performance; neither maps one-to-one onto a single power metric.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 31. Power vs phosphagen / capacity vs glycolysis mapping rejected

Reject:

`peak power = phosphagen system`

and

`anaerobic capacity = glycolytic system`.

Why:

- peak power is mechanical output;
- capacity integrates more than glycolysis;
- PCr also contributes beyond the first instant;
- oxidative contribution rises with duration/repetition;
- glycolysis can contribute to very short maximal work.

Canonical rule:

**performance constructs and metabolic pathways are related but not interchangeable taxonomies.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 32. High power does not require high lactate

A brief very-high-power effort can end before large systemic lactate accumulation develops.

This does not mean:

- no glycolysis occurred;
- no metabolic disturbance occurred.

Canonical rule:

**high anaerobic power ≠ high blood lactate requirement.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

## 33. High lactate does not prove high anaerobic power/capacity

Blood lactate reflects:

- production;
- transport;
- oxidation;
- redistribution;
- sampling timing.

Therefore:

**high lactate ≠ high anaerobic power and ≠ direct anaerobic-capacity measurement.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 45. Testing metric selection must follow the decision

Examples:

If the decision concerns:

- first-step/brief explosive output → peak/very-short sprint output may be relevant;

- repeated sprint → repeated-bout average/decrement and recovery matter;

## 46. Practical trainer implications — non-prescriptive layer

1. Separate peak output, sustained output and repeatability.
2. Treat peak power as mechanical performance, not direct PCr measurement.
3. Treat mean power as test-interval performance, not direct glycolytic capacity.
4. Do not use blood lactate as an anaerobic-capacity score.
5. Do not diagnose one fatigue mechanism from power decrement.
6. Compare repeated-bout profiles using both absolute output and maintenance/decrement.
7. Recognize that oxidative recovery contributes materially to repeated high-intensity performance.
8. Use mode-specific performance when transfer matters.
9. Interpret test duration/load as part of the construct.
10. Leave formal test protocol/reliability/MDC to TST.

## 48. Measurement boundaries

### Peak power
External performance output; not direct ATP flux.

### Mean power
Average external performance over a defined interval; not direct anaerobic capacity.

### Total work
Mechanical work; not identical to metabolic energy.

### Fatigue/decrement
Performance pattern; not one fatigue mechanism.

### Blood lactate
Systemic concentration; not direct glycolytic ATP production.

### VO2
Whole-body oxidative signal; not total ATP production.

### PCr
Local muscle state; not directly inferred from external power.

### MAOD / oxygen deficit
Model-derived anaerobic estimate; not a direct tank measurement.

Canonical rule:

**the measurement construct must match the physiological claim.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 51. Evidence-quality interpretation

### Stronger trainer-level evidence

- anaerobic power and capacity are distinct constructs;
- peak and mean power are distinct;
- mechanical power is non-equivalent to metabolic ATP-production rate;
- energy systems overlap during high-intensity work;
- PCr is a major brief-high-power contributor but not the only pathway;
- glycolytic contribution can be large during sustained high-intensity work;
- repeated-bout performance depends strongly on recovery state;
- oxidative metabolism contributes to repeated-bout recovery;
- no universal fixed PCr recovery time exists;
- performance-test outputs are not direct pathway-flux measurements;
- test modality/duration/load are part of the construct.

### Translation cautions

- many anaerobic tests are protocol specific;
- external power is partly neuromuscular/mechanical;
- lactate does not directly quantify glycolytic ATP;
- MAOD and pathway-contribution models are indirect;
- repeatability metrics can be mathematically influenced by first-bout performance;
- exact test reliability/validity/normalization is TST-owned and requires separate current verification.
