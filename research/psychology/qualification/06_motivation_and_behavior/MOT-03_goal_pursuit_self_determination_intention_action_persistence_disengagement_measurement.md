# MOT-03 — Goal pursuit, self-determination, intention–action gap, persistence/disengagement, and regulation-quality measurement validity

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**.
Research scope: public scientific findings and generic inference methodology only; no individualized material.

## Purpose

MOT-01 separated motivation, learning, valuation, control and performance. MOT-02 clarified risk, delay, effort and opportunity costs. The outstanding dependency is **how valued goals become (or fail to become) action, and when continuing versus revising a goal is adaptive**.

Prevent the following reasoning errors:
- intention implies action;
- action failure proves a goal was not valued;
- autonomous motivation means intrinsic enjoyment;
- controlled regulation means the goal is always rejected;
- implementation intention creates an S–R habit;
- persistence is globally good or quitting globally bad;
- disengagement, reengagement and flexible adjustment are one undifferentiated construct;
- self-report goal orientation identifies a causal stable trait;
- a meta-analytic association proves mediation or an individual's psychological mechanism.

## Canonical claim

**Goal pursuit is a staged, context-sensitive process: goal endorsement and regulatory quality, expectancy/controllability, goal hierarchy, commitment, implementation opportunities, execution resources, feedback, persistence, and reassessment are separable. The same action or nonaction can follow multiple pathways.**

No single questionnaire or observed delay uniquely measures the reason for action failure.

## 1. Goal architecture

Distinguish:
1. **Goal content:** the desired end state or standard.
2. **Goal motive:** why this outcome is valued or pursued.
3. **Goal source:** self-selected, requested, negotiated, assigned or culturally inherited.
4. **Goal commitment:** willingness to prioritize and sustain pursuit.
5. **Goal intention:** declared future action or desired behavior.
6. **Goal planning:** a specified how/when/where sequence.
7. **Goal implementation:** initiation and execution under actual opportunities.
8. **Goal monitoring:** comparison of current states with an intended reference.
9. **Goal adjustment:** revision, downgrading, disengagement, reengagement, or flexibility.

Do not infer motive, commitment or a unique controller from goal content alone.

## 2. Goal value is not goal attainability

An outcome can be deeply valued but expected to be impossible under current constraints. A goal may be attainable yet not valued enough to justify its costs. Subjective control can be low even when objective attainability is high.

For each goal, separate desirability, expectancy of success, causal efficacy of proposed action, cost, opportunity, time horizon and social constraints.

**Failure to start cannot be assigned uniquely to low desire.**

## 3. Self-determination theory regulation continuum

SDT distinguishes:
- external regulation: action controlled by explicit rewards, penalties or demands;
- introjected regulation: internalized pressure, guilt, contingent self-worth;
- identified regulation: conscious endorsement of personal importance;
- integrated regulation: identified goals coherently integrated with values;
- intrinsic motivation: interest/enjoyment of activity itself.

Quality of regulation and overall intensity are distinct. A boring but important goal can be autonomously endorsed.

Do not treat these categories as a deterministic sequence that every individual must complete.

## 4. Autonomy is volition, not independence

Autonomy refers to experienced endorsement or volition. Someone can autonomously choose to follow advice, coordinate with others, fulfill obligations, or accept external structure.

The number of choices available is not a sufficient measure of autonomy. Pressure can be internal or external; social context and competence also influence perceived freedom to act.

SDT needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) are constructs with empirical support, not interchangeable with neural resources or clinical diagnoses.

## 5. Need satisfaction versus frustration

A merely absent supportive context differs from active thwarting. A coercive context, humiliation during skill learning, or active exclusion may have distinct consequences compared with neutral lack of support.

A 2024 meta-analysis examined separate autonomy, competence and relatedness support/thwarting categories in student samples. Transfer to different ages and life domains requires evidence.

**No generalization from one need-support score to global personality needs.**

## 6. Autonomous and controlled motives can co-occur

A person can partly endorse a goal while also experiencing reward pressure, guilt, reputation concerns or fear of loss.

Summing intrinsic and identified motives into a single "good motivation" score may conceal opposing predictors. Likewise, labeling any externally rewarded action "controlled" is inaccurate.

The 2025 Sezer et al. systematic review/meta-analysis explicitly modeled autonomous and controlled goal motives separately and assessed links to approach/avoidance appraisals, psychological needs and well-being: 77 studies, 978 effect sizes. Its meta-analytic structural model does not itself establish each direction as causal.

## 7. Self-concordance

Self-concordant goals are relatively aligned with endorsed values and interests. This is not identical to immediate pleasure, self-benefit, social isolation or immunity to obstacles.

A self-concordance score can reflect how people explain motives, personal narratives and contexts as well as processes relevant to sustained pursuit.

Claims about self-concordant goals causing achievement require longitudinal and experimental support, not just concurrent self-report correlations.

## 8. Distinguish approach/avoidance from autonomy/control

Approach versus avoidance describes desired movement toward positive states or away from negative states; autonomous versus controlled describes the regulatory reason/quality.

These dimensions are not identical. An avoidance goal can be endorsed as important; an approach goal can be driven by pressure.

When studies use "intrinsic/extrinsic", "approach/avoidance" and "self-concordance", do not silently treat the scales as interchangeable.

## 9. Goal commitment versus planning

Commitment is not the same as specifying a response sequence. A strongly committed person may have a vague plan; someone with a detailed plan may not value the goal.

Measure:
- commitment and priority;
- intention strength;
- task knowledge;
- next-action planning;
- conflicting goals;
- actual opportunities;
- behavior and completion.

Strong plans without opportunity, resources or intention can fail.

## 10. The intention–behavior gap

Intention predicts behavior imperfectly. Intentions and actions require separate measurement.

A 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis of physical activity found a **47.6% intention–behavior gap among intenders**, based on the paper's specific classification. It is a domain- and measurement-specific estimate, **not** a universal percentage for all decisions.

A 2025 meta-analysis across 92 empirical studies (109 samples; 47,548 participants) further examined moderators and planning/action-control mediation in exercise behavior, but correlational mediation is not by itself proof of causal pathways.

## 11. Intentions can have several forms

Distinguish:
- desire: wishing for an outcome;
- goal intention: commitment to pursue an outcome;
- behavioral intention: stated plan to perform behavior;
- implementation intention: conditional cue–response plan;
- expectation: prediction of what one actually will do;
- self-efficacy: belief one can execute a necessary action;
- perceived behavioral control: belief about opportunities and control.

"Will do" and "want to do" can differ. Survey wording matters.

## 12. Action-phase models as organizational tools

Common models distinguish deliberative/motivational phases of goal selection from volitional/implementation phases and later evaluation. These are useful for research questions, not uniquely proven discrete brain states or mandatory universal sequences.

A person may revisit earlier stages or pursue overlapping goals simultaneously. Do not claim a simple fixed one-way pipeline.

## 13. Implementation intentions

A conditional plan specifies an anticipated cue and a response: "If situation C occurs, then perform action R."

The 2024 Sheeran et al. meta-analysis covered **642 tests**, analyzing plan format, formation procedure, content, samples and outcomes. It supports the relevance of if–then planning across varied settings while highlighting moderators.

**Implementation intentions ≠ guaranteed execution; implementation intentions ≠ devaluation-insensitive instrumental habit.**

They may promote cue-linked preparation or automaticity, but valid habit inference still requires LEA-04-style evidence.

## 14. Planning versus coping planning

Action planning specifies when/where/how to perform the desired behavior. Coping planning specifies responses to anticipated barriers or competing tendencies.

A coping plan may reduce failure when barriers are predictable, but ineffective cues, unrealistic assumptions, complex contingencies or missing resources can prevent implementation.

Do not infer the plan caused behavioral success from a simple association between planning and activity.

## 15. Goal setting: specificity is conditional

Specific challenging goals may help under suitable conditions of task familiarity, feasibility, feedback and commitment. For complex learning tasks or inexperienced performers, rigid high performance targets may undermine learning or increase failure.

A 2026 expert statement in exercise science explicitly cautions against universal prescription of specific challenging performance goals to novices/complex tasks. This is a domain-specific expert synthesis, not proof about every task.

Distinguish learning/process goals from outcome/performance targets and assess actual effects.

## 16. Feedback and self-monitoring

Progress feedback helps update discrepancy between goal state and current state and may alter effort allocation or goal priority.

But feedback may also affect competence, anxiety, expectancy and intrinsic interest. Its presence does not uniquely identify a goal mechanism.

Measurement reactivity matters: simply asking people repeatedly about goals can alter prioritization, planning and behavior.

## 17. Multi-goal conflict

Goals can be complementary, competing or hierarchically nested. A missed action may reflect a temporarily higher-priority goal rather than weak commitment to the nominal target.

Check:
- opportunity cost and time budget;
- same-time conflict;
- shared resource requirements;
- interdependent goal hierarchy;
- short- versus long-horizon goals;
- social duties and constrained choice;
- switching and setup costs.

Do not infer lack of values from reallocation toward another value.

## 18. Goal shielding

Prioritizing an active goal can reduce processing of competing alternatives, but a goal-shielding effect is context-dependent and can be maladaptive under new information.

Persistence and flexibility are both potentially useful. Do not treat strong focus as proof of an invulnerable goal representation or an invariant personality trait.

## 19. Initiation, maintenance, recovery

Separate:
1. starting the behavior;
2. repeating it sufficiently;
3. sustaining it under changing costs;
4. resuming after interruption;
5. recovering from relapse or missed opportunities.

The same person may be effective in one stage and not another. A delay in initiation does not prove weak persistence, and successful initiation does not prove long-term maintenance.

## 20. Persistence is conditional on feasibility

Persistence can maintain valuable investments when progress is feasible. It can also produce sunk-cost escalation, depleted alternatives, or continued action on an outdated goal.

A correct inference about whether persistence is adaptive requires:
- current expected attainability;
- current benefit and cost;
- learning/progress evidence;
- available alternatives;
- ethical/social commitments.

Do not equate "grit" with universal virtue or low disengagement with success.

## 21. Three different adjustment constructs

Riddell et al. (published online Nov 2025; journal volume 2026) meta-analyzed **235 studies and 1,421 effect sizes** and distinguished:
1. **Goal disengagement** — reducing commitment and/or effort on a goal;
2. **Goal reengagement** — pursuing a new/modified goal;
3. **Goal-striving flexibility** — general openness to adjusting pursuit, cognitions or goals under altered constraints.

These are related but empirically **nonidentical**. Their antecedents and consequences differ.

## 22. Disengagement is not equivalent to reengagement

Dropping an unattainable goal may reduce immediate burden without producing a replacement goal. Reengaging may introduce meaning or progress but also fresh burdens.

Behavioral cessation and cognitive detachment can also diverge. An individual may stop acting but remain mentally invested, or continue action with reduced valuation under obligations.

**Avoid treating one scale score as a global "healthy goal adjustment" latent trait.**

## 23. Strength of the goal adjustment evidence

Riddell et al. report **low-to-moderate confidence in the accumulated evidence** due to prevalence of cross-sectional designs, substantial heterogeneity and publication-bias risks, despite fairly good individual study quality ratings.

Some relationships survived sensitivity checks restricted to longitudinal/experimental studies, but many proposed directional relations are unresolved.

Therefore:
- goal disengagement is not invariably beneficial;
- reengagement is not invariably beneficial;
- flexible adjustment is not universally better than persistence;
- any clinical recommendations require stronger setting-specific evidence.

## 24. Publication bias and outcome interpretation

The 2025/2026 meta-analysis reported possible publication bias across multiple predictor and outcome groups. Publication-bias assessments are sensitive to model assumptions and available studies; no single bias statistic settles the true causal effect.

The study's results do **not** mean every intervention to quit a goal improves well-being. Distinguish correlation with prior functioning, self-selection, and consequences of goal change.

## 25. Goal hierarchy level matters

Abandoning an **action**, a **strategy**, a **subgoal** or a **core end state** are not the same event.

A person may abandon a failing strategy while strengthening commitment to the higher-order goal. Short-term disengagement can be instrumental persistence at a higher level.

Riddell et al. identify unclear goal-hierarchy levels as a limitation of current measurement. Specify the level under study.

## 26. Goal crisis and ambivalence

An action crisis may involve competing evaluations of continued pursuit, reconsidering attainability and revaluing alternative goals.

Ambivalence can coexist with commitment; it does not uniquely identify absence of autonomy or a clinical disorder.

Repeated thoughts about stopping are not proof of an actual disengagement event.

## 27. Adaptive adjustment versus avoidance

Goal revision can be adaptive if objective or perceived conditions change. It can also function as avoidance of difficulty when the goal remains feasible and important.

To discriminate:
- evaluate actual attainability and resources;
- identify the source and type of obstacle;
- compare past progress and realistic alternatives;
- check whether a replacement strategy preserves the valued higher-order aim;
- measure downstream objective and subjective outcomes.

No single rule ("never quit" or "always let go") survives these controls.

## 28. Disengagement versus loss of control

Persistent inability to act may look like voluntary disengagement; deliberate abandonment may look like lost motivation.

A voluntary, informed reallocation of effort differs from nonvolitional constraints, exhaustion or inability to execute.

Without probing opportunity, choice, cognitive attachment, expectancy and behavior, do not assign disengagement as the mechanism.

## 29. Regulation quality across goal stages

Motivational quality may change from goal selection to action initiation, persistence and revision.

For example, a formerly externally requested goal may become personally endorsed; an initially enjoyable task may become pressure-driven. This is a possible process, not a mandated longitudinal trajectory.

Repeated and context-sensitive measurement is preferable to classifying someone once.

## 30. Measuring goal motivation

Potential measures include:
- self-reported intrinsic/identified/introjected/external motives;
- independent autonomy/controlled-regulation subscales;
- intention/commitment ratings;
- actual initiation and adherence;
- delay to action;
- goal progress and attainability ratings;
- coping plans and contextual barriers;
- disengagement/reengagement scales.

Every measure has limitations: socially desirable explanation, retrospective reconstruction, response scales, shared-method variance, ability and environmental constraints.

## 31. Why self-concordance indices need care

A net self-concordance score often subtracts controlled reasons from autonomous reasons. Two people can have the same net value with different underlying autonomous and controlled levels.

The 2025 meta-analysis of goal motives explicitly compares models retaining separate dimensions.

**Do not use a single difference score to erase co-occurring motives without validating the scoring construct.**

## 32. Goal-adjustment scales

The Goal Adjustment Scale commonly provides disengagement and reengagement dimensions. Flexible Goal Adjustment measures may instead assess a generalized stance toward modifying goals.

In the 2025/2026 meta-analysis, a large majority of effects for disengagement and reengagement came from the same scale family, and flexibility effects mostly from another instrument. This can create instrument-specific apparent differences.

Validation requires factor structure, reliability, measurement invariance across contexts, predictive validity and behavioral convergence.

## 33. Reliability versus validity

A stable score can reliably capture how someone reports intentions without validly predicting enacted behavior. Similarly, behavioral adherence may be reliable but driven by enforced schedules rather than autonomous commitment.

Psychometrics must separate:
1. internal structure and score reliability;
2. test–retest;
3. construct/convergent/discriminant validity;
4. longitudinal predictive validity;
5. invariance across cultures/task settings;
6. causal sensitivity to randomized manipulations.

## 34. Measurement and the time scale

Intentions fluctuate, opportunities appear/disappear, and goals change. Comparing a one-time intention rating with behavior recorded months later may underestimate within-person adjustment or conflate changes in goal priority.

Use time-aligned repeated measures where feasible and distinguish:
- momentary behavioral opportunity;
- daily execution;
- longer-term progress;
- goal replacement after constraints change.

A global score at one time point is not a comprehensive control-process measure.

## 35. Experimental tests that discriminate mechanisms

Possible factorial contrasts:
- autonomy-supportive vs controlling framing with matched task incentives;
- opportunity/barrier available vs blocked with equal stated intention;
- implementation plan vs goal-only instruction with matched goal commitment;
- attainable vs genuinely unattainable targets;
- feedback availability vs no feedback;
- alternative goal opportunity vs none;
- temporary barrier vs irreversible goal impossibility;
- measured behavioral effort vs self-reported commitment.

Claims require ethical study design, valid manipulations, adequate power and preregistration when feasible.

## 36. Interpretation of intervention evidence

Education SDT intervention meta-analysis (2024) examined 36 studies and found support for some autonomy and competence outcomes, with heterogeneity and less consistent relatedness effects.

A positive intervention does not prove autonomy support is the sole causal mechanism when feedback, teacher behavior or social context changed simultaneously.

Intervention efficacy for personal behavior change belongs to BHC, not inferred directly from the MOT foundation.

## 37. Intention–action gap is not one mechanism

Potential contributors include:
- unanticipated barriers;
- missing timely cues;
- weak planning;
- insufficient skill or knowledge;
- shifting rewards and costs;
- opposing habits;
- competing goals;
- intention fluctuation;
- opportunity constraints;
- affective state;
- task misunderstanding;
- executive control demands.

Never explain all failure by "self-sabotage", low willpower or weak values without evidence.

## 38. Behavioral gaps can be reverse-signed

People may act without previously reporting a strong intention due to habits, obligations, environmental defaults or spontaneous opportunities. Hence analyses should distinguish:
- intenders who act;
- intenders who do not;
- nonintenders who act;
- nonintenders who do not.

A 2023 meta-analysis used these quadrants in physical activity. Its distribution should not be extended as fixed human base rates.

## 39. Scope of current 2026 evidence

A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis in younger physical-activity populations examined behavior planning and intention–behavior associations, but its specific results are domain-specific; no universal effect is assumed here.

A 2026 expert statement on physical activity goal setting recommends attention to novice/complex tasks; it cannot independently establish effectiveness in unrelated work, social or clinical settings.

Current research supports distinguishing process stages and quality of regulation, while more experimental longitudinal studies are needed for causal interpretation.

## 40. Cross-domain boundaries

- LEA: learning contingencies, habits and response automation.
- EXE/MET: goal monitoring, control implementation and confidence.
- MOT: goal value, regulation quality, commitment and adjustment.
- AFF/STR: affective and stress-related moderators of persistence.
- SOC/PER: social motives and stable differences.
- PSYCHOMETRICS: trait measurement validity.
- BHC/CLN: intervention efficacy and clinical interpretation.

Avoid labeling every motivational change as one learning process or one psychiatric state.

## 41. Evidence hierarchy

### High confidence for practical inference
- intention differs from action;
- regulation quality differs from intensity;
- autonomy is not equivalent to intrinsic pleasure;
- cues/plans and opportunity affect goal enactment;
- goal disengagement, reengagement and flexibility are conceptually and operationally different;
- questionnaire and behavior measures are not interchangeable.

### Supported but conditional
- if–then plans can improve execution in some contexts;
- autonomy-supportive environments can improve some motivational outcomes;
- goal specificity/challenge effects depend on task/experience and feedback;
- adaptive goal adjustment can accompany better well-being.

### Not established as universal
- all intenders fail at one common rate;
- every implementation plan creates a habit;
- persistence is always superior to disengagement;
- goal adjustment universally causes better health;
- self-concordance scores directly measure neural mechanisms;
- a single self-report subscale defines a stable individual motivational identity.

## 42. Goal Pursuit Inference Gate — 52 questions

### A. Target and goal
1. What specific end state is desired?
2. At what goal-hierarchy level is it described?
3. Is the action an end, a strategy or a subgoal?
4. Is the goal personally endorsed, assigned or partly both?
5. Is current value distinguished from attainability?
6. Is the goal feasible under present constraints?

### B. Regulation quality
7. Are external and introjected motives separated?
8. Are identified/integrated motives separated from intrinsic enjoyment?
9. Are autonomous and controlled motives allowed to coexist?
10. Does autonomy mean volition rather than independence?
11. Are need satisfaction and active frustration distinguished?
12. Is self-concordance measured with validated subscales?
13. Could social pressure shape self-reported motive explanations?

### C. Intention to action
14. Is desire distinguished from behavioral intention?
15. Is intention distinguished from expectation of actual action?
16. Is commitment measured separately from planning?
17. Are precise implementation cues present?
18. Was the opportunity to act actually available?
19. Are skill and task knowledge sufficient?
20. Are obstacles and competing actions measured?
21. Is implementation planning separated from instrumental habit?
22. Is behavior measured instead of inferred from reported intentions?

### D. Goal setting and feedback
23. Is goal specificity appropriate for the task?
24. Is difficulty feasible relative to ability?
25. Is the participant a novice or expert?
26. Is feedback present and reliable?
27. Are process and performance goals distinguished?
28. Does goal monitoring itself change behavior?
29. Are action initiation and sustained maintenance separately assessed?
30. Could the chosen outcome metric incentivize gaming?

### E. Persistence and adjustment
31. Is current progress observable?
32. Are obstacle permanence and severity known?
33. Is persistence still beneficial given opportunity costs?
34. Is disengagement cognitive, behavioral or both?
35. Is reengagement a separate new goal?
36. Is goal-striving flexibility a trait claim or an observed event?
37. Is abandonment of a strategy confused with abandoning a core goal?
38. Could apparent quitting be forced by constraints?
39. Could apparent persistence be externally enforced?
40. Are replacement goal values and costs measured?

### F. Measurement and inference
41. Are self-report scales reliable?
42. Do scores show discriminant validity across regulation types?
43. Is test–retest relevant given changing goals?
44. Is cultural/age/task measurement invariance assessed?
45. Is behavioral progress observed longitudinally?
46. Are correlational, longitudinal and experimental evidence separated?
47. Does a meta-analysis show substantial heterogeneity?
48. Was publication bias examined?
49. Could common-method bias explain associations?
50. Can a local task finding generalize to other goals?
51. Which alternative causes of action/nonaction remain?
52. What is the narrowest claim justified by evidence?

## 43. Failure modes

A. Endorsed goal → behavior guaranteed: **invalid**.
B. No action → no valuation: **invalid without opportunity/control checks**.
C. Intrinsic = autonomous: **invalid; identified/integrated regulation may be autonomous**.
D. Extrinsic = coercion: **invalid categorical conflation**.
E. One self-concordance score → full motive profile: **invalid without component validity**.
F. Implementation intention → habit: **not established**.
G. If–then plan → action guaranteed: **invalid**.
H. Specific challenging target → universally best: **invalid task-generalization**.
I. Perseverance → always healthy: **invalid**.
J. Disengagement → weak character: **unsupported normative inference**.
K. Disengagement → reengagement: **distinct constructs**.
L. Strategy change → identity change: **unsupported level shift**.
M. Correlated goal-adjustment score → causal wellness improvement: **invalid**.
N. Retest stability → stable personal motive trait: **insufficient validity**.
O. One exercise-domain intention-gap percentage → all people/all goals: **invalid**.
P. Intention–behavior mediation via a correlational model → identified mechanism: **invalid**.

## Canonical working rule

**Goal/end-state and hierarchy → regulatory quality → subjective value/attainability → intention and commitment → implementation cues and opportunities → execution under competing goals → feedback and feasibility → persist, revise, disengage or reengage → measurement/causal validity → task-bounded inference.**

Never: **goal → behavior; nonaction → lack of desire; external reward → no autonomy; repeated effort → adaptive perseverance; stopping → personal failure; correlation → causal individual mechanism**.
