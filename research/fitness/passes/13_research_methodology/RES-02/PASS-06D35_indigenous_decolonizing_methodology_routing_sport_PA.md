Этот micro-pass закрывает четвёртый methodology-level blocker, найденный в PASS-06D31.

Цель PASS не состоит в создании одного «Indigenous design». Это было бы методологически неверно и воспроизводило бы именно ту универсализацию, которую Indigenous research traditions критикуют.

Вместо этого PASS строит **routing architecture**, позволяющую:

1. различать Indigenous paradigm / methodology / method / governance layer;
2. не смешивать Māori, Aboriginal, Torres Strait Islander, First Nations и другие Indigenous traditions;
3. фиксировать community/nation-specific ontology, epistemology, axiology и protocol;
4. отделять Indigenous governance/data-sovereignty requirements от conventional study design;
5. корректно маршрутизировать Kaupapa Māori;
6. корректно маршрутизировать Aboriginal/Torres Strait Islander Indigenous methodologies;
7. различать yarning как method / methodological practice и broader Indigenous methodology;
8. не универсализировать Dadirri;
9. не универсализировать Talking Circles;
10. фиксировать relational accountability;
11. фиксировать Indigenous leadership and decision authority;
12. фиксировать Indigenous Data Sovereignty / governance;
13. фиксировать ICIP / knowledge-holder rights where relevant;
14. проверять community benefit / reciprocity;
15. отделять Indigenous methodology от PAR/CBPR;
16. отделять Indigenous methodology от generic “cultural adaptation”;
17. показать, что Indigenous methodology can overlay qualitative, quantitative and mixed empirical designs;
18. проверить direct sport / physical-activity examples;
19. определить claim limits;
20. передать unresolved gaps в следующий blocker pass.

# 3. First taxonomy rule: there is no single universal “Indigenous methodology”

The strongest cross-source conclusion is negative:

> **Indigenous methodologies must not be collapsed into one universal research design.**

Why:

- Indigenous peoples are not one homogeneous population;
- nations/communities have distinct laws, languages, knowledges, histories and protocols;
- legitimate authority can be community/nation-specific;
- knowledge can be place-specific;
- some methods belong to particular custodians/traditions;
- research governance may differ by community, Country, iwi/hapū, First Nation or organisation.

AIATSIS explicitly requires recognition of diversity and warns against generalisation that masks that diversity.

### Project rule

Create a **routing family**, not a single atomic design:

**Indigenous/decolonizing methodology route  
→ identify Nation/community/tradition  
→ identify declared paradigm/methodology  
→ identify governance/authority  
→ identify methods  
→ identify empirical design  
→ identify analysis  
→ identify data-governance obligations.**

# 4. Indigenous paradigm / methodology / method must be separated

A recurring classification error is to use these words interchangeably.

## Paradigm / worldview layer

Can include:

- ontology;
- epistemology;
- axiology;
- relationality;
- connection to Country/land;
- kinship;
- collective responsibility;
- spiritual/cultural dimensions;
- sovereignty/self-determination.

## Methodology layer

Organises:

- who has authority;
- what counts as knowledge;
- what relationships are legitimate;
- whose priorities define the study;
- how research proceeds;
- how analysis is accountable;
- how benefit/return occurs.

Examples:

- Kaupapa Māori methodology;
- community-specific Indigenous methodology;
- Dadirri when used as a full place-based methodology;
- other nation/community-specific frameworks.

## Method layer

Concrete practice for generating or working with information.

Examples may include:

- yarning;
- pūrākau;
- interviews;
- observation;
- photovoice;
- quantitative measures;
- surveys;
- physiological testing;
- record linkage.

### Critical rule

A culturally recognisable method does not by itself establish an Indigenous methodology.

---

# 5. Indigenous methodology is not defined by qualitative data

This is a major RES-02 routing correction.

The 2022 Ironbark physical-activity study used:

- longitudinal repeated quantitative measurement;
- physical function outcomes;
- cardiovascular risk measures;
- mixed-effects modelling;

while explicitly stating that the study was framed within **Indigenous methodology**.

### Taxonomy consequence

Indigenous methodology is **orthogonal to empirical design family**.

A study can be:

**Indigenous methodology  
+ longitudinal observational/intervention evaluation design  
+ quantitative outcomes  
+ yarning component.**

Therefore do not code:

> Indigenous methodology = qualitative design.

---

# 6. Indigenous methodology is not merely a participant demographic label

A study with Indigenous participants is not automatically Indigenous research methodology.

A paper can include Aboriginal, Torres Strait Islander or Māori participants while using only a conventional Western design.

### Stronger classifier

Look for actual evidence of:

- Indigenous leadership;
- Indigenous worldview/standpoint;
- community priorities;
- Indigenous governance;
- culturally grounded methodological reasoning;
- decision authority;
- data sovereignty/governance;
- reciprocal benefit;
- Indigenous knowledge systems;
- local protocols.

### Rule

**Indigenous sample ≠ Indigenous methodology.**

---

# 7. Indigenous methodology is not “cultural adaptation” alone

A conventional intervention can be culturally adapted by:

- changing imagery;
- changing language;
- changing examples;
- hiring a community liaison.

Those changes may improve relevance.

But they do not automatically create Indigenous methodology.

### Indigenous methodology can alter deeper architecture

- who owns the question;
- who leads;
- who controls data;
- whose knowledge is authoritative;
- who interprets findings;
- who approves dissemination;
- how community benefit is defined;
- whether Country/kinship/spiritual dimensions are epistemically relevant.

### Critical rule

**culturally adapted Western study ≠ Indigenous methodology automatically.**

# 8. Relationality

Wilson's foundational formulation treats knowledge as relational rather than as detached information possessed by an isolated researcher.

Relevant relations can include:

- participants;
- whānau/family;
- community;
- Country/land;
- Ancestors;
- nonhuman relations;
- knowledge holders;
- institutions;
- ideas/knowledge itself.

### Taxonomy implication

Context is not merely a confounder.

In an Indigenous research paradigm, relationships can be constitutive of what knowledge is and how it is legitimately generated.

---

# 9. Relational accountability

Wilson frames methodology/axiology as accountability to relationships.

This affects:

1. topic selection;
2. data-generation methods;
3. analysis;
4. representation/dissemination.

### Project extraction

Ask:

- Accountable to whom?
- Through what relationship?
- Who can challenge the researcher?
- Who decides whether an interpretation is legitimate?
- What obligations remain after publication?

### Critical rule

“Built rapport” is much weaker than relational accountability.

---

# 10. Self-determination / sovereignty

Across AIATSIS, Kaupapa Māori and Indigenous data-governance frameworks, self-determination is central.

Research can conflict with self-determination when communities are:

- merely recruited;
- defined by external priorities;
- denied interpretation authority;
- denied data access/control;
- represented without approval;
- treated as objects of deficit surveillance.

### Project rule

Store separately:

- community priority?;
- Indigenous-led?;
- Indigenous governance?;
- Indigenous decision authority?;
- veto/approval rights?;
- benefit defined by whom?

---

# 11. Indigenous leadership is not token authorship

AIATSIS 2020 defines Indigenous-led research in terms of genuine decision-making responsibility from:

- conceptualisation;
- project conduct;
- analysis;
- interpretation;
- communication of results.

The 2026 NHMRC IREC similarly includes Aboriginal/Torres Strait Islander leadership as a distinct research-excellence criterion.

### Critical rule

One Indigenous co-author does not prove Indigenous leadership.

Extract decision authority, not demographics alone.

---

# 12. Governance ≠ consultation

Consultation can mean:

> researchers have already decided the study and ask for feedback.

Governance means Indigenous people/organisations have real authority over matters such as:

- question;
- methods;
- recruitment;
- interpretation;
- data access;
- dissemination;
- continuation/termination.

### Project rule

Do not code an advisory meeting as full shared governance without evidence of decision rights.

---

# 13. Reciprocity and benefit

NHMRC and AIATSIS both require research to go beyond extraction.

Potential benefit includes:

- locally useful knowledge;
- service/program improvement;
- community capacity;
- workforce development;
- data access;
- cultural strengthening;
- policy influence;
- direct program benefit;
- return of results in community-useful formats.

### Critical rule

“Publication may help Indigenous people someday” is not automatically an adequate benefit architecture.

---

# 14. Two-way capability

The 2026 NHMRC refreshed IREC explicitly includes:

> building two-way capability.

This corrects a one-directional model in which researchers “build community capacity” while assuming academic institutions have nothing to learn.

### Project extraction

Record:

- Indigenous researcher development;
- community capability;
- non-Indigenous researcher learning/accountability;
- institutional capability change;
- long-term sustainability.

# 15. Data sovereignty is methodology-relevant, not IT housekeeping

Maiam nayri Wingara defines Indigenous Data Sovereignty as Indigenous peoples’ right to exercise ownership over Indigenous data throughout:

- creation;
- collection;
- access;
- analysis;
- interpretation;
- management;
- dissemination;
- reuse.

Te Mana Raraunga similarly frames Māori Data Sovereignty as inherent Māori rights/interests in Māori data.

### Critical rule

A secure server alone does not satisfy Indigenous Data Sovereignty.

The question is **who has authority over data and why**.

---

# 16. Indigenous Data Governance

Data governance operationalises sovereignty.

Potential fields:

- who determines collection;
- what data may be collected;
- what categories are legitimate;
- storage location;
- access permissions;
- analysis authority;
- interpretation authority;
- linkage;
- reuse;
- AI/model use;
- dissemination;
- destruction/retention;
- community access;
- intergenerational interests.

### Current 2026 implication

NHMRC IREC now explicitly includes:

> data sovereignty and governance

as a research-excellence criterion.

This is no longer optional “advanced” metadata for Australian Indigenous health research.

# 17. CARE vs FAIR

The CARE Principles address Indigenous rights/interests that FAIR alone does not.

CARE:

- **Collective Benefit**;
- **Authority to Control**;
- **Responsibility**;
- **Ethics**.

### Critical rule

A dataset can be technically FAIR and still violate Indigenous governance interests.

Open science must not be treated as an automatic override of Indigenous authority/control.

Te Mana Raraunga defines Māori data broadly as information/knowledge:

- about/from Māori people;
- language;
- culture;
- resources;
- environments.

Māori Data Sovereignty concerns Māori rights and interests in:

- collection;
- ownership;

For Māori research, do not substitute generic privacy language for Māori Data Sovereignty when Māori data governance is materially relevant.

# 19. Australian Indigenous Data Sovereignty

Maiam nayri Wingara’s current principles assert rights to:

1. control the data ecosystem;
2. contextual/disaggregated data;
3. relevant data supporting self-determination/self-governance;
4. accountable data structures;
5. protective data respecting individual and collective interests.

### Important

Data sovereignty is collective as well as individual.

Individual consent alone may not settle:

- collective cultural interests;
- community-level harms;
- group identifiability;
- cultural knowledge rights.

# 20. Indigenous knowledge / ICIP boundary

AIATSIS requires respect and protection of Indigenous:

- knowledge;
- practices;
- science;
- innovation;
- cultural expression;
- knowledge holders/custodians.

This can require:

- permission beyond ordinary participant consent;
- restricted dissemination;
- acknowledgement;
- negotiated ownership;
- culturally appropriate access;
- legally binding arrangements where appropriate.

### Sport example

Haka may be publicly visible but still constitute taonga with whakapapa and custodial responsibilities.

Public visibility ≠ unrestricted research/use right.

# 21. Kaupapa Māori is a methodology/theory architecture, not “Māori-flavoured qualitative research”

Current and foundational Kaupapa Māori literature identifies Māori:

- epistemology;
- ontology;
- tikanga;
- mātauranga;
- self-determination;
- collective aspirations;

as legitimate foundations of research.

The commonly cited Smith principles include:

1. **tino rangatiratanga** — self-determination;
2. **taonga tuku iho** — cultural aspirations / inherited treasures;
3. **ako Māori** — culturally preferred pedagogy/learning;
4. **kia piki ake i ngā raruraru o te kāinga** — socio-economic mediation;
5. **whānau** — extended family/relational structure;
6. **kaupapa** — collective philosophy.

Later work may additionally foreground:

- āta;
- Te Tiriti;
- other contextual principles.

### Critical rule

Do not turn the six principles into a universal tick-box detached from iwi/hapū/community context.

---

# 22. Tino rangatiratanga

For research routing, tino rangatiratanga concerns meaningful Māori control/self-determination.

Potential methodological implications:

- Māori-led questions;
- Māori authority;
- Māori-controlled interpretation;
- Māori benefit;
- Māori data governance;
- ability to reject deficit framing.

### Critical rule

“Research on Māori” is not equivalent to “research under Māori self-determination.”

---

# 23. Taonga tuku iho

This principle affirms the validity of Māori:

- language;
- tikanga;

- cultural knowledge;
- inherited treasures.

### Sport implication

Research on:

- haka;
- whakapapa;
- Māori rugby identity;
- Māori sport practice;

# 28. Aboriginal/Torres Strait Islander research cannot be represented by one pan-Australian method

AIATSIS explicitly requires recognition of diversity:

- Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples;
- nations;
- communities;
- languages;
- laws;
- cultures;
- practices;
- histories;
- perspectives.

### Taxonomy consequence

Do not create:

> “Aboriginal method = yarning.”

Yarning is important and widely used, but:

- its form varies;
- not every community uses it identically;
- other legitimate methods/methodologies exist;

# 29. Yarning: method, methodology, or relational practice?

The literature uses all three levels.

Bessarab & Ng’andu 2010 explicitly present yarning as a legitimate **method** / data-gathering tool and distinguish forms such as:

- social yarning;
- research-topic yarning;
- collaborative yarning;
- therapeutic yarning.

Later work also describes yarning as:

- methodology;
- relational methodology;
- protected relational space.

### Project decision

Never infer level from the word “yarning” alone.

Extract the author-declared role:

- method?;
- data-generation practice?;
- full methodology?;
- relational framework?;
- analytic method?

Then inspect actual architecture.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

# 30. Social yarning

Social yarning can precede formal research-topic discussion.

Functions may include:

- relationship building;
- trust;
- contextual understanding;
- mutual positioning;
- researcher accountability.

### Critical rule

Do not delete “off-topic” relational conversation from the methodological account simply because it is not direct question-answer data.

In some Indigenous research architectures, relationship formation is part of legitimate research practice.

---

# 31. Research-topic yarning

Research-topic yarning has a purposeful research focus but remains:

- conversational;
- flexible;
- story-led;
- relational.

### Boundary with conventional semi-structured interview

Both can gather narrative information.

The difference is not merely lack of structure.

Yarning can involve culturally grounded relational expectations, reciprocity and researcher positioning that a generic semi-structured interview does not automatically enact.

---

# 32. Yarning is not “informal interview” as a synonym

Describing yarning as merely:

> relaxed interviewing

erases its methodological/relational basis.

Potential constitutive elements include:

- reciprocity;
- cultural safety;
- relationship;
- listening;
- participant control over narrative flow;
- flexibility;
- accountability.

### Critical rule

The presence of conversational speech style alone does not prove yarning.

# 37. Yarning in Aboriginal sport history

Osmond & Phillips use yarning in Aboriginal sport history and show that archival reconstruction and yarning can produce materially different historical knowledge.

### Methodological lesson

Indigenous methods can reveal:

- community meaning;
- agency;
- resistance;
- counter-narratives;

that are absent from colonial/administrative archives.

### Claim boundary

Yarning does not automatically verify every recalled historical detail.

It adds Indigenous testimony and interpretation to the evidentiary landscape.

# 38. Dadirri must not be universalised

The 2022 Dadirri methodology paper is explicit:

Dadirri is:

- place-based;
- a deep contemplative listening process;
- co-developed in Nauiyu;
- applied on Country;
- connected to Ngan’gikurunggkurr cultural custodians.

### Critical taxonomy rule

Do **not** encode:

> Dadirri = generic Indigenous deep listening usable anywhere.

This would detach the methodology from its cultural custodianship and place.

# 39. Dadirri can be a methodology, not merely interview technique

The 2022 paper explicitly calls Dadirri an Indigenous **place-based research methodology**.

Earlier literature also describes it as:

- Indigenous research method;
- way of life;
- research framework.

### Project routing

Store:

1. author label;
2. Nation/community;
3. custodianship;
4. Country/place;
5. permission/authority;
6. how Dadirri governs the research process.

Do not reduce it to:

> “interviewer listened carefully.”

# 40. Dadirri sport boundary example

A sport-career-transition project used Dadirri when researching elite Indigenous Australian sportsmen.

### Taxonomy lesson

Dadirri can appear in sport research.

But its legitimate use cannot be inferred merely because the participants are Indigenous athletes.

Community/cultural legitimacy must be reported.

---

# 41. Talking Circles must not be universalised

Talking Circles occur in multiple Indigenous contexts, especially among some First Nations traditions.

But:

- protocols vary;
- meanings vary;
- ceremonial status varies;
- authority varies;
- not every Indigenous people uses Talking Circles.

### Critical rule

Do not build:

> “Talking Circle = universal Indigenous focus group.”

A Talking Circle should be routed according to the specific Nation/community/tradition and declared methodological role.

---

# 42. Talking Circle vs focus group

A Talking Circle may differ from a focus group through:

- ordered turn-taking;
- relational/ceremonial protocol;
- uninterrupted speaking;
- community authority;
- reciprocity;
- spiritual/cultural significance;
- collective interpretation.

But not every circle has every one of these features.

### Project rule

Do not infer methodology from seating geometry.

A group sitting in a circle is not automatically an Indigenous Talking Circle.

---

# 43. Method ownership / cultural authority

Some Indigenous methods or knowledges cannot be safely “borrowed” because they appear in published literature.

Publication does not automatically eliminate:

- custodianship;
- permission;
- community authority;
- sacred/restricted knowledge boundaries.

### Project rule

Add a field:

> **method/knowledge culturally owned or permission-governed?**

This is distinct from copyright.

---

# 44. Indigenous methodology vs PAR

There can be strong overlap:

- participation;
- power sharing;
- action;
- co-design;
- community priorities.

But PAR does not inherently encode:

- Indigenous ontology;
- Indigenous sovereignty;
- Country/kinship;
- Indigenous knowledge authority;
- Indigenous data sovereignty;
- Indigenous cultural protocols.

### Critical rule

**Indigenous PAR** can be a valid hybrid.

But:

> PAR + Indigenous participants ≠ Indigenous methodology automatically.

---

# 45. Indigenous methodology vs CBPR

CBPR can support:

- shared governance;
- reciprocal benefit;
- community partnership.

But generic CBPR remains insufficient when the relevant Indigenous methodology requires:

- sovereignty;
- nation-specific authority;
- data governance;
- Indigenous epistemology/axiology;
- cultural knowledge protocols.

### Routing rule

Store both when present:

**Indigenous methodology  
+ CBPR/community-partnership architecture.**

# 46. Indigenous methodology vs decolonizing methodology

These overlap but are not universal synonyms.

## Indigenous methodology

Grounded in Indigenous ways of:

- knowing;
- being;
- doing;
- valuing;
- relating.

## Decolonizing methodology

Can include critique/transformation of:

- colonial knowledge systems;
- extraction;
- deficit framing;
- institutional power;
- researcher authority.

### Critical rule

A non-Indigenous critical study can call itself decolonizing without thereby becoming an Indigenous methodology.

Conversely, Indigenous methodology often has decolonizing effects/commitments.

---

# 47. Decolonization is not a metaphor for “more inclusive”

For taxonomy, “decolonizing” should not be accepted when it means only:

- inclusive recruitment;
- culturally sensitive wording;
- diverse authorship.

Stronger decolonizing architecture addresses:

- colonial power;
- sovereignty;
- knowledge authority;
- extraction;
- ownership;
- institutional control;
- epistemic hierarchy.

### Project rule

Extract the specific decolonizing operation.

# 48. Indigenous standpoint

Some Australian research explicitly uses an Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander standpoint.

Potential functions:

- position knowledge in lived/community histories;
- challenge settler-colonial default categories;
- guide interpretation;
- privilege Indigenous epistemic authority.

### Critical rule

Standpoint is not simply:

> author identity disclosed.

It should materially shape the research logic.

- benefit;

# 51. Consent is not the whole governance model

Individual informed consent answers:

> may this person participate?

It does not necessarily answer:

- may this collective knowledge be published?
- may these data be reused?
- may another institution access them?
- may an AI model be trained on them?
- may culturally restricted knowledge be shared?
- may community-level identity be inferred?

### Critical rule

Individual consent and collective governance are separate fields.

---

# 52. Collective risk

Research can harm a community even when no individual is identifiable.

Examples:

- deficit stereotyping;
- stigmatizing community-level statistics;
- inappropriate cross-community generalisation;
- disclosure of cultural knowledge;
- extraction without benefit;
- policy misuse;
- algorithmic profiling.

### Project implication

Risk appraisal must include:

**individual risk + collective/community risk.**

---

# 53. Deficit framing

Indigenous scholarship repeatedly critiques research systems that define Indigenous peoples mainly through:

- pathology;
- failure;
- risk;
- disadvantage.

### Project rule

Extract:

- deficit framing?;
- strengths framing?;
- community-defined outcomes?;
- colonial/contextual determinants acknowledged?;
- Indigenous conceptions of wellbeing included?

### Critical caveat

Avoiding deficit framing does not mean hiding real disease/risk data.

It means interpreting those data without decontextualising colonial/systemic causes or denying community strengths/agency.

---

# 54. Outcome selection can be epistemic governance

Conventional fitness/health research may privilege:

- VO2max;
- steps;
- BMI;
- disease risk;
- adherence.

Indigenous communities may prioritise additional outcomes such as:

- connection to community;
- connection to Country;
- cultural continuity;
- identity;
- belonging;
- collective wellbeing;
- participation;
- confidence;
- family/whānau effects.

### Critical rule

An outcome set can be statistically valid yet epistemically incomplete.

The community-running-group study demonstrates direct participant involvement in selecting meaningful evaluation measures.

---

# 55. Western measures are not automatically invalid

Indigenous methodology does not require rejection of:

- accelerometry;
- strength tests;
- gait speed;
- physiological measures;
- statistical modelling.

The Ironbark study used conventional physical-function metrics inside an Indigenous methodology.

### Taxonomy rule

The issue is not:

> Western measure vs Indigenous measure.

It is:

- who selected it;
- whether it measures a meaningful construct;
- whether it is culturally valid;
- how it is interpreted;
- how data are governed;
- what is missing.

---

# 56. Mixed-method/hybrid architecture is expected, not exceptional

An Indigenous methodology study may legitimately combine:

- yarning;
- interviews;
- quantitative scales;
- physical testing;
- administrative data;
- case study;
- photovoice;
- program evaluation;
- longitudinal analysis.

### Project rule

Preserve layers:

**Indigenous methodology/governance  
+ empirical study design  
+ data-generation methods  
+ analysis methods.**

Do not force one label to replace all others.

# 57. Analysis authority

A frequent extraction risk is:

> community helped collect data, academics interpreted it alone.

Indigenous methodology may require Indigenous/community roles in:

- coding;
- theme development;
- contextual interpretation;
- checking cultural meaning;
- deciding what cannot be reported.

Record:

1. who analyzed?;
2. who interpreted?;
3. who validated cultural meaning?;
4. who approved publication?;

# 61. What Indigenous methodology can establish

The methodology layer can strengthen legitimacy and support claims about:

- culturally grounded meanings;
- Indigenous priorities;
- relationships;
- community-defined benefit;
- locally legitimate constructs;
- Indigenous knowledge;
- experience of sport/PA;
- program acceptability;
- culturally relevant implementation;
- relational/collective wellbeing;
- community interpretation;
- context-specific intervention processes.

But what the study can establish scientifically still depends on the **empirical design**.

# 62. Indigenous methodology does not upgrade causal identification

Example:

Indigenous methodology  
+ uncontrolled before/after PA program

does not become:

> randomized causal evidence.

Likewise:

Kaupapa Māori interviews

do not estimate:

> population prevalence.

### Critical rule

Indigenous methodology strengthens epistemic/cultural/governance validity.

It does not override ordinary design limits for:

- causality;
- prediction;
- prevalence;
- diagnostic accuracy.

---

# 63. Conversely, conventional design validity does not guarantee Indigenous validity

An RCT can have excellent:

- randomisation;
- concealment;
- retention;
- statistical analysis;

and still fail Indigenous research standards if it:

- addresses an externally imposed question;
- uses culturally invalid outcomes;
- lacks Indigenous governance;
- violates data sovereignty;
- produces no community benefit;
- misinterprets Indigenous experience.

### RES-02 implication

Study quality is multidimensional:

**internal validity  
+ construct validity  
+ cultural/epistemic legitimacy  
+ relational ethics  
+ governance/data sovereignty.**

Results from one:

- iwi;
- hapū;
- Aboriginal Nation;
- Torres Strait Islander community;
- Country;
- urban/rural setting;

Record:

- Country/region;
- context;

# 65. Cross-community transfer

A method successful in one community may be inappropriate in another.

Examples:

- Dadirri is not universal;
- Talking Circles are not universal;
- one form of yarning should not be imposed rigidly everywhere;
- Kaupapa Māori is Māori-specific and should not be relabeled as generic Indigenous methodology elsewhere.

### Critical rule

**Indigenous ≠ interchangeable.**
