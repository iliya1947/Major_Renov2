# 3. Lineage

The most commonly cited historical precursor is the **Fogo Process** on Fogo Island, Newfoundland, beginning in 1967.

Its defining innovation was not simply:

> giving a community a camera.

The process used film to:

- surface community concerns;
- screen local perspectives back to communities;
- create dialogue among geographically/socially separated groups;
- communicate concerns to officials;
- return responses to the community;
- support collective action and policy dialogue.

### Project lineage representation

**Fogo Process / community media  
+ participatory-development epistemology  
+ Freirean dialogue/reflection/action  
+ community arts / social-change video  
→ modern Participatory Video.**

### Critical rule

PV's historical lineage makes the **communication process** at least as important as the final film.

---

# 4. Canonical scientific / participatory target

Participatory Video asks participants to become active makers and interpreters of moving-image representations of their own realities.

The methodology can support:

- subjective knowledge;
- group reflection;
- relational sense-making;
- collective agency;
- community dialogue;
- advocacy;
- evaluation;
- social/policy engagement.

### Canonical project representation

group formation  
+  
inclusive media-skill development  
+  
participant recording / role rotation  
+  
playback and reflection  
+  
participant-led issue exploration  
+  
collective production/editing decisions  
+  
community screening/dialogue  
+  
optional external engagement/action  
+  
ongoing power/consent/ownership governance.

---

# 5. Process matters more than the presence of a video

A key modern methodological distinction is:

> PV is not defined by having a video at the end.

The process itself is the methodology.

A high-production-quality film made by professionals about a community may be:

- documentary;
- advocacy film;
- research film;

without being Participatory Video.

### Critical rule

**video product ≠ Participatory Video methodology.**

---

# 6. Current extended PV architecture

Current IDS/Participatory Methods guidance identifies five broad stages:

1. **group forming and building**;
2. **group-level internal exploration and reflection**;
3. **peer-level videoing and sense-making**;
4. **community-level video-mediated dialogue and mobilisation**;
5. **influencing external audiences through video-mediated engagement**.

### Taxonomy decision

Use this five-stage structure as the strongest current process router.

It should not be treated as an inflexible universal recipe, because PV traditions remain plural and context-responsive.

---

# 7. Skipping early stages can invalidate the participatory claim

Current methodology guidance explicitly warns that when early group-building/exploration stages are skipped or rushed, the activity can become:

> group film-making

rather than meaningfully participatory video.

### Why

The early stages develop:

- confidence;
- role familiarity;
- trust;
- equitable participation;
- collective agenda;
- reflexive skill;
- decision competence.

### Project rule

A short film workshop is not automatically PV.

Extract actual process duration and stage coverage.

---

# 8. Group formation is methodological

Group-building is not administrative warm-up.

It affects:

- who speaks;
- who takes the camera;
- who appears on camera;
- who becomes editor/director;
- who challenges others;
- whether vulnerable topics emerge;
- whether disagreement is safe.

### Critical rule

A film cannot be called participant-led merely because participants appear on screen.

---

# 9. Media literacy is part of participation

Participants commonly learn:

- camera operation;
- framing;
- interviewing;
- sound;
- playback;
- story planning;
- editing;
- screening/presentation.

This is not purely technical training.

Media literacy changes participants' ability to:

- represent themselves;
- critique representation;
- challenge researcher/media authority;
- make informed dissemination choices.

---

# 10. Camera-role rotation

Classic PV practice often rotates roles such as:

- camera operator;
- interviewer;
- interviewee/on-screen participant;
- director;
- sound operator;
- editor;
- reviewer.

### Scientific purpose

Role rotation can:

- distribute authority;
- expose participants to multiple perspectives;
- reduce fixed role hierarchy;
- create empathy for being filmed;
- improve collective production competence.

### Caveat

Role rotation does not automatically equalise power.

The 2026 Tavy et al. study shows film roles can themselves create new hierarchies.

---

# 11. Recording ↔ playback is an epistemic loop

PV commonly uses immediate/repeated playback.

Canonical loop:

record  
→ watch together  
→ reflect  
→ discuss  
→ reinterpret  
→ record again.

### Why video matters

Playback can allow participants to:

- see themselves;
- inspect interactions;
- notice body language;
- hear language differently;
- identify contradictions;
- reconsider representation;
- refine messages.

### Critical rule

Video is not merely a storage device.

Playback can become part of analysis.

---

# 12. Action–reflection–action

InsightShare frames PV learning as:

action  
→ reflection  
→ action.

This parallels participatory-learning traditions but is video-mediated.

### Project rule

Store whether playback actually altered:

- filming choices;
- topic framing;
- group interpretation;
- later action.

If playback is only technical quality-checking, the participatory/reflexive architecture is weaker.

---

# 13. Participant-led issue generation

Strong PV allows topics/messages to emerge from participant priorities.

Researchers/funders may begin with a broad issue, but participants should have meaningful influence over:

- what matters;
- what is filmed;
- whose story appears;
- what is omitted;
- what message is communicated.

### Critical rule

A researcher-scripted film performed by participants is not strong PV merely because participants hold cameras for some shots.

---

# 14. Internal reflection and external communication must be distinguished

One of Shaw's important ethical/process distinctions is between:

## Internal videoing

Used for:

- learning;
- trust-building;
- private reflection;
- experimentation;
- sensitive dialogue.

## External video

Produced for:

- community screening;
- stakeholders;
- policymakers;
- public dissemination;
- advocacy.

### Critical rule

Consent to internal workshop playback does **not** imply consent to public dissemination.

---

# 15. Community-level dialogue

PV can move beyond the core group through local screenings.

Community screenings can:

- test interpretation;
- generate disagreement;
- bring in excluded perspectives;
- refine priorities;
- build collective purpose;
- challenge the film-makers' assumptions.

### Critical rule

The screening is not only dissemination.

It can generate additional research/interpretive material.

---

# 16. External audience pathway

Later-stage PV may engage:

- service providers;
- policymakers;
- funders;
- local government;
- clinicians;
- sport organisations;
- community leaders.

### Potential function

video  
→ mediated encounter  
→ discussion  
→ response  
→ action / accountability.

### Critical caveat

Showing a film to decision-makers does not prove policy influence.

Actual influence must be separately evidenced.

---

# 17. PV can be research, evaluation, advocacy or community development

The same PV architecture can serve different parent purposes:

- participatory research;
- PAR;
- programme evaluation;
- community development;
- advocacy;
- organisational learning.

### Project rule

Store:

1. PV methodology/method layer;
2. parent research/evaluation architecture;
3. action/advocacy objective;
4. empirical analysis method.

Do not let “PV” erase the rest of the study design.

---

# 18. PV and PAR overlap but are not synonyms

PV can be used **within** PAR.

But a PV project can also be used for:

- evaluation;
- dialogue;
- representation;
- learning;

without a full action-research cycle.

### Critical rule

PV = video-mediated participatory process.

PAR = broader participatory action-research architecture.

They can coexist.

---

# 19. Facilitator role is structurally important

Facilitators can influence:

- who participates;
- whose idea becomes central;
- filming rules;
- pace;
- emotional safety;
- equipment access;
- editing;
- screening;
- conflict;
- external audiences.

### Strong facilitator stance

The facilitator supports:

- technical capability;
- inclusive process;
- reflection;
- ethical deliberation;

without silently becoming the director.

---

# 20. “Minimal researcher control” is an aspiration, not an empirical assumption

Participatory rhetoric often suggests researchers relinquish control.

In practice, researchers may still control:

- funding;
- timetable;
- equipment;
- ethics approval;
- storage;
- editing software;
- transport;
- institutional access;
- publication;
- dissemination channels.

### Project rule

Extract concrete decision rights rather than accepting claims of empowerment at face value.

---

# 21. Power can move within the participant group

The 2026 older-adult PV study shows that power is not simply:

researcher  
vs  
participants.

Participant film roles can create:

- authority;
- prestige;
- gatekeeping;
- silencing;
- narrative control.

A participant acting as director/interviewer can exert pressure on another community member.

### Critical rule

Participant control is not automatically ethically benign.

---

# 22. Representation claims require caution

PV is often described as “giving voice.”

This language is risky.

Participants already have voices.

The methodological issue is:

- who is heard;
- who controls representation;
- who has access to platforms;
- whose interpretation is authorised.

### Project rule

Prefer:

- participant-led representation;
- amplified access;
- co-produced communication;

over simplistic:

> “researchers gave participants a voice.”

---

# 23. The film cannot automatically represent “the community”

A PV group is usually small.

Selection itself may exclude:

- people with language barriers;
- people without time;
- people uncomfortable on camera;
- lower-status members;
- highly vulnerable members;
- dissenters.

The 2026 power study documents exclusion decisions made by participant co-researchers themselves.

### Critical rule

**participant-made film ≠ representative community voice.**

---

# 24. Editorial control is a major power node

Editing determines:

- what enters;
- what disappears;
- sequence;
- pacing;
- contrast;
- music;
- titles;
- subtitles;
- emotional framing.

### Critical rule

If professional researchers/film-makers retain final cut authority, “participant ownership” can be substantially reduced even when filming was participatory.

---

# 25. Participant editing can take multiple forms

Participants may:

- directly edit software;
- jointly edit with professionals;
- perform a paper edit/storyboard;
- select clips;
- approve cuts;
- veto scenes;
- approve final versions.

### Project rule

Do not require every participant to operate editing software.

Require evidence of meaningful **editorial decision authority**.

---

# 26. Process quality vs production quality

A technically rough video can be methodologically strong PV.

A cinematic professional film can be methodologically weak PV.

### Why

Research quality depends more on:

- participant decision authority;
- reflexive process;
- collective analysis;
- ethical control;
- meaningful representation;

than on image quality.

---

# 27. Ownership has several layers

“Who owns the video?” can mean:

1. physical device;
2. raw footage;
3. project files;
4. final edited film;
5. copyright;
6. moral authorship;
7. permission to publish;
8. permission to reuse;
9. platform account/control;
10. copies after project completion.

### Project rule

Extract these separately.

Do not treat “participants got a copy” as equivalent to ownership/control.

---

# 28. Strong participatory ownership model

InsightShare's current model treats participant groups as owners of their produced video communication, with facilitators/partners receiving permission to share under participant-defined terms.

### Caveat

This is a strong practitioner model, not a universal legal rule across jurisdictions/institutions.

### Project decision

Store:

- legal ownership;
- practical control;
- ethical control;

as separate fields.

---

# 29. Equipment access after the project

Current PV guidance recommends considering whether participants retain:

- copies of films;
- footage;
- equipment access;
- capability to continue production.

### Why

If all technology disappears when researchers leave, “capacity building” may be temporary.

### Caveat

Equipment control can itself generate intra-community power concentration.

---

# 30. Consent is a process, not one signature

Moving-image research creates multiple consent moments.

Useful stages include:

1. consent to participate;
2. consent to be filmed;
3. consent after filming / playback;
4. consent during editing;
5. consent to a specific rough/final cut;
6. consent to specific audiences/platforms;
7. consent to future reuse where applicable.

### Critical rule

A participant may consent to being filmed and later reject public use of that footage.

---

# 31. Participants and contributors are different ethical roles

A **participant/co-researcher** may help:

- plan;
- film;
- edit;
- decide dissemination.

A **contributor** may simply be:

- interviewed;
- filmed;
- visible in the background;
- part of a community scene.

### Project rule

Do not infer that contributors agreed to the broader PV project's governance merely because the participant film crew recorded them.

---

# 32. Being filmed creates special identifiability

Video may reveal:

- face;
- voice;
- body;
- home;
- workplace;
- disability;
- neighbourhood;
- clothing;
- relationships;
- distinctive movement.

Therefore pseudonymization is often insufficient.

### Possible mitigation

- blur face;
- obscure voice;
- film hands/objects;
- use actor;
- use voice-over;
- recreate scene;
- restrict screening;
- remove clip.

---

# 33. Third-party filming is a structural risk

Participants with cameras can unintentionally record people who:

- did not consent;
- do not understand the project;
- are vulnerable;
- can be identified indirectly.

### Project extraction

Record:

- filming-consent training?;
- public-space protocol?;
- incidental capture?;
- child/minor protocol?;
- bystander handling?;
- withdrawal/delete process?

---

# 34. Public distribution changes the risk

A clip shown:

- inside a closed workshop

has a different risk profile from the same clip on:

- YouTube;
- social media;
- conference livestream;
- TV;
- institutional website.

### Critical rule

Consent must be audience/platform-specific enough to be meaningful.

“May use for research” can be too vague for global online dissemination.

---

# 35. Digital permanence

Once public video is downloaded/reposted, later withdrawal may be practically incomplete.

### Project rule

Participants should be informed that:

- withdrawal before publication can be effective;
- withdrawal after broad online dissemination may not remove all copies.

Do not promise fully reversible public dissemination when it is technically impossible.

---

# 36. Raw footage is more sensitive than final film

Raw footage may contain:

- rejected scenes;
- names;
- private discussion;
- failed takes;
- emotional disclosure;
- non-consenting bystanders;
- location information.

### Project rule

Store raw-footage governance separately:

- access;
- storage;
- retention;
- deletion;
- secondary analysis;
- archive;
- participant copy rights.

# 37. PV vs Photovoice

Both are participatory visual approaches and may share:

- participant-generated media;
- critical dialogue;
- community voice;
- advocacy/action;
- visual ethics.

## Photovoice

Canonical object/process:

participant-generated photographs  
+ contextualization/discussion  
+ participatory interpretation  
+ possible action/policy communication.

## Participatory Video

Adds moving-image-specific and process-specific architecture:

- camera-role rotation;
- filming as group practice;
- playback loops;
- audiovisual performance;
- collaborative production;
- editing control;
- staged screenings;
- sequential dialogue with audiences.

### Critical rule

**Photovoice ≠ PV with still images removed.**

The temporal/audiovisual and production-process dynamics materially change participation and ethics.

---

# 38. PV vs documentary filmmaking

A conventional documentary may include:

- interviews;
- community footage;
- participant stories;
- observational scenes.

But the filmmaker usually retains primary control over:

- framing;
- camera;
- editing;
- narrative;
- final cut.

### PV

Participants should meaningfully shape:

- what is filmed;
- how;
- by whom;
- editing;
- representation;
- dissemination.

### Critical rule

A documentary **about** participants is not automatically PV.

---

# 39. Professional assistance does not automatically invalidate PV

Participants may need help from:

- film-maker;
- editor;
- sound technician;
- accessibility specialist.

The NU-BIG PA project directly illustrates this practical need.

### Project rule

Professional support is compatible with PV if participants retain meaningful:

- topic control;
- representational authority;
- editorial decision rights.

---

# 40. PV vs Digital Storytelling

Digital Storytelling commonly centers:

- individual first-person narrative;
- short digital story;
- still images / audio / short video;
- storytelling-circle feedback;
- individual authorship.

PV more often emphasizes:

- group process;
- rotating collaborative roles;
- collective filming;
- collective reflection;
- collaborative production;
- community/external dialogue.

### Caveat

The boundary is not purely technological.

Both can use phones/video.

Classify by process and authorship architecture.

---

# 41. PV vs video elicitation

Video elicitation may involve researchers showing participants video to prompt discussion.

Examples:

- replaying clinical encounter;
- showing training footage;
- showing researcher-recorded environment.

That can be useful qualitative data generation.

### Critical rule

Video elicitation ≠ PV unless participants also have meaningful role in the production/governance process.

---

# 42. PV vs videography / video ethnography

Video ethnography can use video as observational field data.

The camera may remain controlled by researchers.

PV redistributes production and interpretive roles toward participants.

### Rule

Participant visibility in footage does not establish participatory production.

---

# 43. PV vs peer video interview

A peer-to-peer video interview can remove the researcher from the immediate encounter.

But one peer interview exercise does not necessarily create the extended PV process.

### Project rule

Store peer video interview as a possible PV method/component when:

- participant control;
- playback/reflection;
- collective analysis;
- production/dissemination governance

are present.

Otherwise classify more narrowly.

---

# 44. Duration

PV can be implemented over different timescales.

Current methodological guidance cautions that very short projects can:

- over-focus on film product;
- underdevelop trust;
- increase exposure risk;
- reduce genuine participant control.

### Project rule

Long duration is not an absolute validity requirement.

But short projects have a higher burden to demonstrate:

- meaningful participation;
- adequate consent;
- safe dissemination;
- real decision authority.

---

# 45. “Empowerment” must be evidenced, not assumed

PV literature historically uses empowerment language heavily.

Shaw's critical work shows that empowerment cannot simply be inferred from:

- holding a camera;
- speaking on film;
- attending workshops.

### Stronger evidence

Report observable changes such as:

- increased decision authority;
- media literacy;
- confidence;
- group agency;
- actual stakeholder access;
- changed programme decisions;
- new community organisation.

---

# 46. Social-change claim hierarchy

PV can plausibly support different levels:

## Level 1 — expression

Participants communicate experience.

## Level 2 — reflection

Participants reinterpret experience.

## Level 3 — group agency

Group priorities / action capacity change.

## Level 4 — community dialogue

Wider community discussion changes.

## Level 5 — external influence

Stakeholders respond / decisions change.

### Critical rule

Do not claim Level 5 merely because a film was screened externally.

---

# 47. Direct PA exemplar — NU-BIG

Sommer et al. 2023 report a Participatory Video evaluation inside the NU-BIG physical-activity programme.

Participants:

- six women in difficult life situations.

Purpose:

- create a video reflecting long-term effects of a PA programme.

The process lasted:

- November 2021 to July 2023.

### Relevance

This is a direct physical-activity-program evaluation using PV as a participatory visual process.

---

# 48. NU-BIG process evidence

Researchers used:

- semi-standardized monitoring;
- 12 semi-structured interviews;
- field notes;
- reflexive focus-group interview

to study the video-making process itself.

### Findings

Early in the process:

- video creation generated insecurity;
- active participation could be inhibited;
- sustained researcher support was needed;
- media-professional help was needed for technical tasks.

Later:

- media literacy increased;
- self-confidence increased;
- critical awareness increased.

### Critical methodological lesson

PV does not automatically feel empowering from the first session.

Capability and agency can develop progressively.

---

# 49. NU-BIG participant control

Women explicitly insisted the video should be produced:

> on their terms.

Researchers had to:

- reflect on their role;
- relinquish control;
- adapt facilitation;
- respond flexibly to participant priorities.

### Methodological lesson

Researcher loss of predetermined control is not project failure.

It can be evidence that participation is becoming substantively real.

---

# 50. NU-BIG PA claim limits

The PV process can support claims about:

- women's interpretations of programme effects;
- participant experience;
- empowerment/voice process;
- media literacy;
- perceived long-term meaning of PA;
- marginalization and self-representation;
- programme evaluation priorities.

It cannot by itself establish:

- causal effect of BIG on PA;
- objective long-term health benefit;
- comparative intervention efficacy;
- prevalence of participant experiences;
- population representativeness.

---

# 51. PV as evaluation layer vs intervention-effect design

A PA programme can have:

**programme/intervention layer**  
+  
**Participatory Video evaluation layer**  
+  
**other quantitative/qualitative evaluation designs.**

### Critical rule

The PV layer does not replace:

- RCT;
- quasi-experiment;
- cohort;
- validated repeated outcomes

when those are needed for intervention-effect questions.

# 56. Confidence

## HIGH

Well-supported:

- Participatory Video is a distinct participatory-visual/process methodology family.
- The Fogo Process is a major historical precursor.
- PV has additional roots in participatory development, Freirean dialogue and community arts.
- Process is methodologically more important than simply producing a film.
- Current extended PV architecture includes group formation, internal reflection, peer videoing/sense-making, community dialogue/mobilisation and external engagement.
- Recording/playback can function as an analytic/reflexive loop.
- Participant media literacy and production-role access are central to meaningful participation.
- Participant control should extend beyond filming into editorial and dissemination decisions.
- Facilitators remain powerful actors and require explicit reflexivity.
- Intra-group power can produce new exclusions even when researcher power is reduced.
- A small PV group cannot automatically represent an entire community.
- Consent in moving-image research is staged/iterative rather than reducible to one initial form.
- Participant vs filmed-contributor roles must be distinguished.
- Footage ownership, editing authority, storage, reuse and dissemination are distinct governance questions.
- PV is distinct from Photovoice, documentary filmmaking, digital storytelling and generic video elicitation.
- Direct physical-activity-program application is verified in NU-BIG.
- PV can support experiential/evaluation/agency claims but does not by itself establish intervention efficacy or prevalence.
