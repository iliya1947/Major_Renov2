# 2. Canonical ethnographic purpose

Ethnography is not simply observation of people in a real-world setting.

Its core scientific target is:

understand a social or cultural world through the meanings, practices, relationships, norms, material arrangements and interactions that organize life in context.

Typical questions:

- How does a group understand and enact practice X?
- What shared meanings and norms organize behavior in setting Y?
- How do interactions, artifacts, space and routines shape action?
- How are identities, roles and hierarchies produced in everyday practice?
- How does a community or subculture make sense of body, exercise, health, risk or performance?

### Critical rule

A study is not ethnographic merely because:

- it happens in a natural setting;
- the researcher observes behavior;
- interviews are conducted with members of one group;
- field notes are written.

A defensible ethnographic claim requires an ethnographic analytic orientation toward social/cultural meaning, practice and context.

---

# 3. Ethnography is methodology, not one data-collection method

Ethnography can combine:

- participant observation;
- non-participant observation;
- informal conversation;
- formal interviews;
- ethnographic interviews;
- field notes;
- documents;
- photographs;
- audio/video;
- artifacts;
- maps/spatial records;
- digital traces;
- archival material.

### Rule

Observation is a method.

Ethnography is a broader methodology/analytic orientation.

Likewise:

an interview can be ethnographic, phenomenological, grounded-theory, descriptive, narrative or part of another design.

---

# 4. Conventional/classic ethnography

Conventional ethnography typically emphasizes:

1. sustained engagement with a social world;
2. naturalistic fieldwork;
3. openness to revising the original problem definition;
4. attention to everyday practice, not only participant self-report;
5. contextual interpretation;
6. emic understanding plus analytic interpretation;
7. reflexivity about researcher position;
8. thick contextual description.

## Prolonged engagement

Long-term field presence is historically characteristic because it helps researchers:

- build rapport;
- see routines as well as exceptional events;
- detect contradictions between stated norms and enacted practice;
- understand language and tacit rules;
- observe change;
- become sensitive to what insiders take for granted.

### Critical rule

There is no universal minimum duration that makes a study ethnographic.

Duration is evidence about depth and opportunity for immersion, not an atomic validity threshold.

---

# 5. Focused ethnography

## 5.1 Foundational framing

Knoblauch describes focused ethnography as a complementary form of ethnography suited to socially and functionally differentiated settings.

Typical features include:

- concentrated research question;
- specific social situation/subculture;
- shorter or episodic field visits;
- high data intensity;
- high analytic time intensity;
- heavy use of recording technologies;
- team-based data sessions more often than in classic lone-fieldworker models.

## 5.2 What focused ethnography is not

It is not simply:

- "quick ethnography";
- "short observation study";
- a compromise version of real ethnography;
- any qualitative project with a narrow question.

A focused ethnographic study still needs:

- ethnographic orientation;
- contextual interpretation;
- attention to shared practices/meanings;
- openness to disconfirming assumptions;
- reflexivity;
- coherent field/data strategy.

## 5.3 2023 critical correction

Trundle and Phillips reviewed how focused and "traditional" ethnography were actually practiced in health research.

They found that commonly claimed binaries such as:

- short vs long;
- narrow vs broad;
- insider vs outsider;
- applied vs theoretical;

often failed to map cleanly onto empirical differences in practice.

### Project rule

Do not classify focused ethnography solely by short duration.

Preferred representation:

focused ethnography = a deliberately bounded/focused ethnographic inquiry adapted to a specific setting/problem, often using time-intensive concentrated fieldwork and health/applied-research conventions.

Duration is metadata, not the defining axis.

---

# 6. Focus does not mean conceptual closure

A major risk in focused ethnography is predefining the phenomenon so tightly that the ethnographer cannot discover that participants organize reality differently.

Example methodological error:

researcher begins with "Why do these people fail to exercise?"

This may pre-impose:

- what counts as exercise;
- what counts as failure;
- why non-participation is the central problem.

A more ethnographically open formulation might ask:

How do members of this group understand movement, exercise, self-care and daily activity, and how are these practices organized?

### Critical rule

Even a focused ethnography must preserve enough openness for participants' categories and practices to challenge the investigator's starting assumptions.

---

# 7. Participant observation

Participant observation historically has a central and often defining role in ethnography.

It allows the researcher to examine:

- what people do;
- how they interact;
- tacit routines;
- embodied skills;
- space;
- objects;
- timing;
- informal norms;
- discrepancies between talk and action.

## Degrees of participation

Researcher roles can vary from:

- complete/strong participant;
- participant-as-observer;
- observer-as-participant;
- mainly non-participant observer.

These are not simple quality rankings.

Choice depends on:

- ethics;
- access;
- safety;
- research question;
- researcher competence;
- setting.

### Fitness example

A researcher who actually trains in a bouldering community may gain embodied/sensory knowledge unavailable to a detached observer.

But that increased experiential access also changes:

- relationships;
- positionality;
- risk of over-identification;
- what participants reveal.

---

# 8. Non-participant/direct observation

Observation without active participation can still be ethnographically valuable.

It may be preferable when:

- participation is unsafe;
- clinical/professional roles cannot be assumed;
- researcher intervention would alter the practice;
- the target is interaction or workflow;
- participation is ethically inappropriate.

### Critical boundary

Non-participant observation does not become ethnography automatically.

The analysis still needs an ethnographic target:

shared practice, culture, meaning, relationships, context or social organization.

---

# 9. Ethnographic interview boundary

A major current methodological update is that participant observation should not be treated as universally mandatory for an interview to be ethnographic.

Trundle et al. argue that ethnographic interviewing can stand as a valid method when it preserves an ethnographic epistemic disposition.

They identify orientations such as:

- humility;
- readiness to revise core assumptions;
- attentiveness to context;
- relationality;
- openness to complexity;
- attention to ethnographic writing;
- attention to politics/history of the method.

### Project rule

Do not classify:

interviews only → not ethnography

automatically.

Instead ask:

1. Is the scientific target ethnographic?
2. Is the interview method contextual, relational and open enough to produce ethnographic knowledge?
3. Is the analysis cultural/social/practice-oriented?
4. Is the researcher explicit about ethnographic methodology?

This is a refinement of the older "participant observation is always required" rule.

---

# 10. Field notes

Field notes can include:

- descriptive observations;
- contextual details;
- interactions;
- spatial/material arrangements;
- informal speech;
- temporal sequences;
- researcher reactions;
- analytic ideas;
- methodological decisions.

Useful distinction:

## Descriptive field notes

What happened?

## Reflexive notes

How did researcher position, emotion, expectation or relationship shape access/interpretation?

## Analytic notes/memos

What patterns, contradictions or conceptual possibilities are emerging?

### Rule

Field notes are produced data, not a transparent recording of reality.

Researcher attention determines what enters the record.

---

# 11. Emic and etic

## Emic

Insider/member meanings, categories and ways of understanding the social world.

Questions:

- What does "hard training" mean to this gym community?
- How do members distinguish commitment from obsession?
- What counts as "cheating" a movement?
- What counts as a legitimate athlete?

## Etic

Researcher/analytic concepts used to interpret and compare social patterns.

Examples:

- hierarchy;
- identity work;
- embodied capital;
- norm enforcement;
- gendered practice;
- risk culture.

### Critical rule

Emic and etic are not mutually exclusive alternatives.

Strong ethnography often moves between:

emic understanding ↔ etic analytic interpretation.

Researcher position can also move along insider/outsider continua rather than occupying one fixed binary state.

---

# 12. Thick description

Ethnographic description should preserve enough context to explain the meaning of an act.

Not merely:

Participant increased load.

But potentially:

- who was present;
- who suggested the load;
- what language was used;
- whether peers watched;
- what local norm made the action meaningful;
- how the lifter reacted;
- what status/risk/gender/performance expectations were operating.

### Rule

Thick description is not simply "more words".

It is contextualized interpretation of meaning.

---

# 13. Naturalistic setting

Ethnography usually studies practices where they naturally occur:

- gym;
- team environment;
- rehabilitation clinic;
- competition;
- online training community;
- coaching session;
- community exercise group.

But "naturalistic" should not be interpreted naively.

Researcher presence can alter behavior.

Therefore record:

- access conditions;
- observer visibility;
- relationship duration;
- role disclosure;
- reactivity;
- whether participants adapted to researcher presence.

---

# 14. Sampling in ethnography

Ethnographic sampling is not one stage performed only before fieldwork.

Relevant levels include:

1. site selection;
2. community/subculture selection;
3. event/activity selection;
4. time-period selection;
5. participant selection;
6. key informants;
7. episodes/interactions;
8. documents/artifacts.

## Purposeful site selection

Choose a setting because it gives access to the social world relevant to the question.

## Key informants

Participants with especially rich cultural/practice knowledge.

They are not automatically representative of the whole group.

## Maximum variation / contrast

Can help reveal:

- different roles;
- status positions;
- experience levels;
- demographic differences;
- competing subgroups;
- different settings/times.

## Emergent sampling

Fieldwork may reveal that additional:

- actors;
- sites;
- events;
- documents;
- times of day;

need to be sampled.

### Rule

Sampling unit in ethnography can be more than "person".

The project must record social situations and sites as sampling units where relevant.

---

# 15. Site and boundary definition

Ethnographic "field" is not necessarily one physical place.

Possible fields:

- one gym;
- several gyms;
- gym + online Discord;
- team facility + travel + competition;
- coach-athlete interactions across digital and physical spaces;
- community program + home practice.

## Multi-sited ethnography

Appropriate when the social phenomenon moves across locations/networks.

### Rule

Do not equate:

one site = ethnography;
multiple sites = better ethnography.

The field boundary should follow the phenomenon and research question.

---

# 16. Team ethnography

Focused/applied ethnography often uses teams.

Potential advantages:

- parallel observation;
- multiple disciplinary viewpoints;
- broader site coverage;
- joint data sessions;
- challenge to one observer's interpretation.

Potential costs:

- inconsistent observation styles;
- different access relationships;
- different positionalities;
- loss of experiential continuity.

### Project extraction

Record:

- number of field researchers;
- role assignment;
- shared field-note structure;
- calibration/data sessions;
- how conflicting interpretations were handled.

---

# 17. Reflexivity and positionality

Ethnography has unusually strong researcher-field interaction.

Important dimensions:

- insider vs outsider status;
- sport/training expertise;
- gender;
- age;
- class;
- ethnicity/cultural background;
- professional status;
- coach/researcher dual role;
- athlete/researcher dual role;
- physical ability/disability;
- prior relationship with the group.

### Example

A strength coach studying a powerlifting gym may:

- understand technical language immediately;
- notice subtle training practices;
- gain trust faster.

But may also:

- normalize questionable practices;
- miss what outsiders find strange;
- influence members through perceived expertise.

### Rule

Insider status is neither automatically superior nor automatically biased.

It changes access and interpretation and must be reflexively handled.

---

# 18. Data analysis

Ethnographic analysis should preserve relationships among:

- action;
- meaning;
- context;
- social structure;
- material environment;
- time;
- researcher encounter.

Possible analytic procedures include:

- iterative coding;
- thematic analysis;
- domain/taxonomic analysis;
- narrative interpretation;
- situational mapping;
- constant comparison;
- description → analysis → interpretation frameworks.

### Critical rule

Coding alone is insufficient to define ethnographic analysis.

The analysis should return coded fragments to:

- social situation;
- cultural meaning;
- field context;
- interactional pattern.

Current methods literature explicitly warns against reducing ethnography to decontextualized coding.

---

# 19. Triangulation

Ethnography often combines sources:

- observation;
- interview;
- field notes;
- documents;
- artifacts;
- video;
- records.

Triangulation can reveal:

## Convergence

What people say matches what they do.

## Complementarity

Different methods reveal different dimensions.

## Dissonance

Stated values conflict with enacted practice.

### Critical rule

Triangulation is not a vote in which majority sources determine truth.

Contradiction can itself be the central ethnographic finding.

---

# 20. Time and immersion

## Conventional ethnography

Longer engagement is typical and often useful for:

- rapport;
- routine observation;
- temporal change;
- tacit norms;
- language competence.

## Focused ethnography

Often uses:

- shorter;
- repeated;
- strategically timed;
- high-intensity field visits.

### Modern correction

Do not enforce a simplistic cutoff such as:

less than X months = focused;
more than X months = conventional.

Duration should be interpreted relative to:

- scope;
- social world;
- event frequency;
- prior researcher familiarity;
- data intensity;
- research question.

---

# 21. What ethnographic evidence can establish

Ethnography can strongly support claims about:

- shared practices;
- local norms;
- social organization;
- cultural meanings;
- identity;
- hierarchy;
- tacit knowledge;
- embodied practice;
- interactions;
- material/spatial influences;
- discrepancies between stated and enacted behavior;
- context;
- change within the observed field.

It is particularly useful for phenomena that are:

- difficult to self-report;
- taken for granted;
- interactional;
- embodied;
- spatial;
- ritualized;
- norm-governed.

---

# 22. What ethnographic evidence cannot establish by itself

Ethnography alone does not estimate:

- population prevalence;
- incidence;
- causal effect size;
- treatment efficacy;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism.

## Example

Observation that high-status gym members normalize training through pain can support:

a local cultural/normative explanation.

It does not establish:

training through pain causes more injuries.

That causal claim requires different evidence.

---

# 23. Transferability and generalization

Ethnography does not primarily aim for statistical representativeness.

Inference is usually:

- contextual;
- conceptual;
- theoretical;
- transferable by similarity of social mechanism/context.

### Rule

Do not convert:

observed in one CrossFit gym

into:

all CrossFit gyms operate this way.

Instead ask:

- what context produced the pattern?;
- what features may plausibly transfer?;
- where are comparison cases needed?

---

# 24. Boundary with case study

Ethnography and case study can overlap.

## Ethnography

Primary organizing target:
- culture;
- shared practice;
- social world;
- meaning-in-context.

## Case study

Primary organizing target:
- a bounded case/system.

A study of one gym can therefore be:

- ethnography;
- case study;
- ethnographic case study;
- mixed-method case study.

### Rule

"One site" does not automatically mean case study.

"Culture" in one site does not automatically mean ethnography unless ethnographic methodology is actually implemented.

---

# 25. Boundary with qualitative description

If a study asks:

What barriers do gym users report?

and only interviews participants to summarize reported barriers, it may fit qualitative description.

If it asks:

How are norms of legitimate effort, pain and status produced and enacted in this gym?

and uses contextual field engagement to understand practice, ethnography fits better.

---

# 26. Boundary with phenomenology

Phenomenology targets:

lived experience / experiential meaning.

Ethnography targets:

shared social/cultural meanings and practices in context.

Overlap is possible.

Example:

What does training pain feel like to an injured athlete?
→ phenomenology.

How does a bodybuilding community distinguish "good pain" from injury pain, and how is that norm socially enforced?
→ ethnography.

---

# 27. Boundary with grounded theory

Grounded theory targets:

development of an explanatory process theory.

Ethnography targets:

culture/practice/social organization in context.

An ethnography can generate theory, and GT can include observation.

### Rule

Classify by primary methodology and scientific architecture, not by one method.

# 30. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| observation vs ethnography | observation is a method |
| participant observation vs all ethnography | historically central but not universally mandatory in contemporary ethnographic interviewing |
| interview-only vs automatically non-ethnographic | ethnographic interviewing can be valid if ethnographic disposition/analysis is present |
| natural setting vs ethnography | setting alone is insufficient |
| one group vs culture | shared membership does not guarantee a cultural analytic target |
| field notes vs ethnography | notes alone do not establish methodology |
| long duration vs quality | time helps but has no universal threshold |
| short duration vs focused ethnography | focused ethnography is not defined only by short duration |
| focused ethnography vs quick-and-dirty study | focused still requires ethnographic rigor |
| emic vs etic as mutually exclusive | strong ethnography often moves between both |
| insider researcher vs better ethnography | insider status changes access and blind spots |
| thick description vs verbosity | thick description contextualizes meaning |
| triangulation vs majority vote | disagreement across sources can be analytically important |
| coding vs ethnographic analysis | decontextualized coding can erase ethnographic meaning |
| one-site ethnography vs case study | organizing logic differs |
| culture/practice explanation vs causal effect | ethnography does not estimate causal magnitude |
| local pattern vs universal population claim | transferability is contextual |
