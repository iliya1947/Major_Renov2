# REL-X01.2 — Love, closeness, responsiveness, intimacy and sexual desire

Date: 2026-11-26. Status: COMPLETE_FOR_SPECIFIED_SCOPE (internal reasoning review only); REL-X01 IN_PROGRESS.

## 1. Question and dependencies
How can components of love, experienced intimacy, enacted and perceived responsiveness, sexual desire and autonomous relationship choice be distinguished in a dyadic analysis? Reopened REL-X01.1 in full at active branch. Retain WOM-03 and IND-03 construct, individual-transfer and revision rules; these dependencies were not reread in this continuation. Scope: selected adult romantic relationship evidence, with initial-acquaintance and diverse relationship samples identified explicitly. Claim types: measurement, association, prediction and limited experimental inference. No universal relationship progression, diagnosis or method for inducing desire. Closure requires convergent and contrary evidence, discriminating rules and synthetic checks.

## 2. Evidence and provenance
Targeted public searches 2026-11-26: cross-cultural triangular love scale validation and measurement challenges; daily-diary interpersonal intimacy process; perceived versus enacted responsiveness and projection; responsiveness and sexual desire in established relationships versus initial acquaintances; dyadic daily desire; sexual communal strength versus controlled motivation. Selected primary studies, not a systematic review. Author abstracts and selected publisher sections were directly inspected; no full article read end-to-end. Several PubMed/PMC pages returned CAPTCHA. No private details were included in searches.

E1. Sorokowski et al. (2021 issue), Universality of the Triangular Theory of Love: Adaptation and Psychometric Properties of the Triangular Love Scale in 25 Countries. https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2020.1787318 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32783568/ . Complete author abstract directly inspected. Analytic sample 7,332 from 25 countries after exclusions. Authors report support for cross-cultural configural, metric and scalar invariance, and associations of component scores with relationship duration. This supports an operational measurement model in sampled populations, not universal completeness of the theory, identical meanings for each individual, or a longitudinal sequence inferred from duration comparisons. Detailed fit, sampling, exclusions and uncertainty were not independently inspected. Intimacy, passion and decision/commitment are related but analytically distinguishable dimensions.

E2. Braida et al. (2023 online), Loving in Consensual Non-Monogamies: Challenging the Validity of Sternberg's Triangular Love Scale. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12119-023-10092-0 . Publisher theory, recruitment, sample, discussion and conclusion excerpts directly inspected. Online community recruitment, 558 completed questionnaires from respondents in 33 countries with at least two sexual and/or affective relationships; age range 16–70. Authors report that STLS items did not separate cleanly into the expected three factors and discuss wording about exclusivity and conventional progression. Selective recruitment, uneven country representation, mixed ages and unverified detailed fit indices limit transfer. Counterexample to blanket applicability of a scale; not proof the underlying love components are absent or that all consensually non-monogamous relationships share a single structure. Consent to non-monogamy is a separate fact, not inferable from multiple relationships.

E3. Laurenceau, Barrett & Rovine (2005), The interpersonal process model of intimacy in marriage: a daily-diary and multilevel modeling approach. https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.19.2.314 ; https://www.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/publications/journal_articles/2819 . Complete author abstract directly inspected via institutional page. Both members of 96 married couples reported daily for 42 days. Own and partner disclosure each contributed to same-day intimacy estimates, and perceived responsiveness statistically accounted for part of their association. Daily measures improve temporal resolution and dyadic coverage but contemporaneous mediation is not an identified causal process; shared method, reverse direction and day-specific circumstances remain possible. Exact effects and attrition not verified. Earlier Laurenceau et al. (1998) event-contingent diary study https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9599440/ was retrieved in search but its complete findings not inspected; not counted as additional verified evidence.

E4. Projection of responsiveness to needs and the construction of satisfying communal relationships (2007). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17484608/ . Indexed author abstract inspected; direct open CAPTCHA. Two dyadic marriage studies reported that a person's own self-rated responsiveness predicted their perception of spousal responsiveness more strongly than the spouse's self-rated responsiveness did. A third study manipulated difficulty recalling one's own supportive acts and reported an effect on perceived partner responsiveness. This is evidence that perceiver-side processes can matter, not proof that a given perception is false or that spouse self-report is ground truth. Sample counts, detailed design and coefficients not verified.

E5. Birnbaum et al. (2016), Intimately connected: The importance of partner responsiveness for experiencing sexual desire. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000069 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27399250/ . Indexed author abstract and institutional extracts; direct PubMed CAPTCHA. Three studies involving a presumed online interaction with an existing partner, coded face-to-face interactions, and daily reports. Overall positive responsiveness-desire association reported, stronger for women in these samples. Experimental and observational outcomes must not be conflated; exact manipulation, sample counts, estimates, and moderator robustness not checked here. No inference that support produces attraction in an uninterested person or that every woman's response is positive. Perceived responsiveness and actual behavior are not identical.

E6. Birnbaum et al. (2014), Why Do Men Prefer Nice Women? Gender Typicality Mediates the Effect of Responsiveness on Perceived Attractiveness in Initial Acquaintanceships. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167214543879 . Complete publisher abstract directly inspected, full text restricted. Three studies (one observational, two experimental) in opposite-sex stranger contexts; reported attractiveness-related benefits of perceived gender typicality following responsiveness for men, not women. Context-specific contrast to E5, not a direct contradiction because relationship stage, outcome, and protocol differ. Absence of a reported effect is not proof of equivalence or negative effects; detailed interactions, sample counts and precision not verified. No sex-specific prescription.

E7. Impett, Muise & Harasymchuk (2019 issue), Giving in the bedroom: The costs and benefits of responding to a partner's sexual needs in daily life. https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407518787349 . Complete publisher abstract directly inspected; full text restricted. 122 community couples, 21-day dyadic diary. Day-level motivation to respond to a partner's sexual needs was associated with both partners' satisfaction; neglect of one's own needs in the process was associated with lower own satisfaction, without the corresponding partner effect. Attention-related statistical pathways are not established causal mediators. This differentiates mutually responsive motivation from self-neglect, not a recommendation to engage in unwanted activity.

E8. Shoikhedbrod et al. (2023 issue), Being Responsive and Self-Determined When it Comes to Sex: How and Why Sexual Motivation is Associated with Satisfaction and Desire in Romantic Relationships. https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2022.2130132 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36223095/ . Complete author abstract directly inspected. Cross-sectional study (103 couples) and longitudinal study (147 couples). Autonomous rather than controlled sexual motivation was associated with more favorable desire and satisfaction patterns among those responsive to partner needs; self-neglect was associated with more controlled motivation and lower satisfaction, including partner associations. Observational paths cannot identify causation; overlap of participants with other studies from this research network not established. Motivation quality, desire, behavior and consent remain distinct. No prescribed obligation.

E9. Prekatsounaki, Loeys & Enzlin (2025 issue), Understanding the Ebbs and Flows of Sexual Desire: A Daily Diary Study on the Temporal Associations Between Dyadic Sexual Desire and Partner Interactions. https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2024.2393378 ; https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/01JDW1S7QJWTS7AES7DRS389WM . Complete institutional author abstract directly inspected. 163 mixed-gender couples, 22 daily reports, experimental group received interaction assignments. Daily desire covaried with giving/receiving/taking across affiliation, sexuality and exploration; women's desire was associated with perceived but not enacted responsiveness in sexuality and exploration domains. The abstract describes association results; the existence of randomization does not make these coefficients causal. Specific randomized assignment effects, confidence intervals and missingness not verified. No claim of successful desire intervention. Null enacted associations do not prove behavior irrelevant, and findings do not imply a universally female mechanism.

## 3. Integrated construct map
Separate:
- love as an umbrella construct; its operational components (experienced closeness, passion, intention to maintain a bond);
- intimacy as subjective connection in an interaction and as a broader relationship evaluation;
- disclosure (own, partner-reported and perceived; content and voluntariness);
- enacted responsiveness (behavior), perceived responsiveness (recipient's appraisal of being understood, valued and cared for), provider's motive, and effect on recipient;
- partner-specific sexual desire, general sexual interest, romantic attraction, arousal, behavior, enjoyment, satisfaction and consent;
- desire for a type of relationship, commitment to it, and actual mutual agreement.

None of these pairs is interchangeable. Self-report, partner-report and independent coding have different measurement errors. Responding to needs is not identical to agreeing, supplying resources, constant availability or compliance. A warm relationship can have limited romantic or sexual desire. Conversely, desire does not establish commitment, compatibility or consent.

Hypothesis map, not an identified causal graph: voluntary disclosure and response behavior can influence perceived responsiveness; prior expectations, own responsiveness, trust, context and current affect can also influence perception; perceived connection and desire may covary with one another and with other relational experiences. Reverse direction, actor/partner effects and shared contextual influences remain viable. A person's existing liking may shape how a response is interpreted. Autonomy versus controlled motivation may differentiate superficially similar behavior. Do not assume a single linear intimacy-to-desire pathway or apply findings from established couples to an unreciprocated relationship.

## 4. Operational inference rules
1. Define actor, target person, construct, outcome, time window and evidence channel. Separate feelings, intended relationship, freely stated choice and enacted behavior.
2. Evaluate closeness, initiative, disclosure and responsiveness on their own dimensions. Compare recipient appraisal, provider's account and naturally observed actions; no one channel establishes all three.
3. Compare competing accounts: affection without romantic preference, attraction with limited opportunity, relationship-specific change, general state/context, interpretive bias, autonomous care versus perceived obligation. Preserve possible coexistence.
4. For each account identify ordinary observations and voluntarily expressed preferences that could strengthen or weaken it. Do not use covert tests, pressure or manufactured emotional contingencies.
5. Interpret diary coefficients at the level actually modeled (between-person, within-person, actor, partner, contemporaneous or lagged). A statistical mediation model is not by itself a causal mechanism. Distinguish assigned intervention effects from observed within-study correlations.
6. Check scale validity for target relationship structure, culture and meaning of items. Do not infer universal stages or individual trajectories from cross-sectional duration patterns.
7. Consent is specific, freely given and revocable. Neither arousal, affection, history, reciprocity nor willingness to support establishes consent. Do not discount explicit choices because a theory predicts latent desire. No diagnosis from low desire alone.

## 5. Internal synthetic assessment
Rubric established before evaluating written responses: one criterion each for construct separation, competing explanation, distinguishing evidence and inference boundary. Critical errors include unsupported motives/diagnosis, group-to-person determinism, causal inflation, ignoring disconfirmation, coercion or privacy breach. Original neutral cases, not held-out.

A. Two respondents have similar total love scores but different intimacy and commitment scores. Response: totals obscure components; measurement interpretation and actual different profiles are alternatives. Inspect subscales, item relevance and voluntary descriptions. No conclusion of identical relationship goals. Four criteria addressed.
B. A recipient experiences little understanding despite a provider reporting extensive supportive actions. Response: behavior, intent and perception differ; mismatch in desired support and differing appraisal or reporting may coexist. Clarify the actual request and response through ordinary voluntary exchange; no assertion of ingratitude or deception. Four criteria addressed.
C. Daily perceived responsiveness and desire rise together. Response: distinguish perception, desire and acts; prior liking, shared context and reverse causation are viable. Repeated temporal measurements and appropriate comparisons could discriminate, not automatically identify mediation. No guaranteed desire effect. Four criteria addressed.
D. A person agrees to an activity but describes obligation rather than enjoyment. Response: behavior, autonomous motivation, desire and consent are separate; routine, genuine valued choice and pressure must be distinguished. Voluntary account and freedom to decline matter. No inference of consent or endorsement of obligation from agreement alone. Four criteria addressed.
E. An initial-acquaintance study and established-couple study give different gender moderation patterns. Response: outcomes and populations differ; relationship stage, protocol and sampling may account for difference. Need matched designs and direct interaction tests. No universal female or male response. Four criteria addressed.
F. A person feels close to a friend but explicitly declines a romantic relationship. Response: closeness and desired relationship type differ; enduring friendship or other priorities may coexist. Respect explicit decision and revise only on freely expressed changed preference. No hidden attraction inferred. Four criteria addressed.

Internal review: 24/24 rubric criteria addressed, no critical error identified in these authored responses. This is a methodological checklist, not independent assessment, validated predictive accuracy or model retraining.
