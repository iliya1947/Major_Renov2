# COG-03 — Attention: selection, priority, capture, sustained attention, and control interfaces

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Establish a current working architecture of attention that separates:

- attentional selection from perception;
- priority assignment from implementation of selection;
- goal-directed, stimulus-driven, and selection-history influences;
- selective attention from sustained attention/vigilance;
- divided attention from a mythical single "attention resource";
- attention from cognitive control, arousal, working memory, awareness, and motivation.

The aim is to prevent broad labels such as "poor attention", "distractibility", "hyperfocus", or "multitasking ability" from being treated as unitary mechanisms.

## Core rule

**Attention is a family of priority and selection processes operating under competition and capacity limits.**

A useful generic architecture is:

`candidate information -> priority computation -> selection/implementation -> downstream processing/action`

Priority can be shaped jointly by:

- current goals/task set;
- physical salience and stimulus properties;
- selection history / learned priority;
- reward and motivational relevance;
- working-memory contents;
- emotional significance;
- context and recent trial history.

These influences interact. They are not isolated channels.

## 1. Attention is not one homogeneous faculty

NIMH RDoC defines attention as a range of processes regulating access to capacity-limited systems such as awareness, higher perceptual processing, and motor action.

Selective and divided attention inherently involve:

- competition;
- capacity limitation;
- prioritization.

Therefore avoid:

`attention = one scalar resource`.

Different attention tasks stress different mechanisms.

## 2. Selection and control of selection are separable

The original RDoC attention framework distinguishes:

1. **control of attention** — processes that determine which sources of information receive higher/lower priority;
2. **implementation of selection** — local enhancement/suppression that realizes the priority pattern.

A performance failure can therefore reflect:

- bad priority assignment;
- poor implementation of an otherwise appropriate priority;
- downstream decision/motor factors;
- insufficient sensory evidence.

Canonical rule:

**attentional failure != one mechanism.**

## 3. Goal-directed attention

Goal-directed attention prioritizes information relevant to current intentions, instructions, or task sets.

Examples:

- search for a red object;
- attend to one spatial location;
- monitor one speaker;
- prioritize one target category.

Goal-directed selection usually depends on:

- maintaining task goals;
- attentional templates;
- context;
- working memory;
- cognitive control.

But goal relevance does not guarantee perfect selection.

## 4. Stimulus-driven / salience-based attention

Some stimuli receive priority because of their physical or temporal properties.

Examples:

- sudden onset;
- strong contrast;
- unique color/shape;
- abrupt motion;
- unexpected event.

Attentional capture refers to situations in which a salient object/event receives priority even when it conflicts with current goals.

However, the degree to which salience automatically captures attention remains context- and paradigm-dependent.

Do not treat "salient stimulus" as synonymous with "unavoidable capture."

## 5. Selection history is a third major influence

Modern attention research no longer fits well into a simple:

`top-down vs bottom-up`

dichotomy.

Selection history captures learned priority arising from prior experience.

Examples include prior:

- target selection;
- distractor suppression;
- reward association;
- punishment association;
- spatial probability;
- feature probability.

A 2025–2026 review literature broadly supports a tripartite framework involving:

- goal-directed influences;
- stimulus-driven influences;
- selection-history influences.

## 6. Selection history can also shape voluntary strategy

Selection history is not only an involuntary force competing against current goals.

Recent work argues that prior learning can bias:

- which attentional strategy is voluntarily selected;
- how a goal-directed search template is instantiated;
- what information is habitually prioritized.

Therefore:

`voluntary = free of history`

is false.

A person may intentionally search in a way that is partly shaped by learned attentional habits.

## 7. Reward and value can bias priority

Stimuli previously associated with reward can continue attracting attention even when reward is no longer relevant.

This demonstrates that learned value can modify attentional priority independently of:

- current goal;
- raw physical salience.

Recent 2026 work also frames value-modulated attention in terms of prediction/prioritization and learned exploitation/exploration.

Do not infer a stable motivational preference merely from attentional capture by a reward-associated cue.

## 8. Priority is dynamic, not a fixed ranking

Attentional priority can change over milliseconds and across contexts.

Factors include:

- target definition;
- cue validity;
- recent selections;
- reward history;
- spatial probability;
- task difficulty;
- competing items;
- working-memory state;
- fatigue/arousal.

A single "priority map" is best treated as a functional abstraction rather than proof of one fixed anatomical map.

## 9. Spatial, feature, object, and temporal attention are distinct

Attention can prioritize:

- **space** — where;
- **features** — color, orientation, motion, etc.;
- **objects** — structured units;
- **time** — when;
- **modalities** — vision, audition, etc.

These forms can interact but are not interchangeable.

A spatial-cueing result should not automatically be generalized to feature-based or temporal attention.

## 10. Overt and covert attention

### Overt attention

Selection accompanied by orienting movements such as eye/head movements.

### Covert attention

Priority shifts without corresponding overt movement.

Eye position is therefore informative but not a complete measure of attention.

Canonical rule:

**looking at != necessarily attending to**

and:

**not looking at != necessarily no attention**.

## 11. Attention and perception remain distinct

Attention can modify perceptual processing by changing:

- sensory gain;
- signal-to-noise relation;
- spatial/feature priority;
- effective precision;
- readout.

But attention is the selection/prioritization process, not the perceptual representation itself.

A perceptual effect may be attention-mediated without attention and perception becoming identical constructs.

## 12. Attention and working memory interact bidirectionally

Working-memory contents can guide selection of external information.

Attention can also select among representations held internally.

This means an attentional bias can originate from:

- externally visible salience;
- current goal;
- prioritized working-memory content;
- learned selection history.

Do not infer a persistent external attentional preference when the effect may be temporary working-memory guidance.

## 13. Internal attention

Attention is not limited to external sensory input.

Selection can operate over internal representations such as:

- working-memory items;
- retrieved memories;
- imagined information;
- competing thoughts.

Internal and external attention can compete for limited processing.

Mind wandering is one example of increased allocation toward internally generated content, but it is not identical to every attentional lapse.

## 14. Sustained attention

Sustained attention concerns maintaining task-relevant selection/engagement over time.

Current reviews emphasize that its underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

Sustained attention should not be reduced to one fixed "capacity."

Performance over time can reflect:

- attentional engagement;
- arousal;
- motivation;
- expectancy;
- task monotony;
- response strategy;
- fatigue;
- learning;
- mind wandering.

## 15. Vigilance and sustained attention overlap but are not perfect synonyms

The literature uses these terms inconsistently.

A practical distinction is:

- **vigilance** — readiness to detect infrequent or critical events over time;
- **sustained attention** — maintaining directed task-relevant processing over time.

NIMH's behavioral-assessment work explicitly notes overlap among vigilance, sustained attention, and arousal.

Recent 2025–2026 reviews also argue that terminology remains unsettled.

Therefore:

`vigilance decrement = one specific mechanism exhausted`

is not justified.

## 16. Vigilance decrement

A vigilance decrement is declining detection performance over time on task.

Possible contributors include:

- changing criterion;
- reduced engagement;
- habituation;
- resource/effort costs;
- arousal changes;
- mind wandering;
- expectation changes;
- learning about event probability.

Observed decline is a phenomenon.

Its mechanism must be tested separately.

## 17. Mind wandering and sustained-attention failure

Mind wandering often increases with time on task and is associated with poorer vigilance performance.

However:

`mind wandering -> all sustained-attention decline`

is too strong.

Some lapses may occur without reportable mind wandering.

Mind wandering itself varies in:

- intentionality;
- awareness;
- emotional content;
- task relation.

Treat it as one competing internal allocation state, not a universal explanation.

## 18. Arousal and attention are distinct

Arousal is a broader activation/readiness state.

Attention concerns selective/directed access and priority.

Low or high arousal can impair attentional performance, but:

`arousal level = attention level`

is invalid.

This distinction matters especially in fatigue, sleep loss, stress, medication, and clinical contexts.

## 19. Divided attention

Divided-attention tasks require managing more than one stream/task.

Dual-task interference can arise from several possible constraints:

- shared capacity;
- central bottleneck;
- response-selection conflict;
- working-memory overlap;
- perceptual overlap;
- motor overlap;
- task-set interference.

The field does not support one universal bottleneck for every dual-task situation.

Recent studies continue to test bottleneck versus sharing accounts.

## 20. Multitasking performance is task-specific

Good performance on one dual-task combination does not establish general "multitasking ability."

Transfer depends on:

- component tasks;
- modality overlap;
- response overlap;
- practice;
- predictability;
- automaticity;
- scheduling strategy.

Canonical rule:

**multitasking ability must be specified relative to task architecture.**

## 21. Attentional blink is not a universal capacity meter

The attentional blink is reduced detection of a second target shortly after a first target in rapid serial presentation.

It is useful for studying temporal competition.

But individual performance can depend on:

- temporal expectations;
- target/distractor similarity;
- strategy;
- working-memory encoding;
- control.

Do not infer a broad attention capacity directly from one attentional-blink measure.

## 22. Visual search is a mixed attentional task

Visual search depends on:

- target template;
- distractor structure;
- salience;
- selection history;
- eye movements;
- stopping rule;
- response criterion;
- perceptual discriminability.

Recent ERP/behavioral work shows that attentional selection during search can sometimes be partially parallel rather than strictly serial.

Therefore old serial-versus-parallel dichotomies should not be treated as universally settled.

## 23. Distractor suppression is not simply "ignoring"

Reduced interference from a distractor can reflect:

- proactive suppression;
- learned spatial avoidance;
- feature-based suppression;
- rapid disengagement;
- weaker capture;
- changed criterion.

The mechanism must be distinguished.

A behavioral interference score alone rarely identifies which one occurred.

## 24. Attention and cognitive control

Cognitive control regulates cognition/action according to goals when prepotent or default behavior is inadequate.

Attention implements prioritization of information.

Their interface is strongest when control establishes:

- task goals;
- templates;
- priority settings;
- suppression policies.

But control also includes:

- response selection;
- inhibition;
- performance monitoring;
- task-rule updating.

Therefore:

**attentional control is part of the attention–control interface, not the whole of cognitive control.**

## 25. Attention and inhibition are not synonyms

Failure to attend a distractor can arise because it never received high priority.

That is different from selecting it and then suppressing/inhibiting it.

Similarly, response inhibition is not equivalent to attentional suppression.

Detailed inhibitory control is routed to EXE.

## 26. Attention and awareness are not synonyms

Attention strongly affects conscious access.

But there is continuing theoretical/empirical debate about:

- unattended processing;
- conscious perception without focal attention;
- report versus experience.

Therefore do not use:

`attended = conscious`

or

`unattended = not processed`

as universal rules.

## 27. Attention is shaped by motivation and emotion

Motivationally relevant or emotional information can alter priority.

Possible pathways include:

- reward learning;
- threat relevance;
- arousal;
- goals;
- selection history.

Attention toward emotionally salient information does not alone prove:

- preference;
- endorsement;
- desire;
- fear;
- clinical bias.

The psychological meaning requires additional evidence.

## 28. Attention is state-dependent

Attentional performance can vary with:

- sleep;
- fatigue;
- arousal;
- stress;
- motivation;
- pharmacological state;
- task engagement;
- recent learning.

A single poor performance session therefore provides weak evidence for a stable trait.

## 29. Attentional trait claims require repeated evidence

A stable trait-like inference such as "highly distractible" requires evidence across:

- occasions;
- task types;
- contexts;
- state conditions.

Prefer:

- repeated performance;
- converging tasks;
- ecologically relevant behavior;
- collateral/self-report evidence where appropriate.

One task failure cannot establish a general attentional phenotype.

## 30. Measurement problems

Common attention measures include:

- reaction time;
- RT variability;
- errors;
- omissions;
- false alarms;
- search slope;
- cueing effects;
- distractor costs;
- eye movements;
- electrophysiological indices;
- pupil measures;
- self-report.

Each indexes different combinations of processes.

No single one is a direct scalar "amount of attention."

## 31. Reaction-time variability

High RT variability can occur with attentional lapses, but also with:

- speed–accuracy tradeoff;
- strategy shifts;
- motor variability;
- fatigue;
- task learning;
- nonstationary criterion.

Therefore:

`high RT variability = attention disorder`

is invalid.

## 32. Attention task impurity

Attention tasks usually require:

- perception;
- working memory;
- motor response;
- task comprehension;
- cognitive control;
- motivation.

Thus a low attention-test score should first be decomposed into alternative bottlenecks.

This directly inherits the COG-01 task-impurity rule.

## 33. Attention Inference Gate

Before making an attentional inference, ask:

### A. Target process

1. Is the claim about selective, sustained, divided, spatial, feature, object, temporal, internal, or external attention?
2. Is the construct defined precisely?

### B. Priority source

3. What are the current task goals?
4. What stimuli are physically salient?
5. What selection history exists?
6. Are reward/threat associations relevant?
7. Could working-memory content guide attention?

### C. Selection mechanism

8. Is the effect priority assignment or implementation of selection?
9. Is there enhancement, suppression, capture, disengagement, or criterion change?
10. Does the measure distinguish these mechanisms?

### D. Perception/control boundary

11. Could the result be caused by sensory/perceptual differences?
12. Could cognitive control/task-set maintenance explain it?
13. Could response inhibition explain it?

### E. Sustained attention

14. Does performance change over time?
15. Could criterion, arousal, motivation, fatigue, or learning explain the trend?
16. Is mind wandering directly measured or merely inferred?

### F. Divided attention

17. Which task components overlap?
18. Is interference perceptual, central, memory-related, response-related, or motor?
19. Is one universal resource being assumed without evidence?

### G. State versus trait

20. Was the pattern repeated across occasions?
21. Across tasks?
22. Across contexts?
23. Could sleep/arousal/stress/motivation explain the session result?

### H. Measurement

24. What dependent measure is used?
25. What reliability/validity evidence supports it?
26. Is RT variability or an error rate being overinterpreted?
27. Does the task have known impurity?

### I. Generalization

28. Does the inference generalize beyond the exact task?
29. Does it generalize beyond the sensory modality?
30. Does it generalize from laboratory attention to everyday functioning?

### J. Conclusion

31. What exact attentional mechanism is supported?
32. Which neighboring mechanism remains viable?
33. What additional observation would discriminate the preferred interpretation?

## 34. High-value failure modes

### Failure A — attention = concentration

Incorrect.

Correct:

"Concentration" can reflect selective attention, sustained engagement, control, motivation, arousal, and task interest.

### Failure B — distractibility = weak top-down control

Incorrect as a default.

Correct:

Capture can reflect stimulus salience, selection history, goal settings, or implementation differences.

### Failure C — voluntary attention = free of learning history

Incorrect.

Correct:

Selection history can bias even voluntarily selected attentional strategies.

### Failure D — salience = inevitable capture

Incorrect.

Correct:

Capture depends on context, task, history, and control settings.

### Failure E — vigilance decrement = resource depletion proven

Incorrect.

Correct:

Declining performance is a phenomenon compatible with several mechanisms.

### Failure F — mind wandering = all attentional lapse

Incorrect.

Correct:

Mind wandering is one internal allocation state and should be measured rather than assumed.

### Failure G — arousal = attention

Incorrect.

Correct:

Arousal is a broader readiness state that can modulate attention.

### Failure H — dual-task cost = one central resource exhausted

Incorrect.

Correct:

Costs can arise from several task-specific bottlenecks or overlap structures.

### Failure I — attention task score = attention capacity

Incorrect.

Correct:

Task impurity and measurement properties must be evaluated.

### Failure J — response inhibition = attentional inhibition

Incorrect.

Correct:

Response inhibition and attentional suppression are different operations.

### Failure K — eye fixation = attention

Incorrect.

Correct:

Overt gaze and covert attention can dissociate.

### Failure L — one poor session = attentional trait

Incorrect.

Correct:

Trait inference requires repeated, cross-context evidence.

## Canonical working rule

**Specify what kind of attention is being inferred, identify what set the priority, separate selection from control and response processes, then test whether the pattern survives state/task/context alternatives.**

Never:

**poor performance -> "attention problem" -> stable trait.**

## Current-standard note

As of 2026-11-19:

- NIMH RDoC continues to define attention as a range of processes regulating access to capacity-limited systems under competition;
- current attention theory broadly recognizes goal-directed, stimulus-driven, and selection-history influences, although their exact decomposition and interaction remain theoretically debated;
- selection history is now understood to shape both involuntary prioritization and aspects of voluntarily chosen attentional strategy;
- sustained-attention/vigilance mechanisms remain unsettled despite decades of research, and current reviews explicitly caution against one-mechanism explanations;
- divided-attention interference remains compatible with several task-dependent bottleneck/resource-sharing accounts rather than one universal central-resource theory.

## Scope note

This block establishes an attention architecture suitable for psychological inference.

Detailed neural implementation, ADHD-specific attention findings, clinical attentional bias, executive inhibition, effort allocation, and attention-training interventions are deferred to NEU, NDEV, CLN, EXE, MOT, and BHC respectively.
