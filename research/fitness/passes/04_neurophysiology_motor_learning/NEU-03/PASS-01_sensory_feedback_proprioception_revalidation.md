## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a sufficiently accurate sensorimotor-feedback model for later reflex, balance, fatigue, technique and motor-learning work without:

- treating proprioception as one single sense or one receptor;
- equating proprioception with balance;
- equating joint-position reproduction error with pure peripheral proprioceptive acuity;
- treating muscle spindle Ia afferents as “velocity only” or II afferents as “position only” in an absolute sense;
- treating GTOs as simple emergency tension sensors that merely inhibit the same muscle;
- treating joint receptors as either irrelevant or sole joint-angle sensors;
- ignoring cutaneous contribution to body-state estimation;
- treating vision or vestibular input as proprioception itself;
- treating vision, vestibular and proprioceptive signals as fixed-weight inputs;
- treating position, movement and force sense as interchangeable;
- treating force sense as a direct readout of GTO activity alone;
- assuming fatigue always degrades proprioceptive accuracy to the same degree across joints/tasks.

### Question type

Sensory neurophysiology / multisensory state estimation for movement.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 12 exact Section 3 items must be covered at content level; muscle spindle, GTO, joint and cutaneous roles must remain distinct but integrated; visual/vestibular contributions must be separated from proprioception proper; position, movement and force sense must be distinguished; fatigue effects must remain task- and measurement-dependent; measurement tasks must not be treated as pure receptor assays.

### Evidence rule

Proprioception is treated here as a **multimodal body-state sensing system** derived primarily from internal somatosensory signals and integrated with central motor predictions.

Vision and vestibular signals contribute to the broader sensorimotor estimate but are not relabeled as proprioceptors.

Therefore:

`proprioceptive receptor signal`
≠
`conscious proprioceptive perception`
≠
`balance performance`
≠
`one joint-reposition test score`.

## 3. Proprioception

For current project purpose, proprioception includes perception/estimation of:

- body/limb position;
- movement/kinesthesia;
- force/effort-related state.

It depends on interacting inputs from:

- muscle spindles;
- Golgi tendon organs;
- joint/capsular receptors;
- cutaneous mechanoreceptors;
- central motor-command/efference-related signals.

Current 2026 reviews emphasize that proprioception is not merely passive peripheral feedback. It is integrated with internal predictions and task context.

Project rule:

**proprioception is a distributed sensorimotor estimate, not one receptor or one clinical test.**

Important boundary:

Balance is a multisensory motor outcome requiring proprioceptive, vestibular, visual and motor-control processes. Balance tests therefore do not isolate proprioception.

---

## 4. Muscle spindle

Muscle spindles are intramuscular proprioceptors arranged broadly in parallel with extrafusal muscle fibers.

They contain intrafusal fibers and sensory endings including:

- group Ia primary afferents;
- group II secondary afferents.

Useful applied model:

- Ia discharge is strongly sensitive to dynamic changes in muscle length/velocity and also carries static information;
- II discharge is more strongly associated with maintained muscle length/static state;
- both are modulated by fusimotor/gamma drive and mechanical context.

Muscle/tendon vibration can strongly alter spindle discharge and generate predictable illusions/errors in perceived movement and position.

The 2026 J Physiol integration review shows that CNS estimates limb state using both position- and velocity-related proprioceptive information.

Project rule:

**muscle spindle = major muscle-length / movement sensor, but Ia ≠ “velocity only” and II ≠ “position only.”**

---

## 5. Golgi tendon organ

Golgi tendon organs are located at musculotendinous interfaces and are innervated by group Ib afferents.

They respond strongly to muscle-generated tension/force and contribute information about mechanical loading.

Modern interpretation does not support the simple fitness cliché:

`high tension → GTO → same muscle switches off`.

Ib input enters spinal/interneuronal networks whose effects depend on:

- task;
- locomotor state;
- descending control;
- other afferent inputs.

Recent integrative modeling emphasizes that spindle and tendon-organ signals can be combined to estimate muscle mechanical state rather than functioning as isolated sensors.

Project rule:

**GTOs provide force/tension-related feedback; they are not simple emergency “autogenic inhibition switches.”**

Force perception is not a direct conscious readout of Ib firing alone.

---

## 6. Joint receptors

Joint/capsular mechanoreceptors can signal:

- joint deformation;
- pressure/tension;
- movement;
- especially mechanically salient regions/ranges.

Historical teaching often reduces them to “limit detectors” near end range.

Current evidence does not justify either extreme:
- they should not replace muscle spindles as the primary explanation for ordinary limb-position sense;
- they should not be treated as irrelevant.

A 2026 biomimetic study showed that Type-I-like joint-receptor signals can theoretically encode joint configuration with useful accuracy, but this is **not direct human receptor evidence** and is retained only as mechanistic support.

Project rule:

**joint receptors are supplementary/context-dependent contributors to proprioception, with exact weighting varying by joint, range and task.**

---

## 7. Cutaneous receptors

Skin mechanoreceptors contribute information through:

- stretch;
- pressure;
- shear;
- contact;
- local deformation.

Human evidence shows natural skin stretch during posture/movement is large enough to provide body-configuration information across joints and even trunk/face regions.

A 2026 active finger-motion experiment showed that experimentally augmenting natural skin stretch systematically shifted perceived finger posture despite intact muscle-spindle and efferent signals.

Thus:

`cutaneous input`
can alter
`proprioceptive estimate`.

Project rule:

**cutaneous sensation is not merely “touch”; skin deformation can materially contribute to limb/body-state estimation.**

---

## 8. Vision

Vision provides:

- external spatial reference;
- limb/environment position information;
- optic-flow/self-motion cues;
- error information during visually guided action.

Vision is **not proprioception**.

However visual and proprioceptive estimates are continuously integrated and can conflict.

The 2026 eLife work shows systematic motor biases can arise from misalignment between visual and proprioceptive reference frames.

Project rule:

**vision and proprioception are distinct sensory sources that are integrated into a common movement estimate.**

Removing vision does not simply “test proprioception” because task strategy and weighting of vestibular/cutaneous/proprioceptive inputs also change.

---

## 9. Vestibular system

Vestibular signals provide information about:

- head angular acceleration/rotation;
- linear acceleration;
- gravity/head orientation.

They are essential for:

- gaze stabilization;
- spatial orientation;
- postural control;
- self-motion estimation.

Vestibular input is also **not proprioception**.

Current visual-vestibular literature supports:
- multisensory integration;
- reliability-dependent weighting;
- causal inference when signals disagree.

Project rule:

**vestibular input supplies head-motion/gravity reference that is integrated with proprioception and vision.**

---

## 10. Sensorimotor integration

The CNS combines:

- proprioceptive afferents;
- cutaneous input;
- vision;
- vestibular signals;
- efference copy/internal predictions;
- task goals/context.

The weighting is not fixed.

When one source becomes:
- noisy;
- unavailable;
- misleading;
- task-irrelevant,

the nervous system can reweight other sources.

This is often described as **sensory reweighting**.

Current human standing and reaching studies show that visual and proprioceptive perturbations shift behavior and dependence across modalities.

Project rule:

**sensorimotor integration is reliability- and task-dependent multisensory weighting, not simple averaging.**

---

## 11. Position sense

Position sense is the estimate of where a body segment is located.

Contributors include:

- muscle spindle signals;
- cutaneous/joint information;
- central predictions/memory;
- visual information when available.

Important measurement boundary:

A joint-reposition task does **not** necessarily isolate peripheral receptor input.

Recent 2025–2026 work by Proske and colleagues shows that some position-matching paradigms can be solved partly through:
- remembered target position;
- centrally generated reference;
- motor-command-related information

rather than peripheral proprioception alone.

Different position-sense tasks can show weak cross-task agreement.

Project rule:

**position-sense test score = task-specific perceptual performance, not a pure spindle/JPS biomarker.**

---

## 12. Movement sense / kinesthesia

Movement sense includes perception of:

- movement occurrence;
- direction;
- velocity;
- displacement over time.

Muscle spindle Ia activity is particularly important for dynamic information.

Tendon vibration can create:
- illusory movement;
- biased perceived velocity;
- biased endpoint position.

The 2026 J Physiol work supports integration of proprioceptive velocity signals into ongoing position estimation.

Project rule:

**movement sense and position sense are related but separable perceptual dimensions.**

A person can perform differently across static-position and movement-detection tasks.

---

## 13. Force sense

Force sense is the perception/estimation of produced force or effort.

It is not equivalent to one receptor signal.

Relevant information can include:

- GTO/Ib feedback;
- spindle/cutaneous signals;
- muscle/joint mechanical consequences;
- efference copy / sense of effort from central motor command.

Therefore:

`force sense`
≠
`GTO firing alone`.

Current literature also shows force-sense tests differ substantially in:
- target force;
- feedback;
- contraction mode;
- reference method.

Project rule:

**force sense is a centrally integrated estimate using peripheral force-related feedback plus motor-command information.**

Detailed effort perception belongs PSY/NEU as relevant downstream.

---

## 14. Fatigue and sensory accuracy

Fatigue can alter proprioceptive performance through combinations of:

- spindle sensitivity/fusimotor changes;
- altered afferent input;
- central processing;
- motor-command changes;
- contractile-state changes;
- attention/cognitive load.

Current evidence supports an effect, but not a universal one.

### Lower-extremity meta-analysis

The 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis found fatigue increased **active absolute repositioning error** at:
- knee;
- ankle;
- hip.

However some passive or relative-error measures did not show significant effects.

### Knee review

The 2024 knee systematic review found higher repositioning errors after many local/general fatigue protocols but also substantial protocol/method heterogeneity.

### Postural control

The 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis shows exercise-induced fatigue can acutely impair postural control, but balance impairment is not identical to proprioceptive impairment.

### Mental fatigue

A 2026 small quasi-experimental study found worse shoulder repositioning consistency after mental fatigue but no clear knee effect.

Project rule:

**fatigue can degrade sensory/proprioceptive accuracy, but the magnitude/direction depends on joint, fatigue type, task and metric.**

No universal “fatigue always destroys proprioception” rule is retained.

## 15. Measurement / interpretation limits

Proprioception has no single universally accepted gold-standard test.

Common tests sample different constructs:

- active joint reposition → perception + memory + motor command + sensory feedback;
- passive reposition → more peripheral sensory weighting but still central judgment;
- threshold to detection of passive motion → movement detection;
- force reproduction → force/effort sense;
- vibration illusions → spindle-biased experimental manipulation;
- balance/postural sway → multisensory motor outcome.

Current 2026 literature shows:
- direct proprioceptive measures can correlate weakly with one another;
- task definition strongly changes what is measured;
- some “proprioceptive training” studies actually measure balance or general neuromuscular performance rather than sensory acuity.

Project rule:

**do not infer a global proprioception trait from one test.**

Detailed measurement validity belongs TST.

## 17. Established project-level model

### Peripheral information

`spindle length/velocity-related signals`
`+ GTO force/tension-related signals`
`+ joint/cutaneous signals`.

### Central integration

`peripheral afferents`
`+ efference copy / internal prediction`
`+ vision`
`+ vestibular reference`
`→ task-dependent sensory weighting`
`→ body-state estimate`
`→ motor correction / movement control`.

Therefore:

`one receptor`
does not equal
`one conscious proprioceptive sense`.

## 18. Training-relevant implications

1. **Do not equate proprioception with balance.**
2. **Do not treat one joint-reposition score as “the person’s proprioception.”**
3. **Do not reduce muscle spindles to pure velocity detectors or GTOs to protective shut-off switches.**
4. **Do not assume joint receptors only matter at end range or that they dominate normal position sense.**
5. **Include cutaneous feedback as part of body-state estimation.**
6. **Keep vision and vestibular input separate from proprioception while recognizing multisensory integration.**
7. **Expect sensory weighting to change when vision/support surface/task changes.**
8. **Treat position, movement and force sense as related but distinct constructs.**
9. **Do not interpret force sense as GTO activity alone.**
10. **Treat fatigue-related proprioceptive changes as task/joint specific.**
11. **Do not infer injury risk directly from one acute proprioception error without downstream evidence.**
12. **Detailed balance/reflex/learning implications remain NEU-04/09/10/11 and TST/TEC/PRO.**

## 20. Confidence / unresolved issues

### HIGH

- muscle spindles are major length/movement-related proprioceptors;
- GTOs provide force/tension-related feedback;
- proprioception is integrated centrally rather than read directly from one receptor;
- cutaneous signals contribute to body-state estimation;
- vision and vestibular signals are distinct from proprioception but interact with it;

### MODERATE-HIGH

- sensory weighting is reliability/task dependent;
- joint receptors make supplementary proprioceptive contributions beyond a simple end-range-only model;
- position-reproduction tasks can include substantial central memory/motor-command components;
- active fatigue effects are generally more consistent than passive-position effects in lower-extremity meta-analytic evidence.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- exact weighting of spindle/GTO/joint/cutaneous inputs for a specific joint/task;
- exact contribution of joint receptors in normal human exercise;
- relationship between a clinical proprioception test and complex dynamic exercise skill;
- exact fatigue threshold at which sensory accuracy changes;
- whether “proprioceptive training” produces receptor-specific sensory adaptation versus broader motor/balance learning.
