## 10. Current in vitro model continuum — 2026

Deng F, Wang Y, Castellano LA, Zhao C.  
**From Primary Cells to Organoids: Expanding the Biological Relevance of In Vitro Cellular Models.**  
Cells. 2026;15(19):1741.

Checked current PMC full text.

Model-specific trade-offs:

### Primary cells
Strength:
- closer to native tissue/donor biology.

Limitations:
- finite lifespan;
- donor variability;
- phenotype drift in culture;
- limited scalability.

### Immortalized cell lines
Strength:
- scalable;
- reproducible;
- experimentally tractable;
- easy genetic manipulation.

Limitations:
- immortalization/clonal selection can change genotype, phenotype, signaling and function.

### iPSC-derived systems
Strength:
- donor-specific;
- genome-editable;
- access to difficult human cell types.

Limitations:
- clone variability;
- reprogramming effects;
- maturation/differentiation variability.

### Organoids
Strength:
- 3D;
- multicellular;
- architecture and heterogeneity closer to tissue.

Limitations:
- batch/protocol variability;
- maturation limitations;
- incomplete vascular/immune/neural/systemic context.

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13664329/

# Part A — “Model” is a relation to a target system

## 1. Core project definition

A mechanistic model system is:

> an experimentally/computationally tractable system used to represent selected properties, processes or causal relations of a biological target system.

Examples:

- mouse skeletal muscle;
- cultured human myotubes;
- isolated tendon;
- organoid;
- engineered muscle tissue;
- mathematical muscle-force model.

### Project rule

A model is not valid in the abstract.

It is valid **for a specified question / target / context of use**.

---

# Part B — Internal mechanistic specificity vs external validity

## 2. Core tension

Reduction often increases control:

`whole organism → organ/tissue → primary cells → cell line → molecular construct`

Potential gains:

- isolation of mechanism;
- direct manipulation;
- measurement access;
- reproducibility.

Potential losses:

- whole-body interactions;
- neuroendocrine regulation;
- circulation;
- immune context;
- biomechanics;
- behavioral/environmental context.

### Project rule

> **Greater experimental control does not automatically mean greater human relevance.**

---

# Part C — Model validity dimensions

## 3. Classical dimensions

Animal-model literature commonly distinguishes:

### Face validity
Does model resemble target phenotype?

### Construct validity
Does model reproduce relevant underlying causal/pathophysiological structure?

### Predictive validity
Does model response predict response in the target system?

### Important limitation

These dimensions are useful but:

- definitions vary by field;
- no single score proves translation;
- predictive validity can only be judged against human/target evidence.

---

## 4. Project preferred approach

Instead of one label “good animal model,” record correspondence separately across:

1. mechanism;
2. phenotype;
3. exposure/intervention;
4. outcome;
5. anatomy/tissue;
6. dose;
7. timing;
8. population/condition.

This is especially aligned with the 2026 physical-exercise animal framework.

Animal systems allow:

- whole-organism physiology;
- invasive tissue sampling;
- repeated controlled interventions;
- genetic manipulation;
- organ cross-talk;
- controlled environment;
- blockade/rescue;
- disease-model induction.

## 6. Core mechanistic designs

Animal mechanism studies can use:

- randomized parallel groups;
- crossover where biologically feasible;
- within-animal/local perturbation;
- factorial designs;
- genetic × intervention interaction;
- dose-response;
- knockout/knockdown;
- activation/overexpression;
- pharmacological blockade;
- rescue.

### Project rule

“Animal study” identifies model system, not allocation design.

## 7. Species matters

Mechanisms can differ across:

- mice;
- rats;

- humans.

Relevant differences include:

- metabolism;
- muscle fiber distribution;
- cardiovascular regulation;
- immune system;
- lifespan;
- size/scaling;
- locomotor biomechanics;

Different strains can differ in:

- pharmacokinetics.

### Project rule

Do not generalize:

> “mouse evidence”

without species/strain/genetic-background metadata.

# Part H — Exercise-model correspondence

## 12. Exercise modality is not automatically homologous

Rodent:

- forced treadmill;
- voluntary wheel;
- swimming;
- ladder climbing;
- weighted wheel;
- electrical stimulation;

do not map one-to-one onto human:

- running;
- cycling;
- resistance training;
- HIIT.

---

## 13. Forced exercise can add non-exercise exposure

Potential co-exposures:

- shock/grid stimulation;
- handling;
- fear/stress;
- coercion;
- restraint.

### Project rule

If stress differs between conditions:

> “exercise effect” can include stress-system effects.

---

## 14. Voluntary wheel running changes dose control

Strength:

- lower coercive stress;
- natural voluntary behavior.

Limitation:

- self-selected:
  - volume;
  - intensity;
  - timing.

Thus:

> voluntary running can model spontaneous activity better than standardized exercise prescription.

# Part I — Translating exercise dose

## 15. Absolute workload is not comparable across species

Need biological anchors such as:

- relative aerobic capacity;
- lactate threshold;
- VO₂ response;
- relative load;
- contraction pattern;
- duty cycle;
- fatigue state.

### Project rule

Do not translate:

> rodent treadmill speed → human running speed

directly.

Translate the **physiological dose/construct**.

---

# Part J — Outcome construct must match across species

## 16. Performance surrogate problem

Rodent time-to-exhaustion can reflect:

- aerobic capacity;
- running economy;
- anaerobic contribution;
- motivation;
- stress;
- aversion;
- neuromuscular performance.

It is not identical to:

> directly measured VO₂max.

### Project rule

Cross-species inference requires construct correspondence, not merely similarly named endpoints.

---

# Part K — Genetic loss-of-function

## 17. Knockout logic

If gene/protein M is removed and phenotype Y changes:

> supports a causal role for M in that model/context.

If response to intervention A disappears:

> supports M-dependence of A→Y in the model.

---

## 18. Knockout ≠ guaranteed absence of function

Current evidence shows potential:

- alternative splicing;
- residual protein;
- translation re-initiation;
- transcriptional adaptation;
- paralog compensation.

### Project rule

Before interpreting a knockout:

1. verify genotype;
2. verify RNA effect where relevant;
3. verify protein/function loss;
4. check compensation/escape where plausible.

---

# Part L — Germline vs conditional knockout

## 19. Germline knockout

Gene absent throughout development.

Strength:

- strong system-wide loss-of-function.

Limitation:

- developmental compensation;
- altered anatomy/development;
- survival/selection;
- lifelong network adaptation.

---

## 20. Conditional/tissue-specific knockout

Can improve causal localization:

- tissue;
- cell type;
- developmental stage;
- inducible timing.

### Project rule

Conditional knockout can reduce developmental/global confounding but does not automatically eliminate:

- recombination incompleteness;
- off-target Cre expression;
- compensation;
- tissue cross-talk.

---

# Part M — Knockdown / partial inhibition

## 21. Partial reduction

Methods:

- RNA interference;
- antisense;
- pharmacological suppression;
- inducible expression reduction.

Mechanistically useful because:

- can model partial target suppression;
- may avoid lethality.

But:

> partial perturbation cannot be interpreted as complete loss-of-function.

Dose of target suppression is part of the mechanistic estimand.

---

# Part N — Gain-of-function / overexpression

## 22. Logic

Increase M and observe Y.

Can support:

> M can induce/modulate Y in this model.

But overexpression may be:

- supraphysiological;
- ectopic;
- temporally abnormal;
- stoichiometrically disruptive.

### Project rule

Gain-of-function supports capability, not necessarily normal physiological mediation.

---

# Part O — Rescue

## 23. Strong specificity logic

If:

1. M loss changes Y;
2. restoring M restores Y;

then specificity of M→Y claim increases.

Especially strong when rescue:

- restores normal-level function;
- is tissue-specific;
- uses orthogonal construct.

---

## 24. Rescue still has caveats

Need check:

- expression level;
- spatial pattern;
- timing;
- vector effects;
- incomplete restoration.

### Project rule

Rescue strengthens but does not automatically prove complete physiological equivalence.

---

# Part P — Epistasis / pathway ordering

## 25. Genetic interaction

Perturb M1 and M2 separately and jointly.

Can help infer:

- same pathway;
- parallel pathways;
- upstream/downstream ordering.

### Important caution

Phenotypic interaction does not uniquely identify molecular pathway order without additional biological assumptions.

---

# Part Q — Pharmacological perturbation in animal/cell systems

## 26. Advantages

Can be:

- reversible;
- dose controlled;
- temporally precise;
- closer to potential intervention.

---

## 27. Limitations

- target engagement;

> blocker effect ≠ mechanism proof unless engagement/selectivity are established.

# Part R — Primary cells

## 28. Strength

Closer to donor/tissue biology than immortalized lines.

Potentially preserve:

- receptor expression;
- metabolic phenotype;
- lineage-specific function.

---

## 29. Limitations

- donor variability;
- finite lifespan;
- culture adaptation;
- dedifferentiation;
- loss of mechanical/systemic environment.

### Project rule

“Primary human cells” improves species relevance but does not reproduce the intact human organism.

---

# Part S — Immortalized cell lines

## 30. Strength

- scalable;
- reproducible;
- cheap;
- gene-editable;
- high-throughput.

---

## 31. Major mechanistic risk

Immortalization/clonal selection can change:

- genotype;
- epigenome;
- metabolism;
- signaling;
- receptor expression.

### Project rule

Mechanism shown in one line is:

> mechanism in that model line

until reproduced in more physiologically relevant systems.

---

# Part T — Ex vivo tissues

## 32. Definition

Fresh isolated tissue/organ preparation studied outside organism.

Examples:

- isolated muscle;
- tendon;
- vascular ring;
- nerve-muscle preparation.

---

## 33. Strength

Preserves more:

- native architecture;
- extracellular matrix;
- cell-cell interactions;

than dissociated culture.

---

## 34. Limitation

Removes some or all:

- circulation;
- endocrine inputs;
- immune trafficking;
- neural/systemic feedback.

### Project rule

Ex vivo evidence can be highly informative for local tissue mechanism but cannot automatically establish whole-body response.

---

# Part U — Human ex vivo tissue

## 35. Translation advantage

Direct human tissue can bypass species differences for local tissue biology.

But donor tissue can differ by:

- disease;
- age;
- sex;
- medication;
- inactivity;
- surgery;
- sampling site.

### Project rule

Human origin ≠ representative healthy-training physiology automatically.

---

# Part V — Organoids

## 36. Core strength

3D self-organizing cellular systems can model:

- tissue architecture;
- multiple cell types;
- development;
- patient-specific biology;
- disease mechanisms.

---

## 37. Current limitations

Current 2026 reviews highlight:

- incomplete maturation;
- batch/protocol variability;
- incomplete vascularization;
- incomplete immune/innervation;
- missing whole-body cross-talk.

### Project rule

Organoid complexity improves some dimensions of fidelity while remaining reductionist.

---

# Part W — Assembloids / microphysiological systems

## 38. Purpose

Combine:

- multiple cell/tissue types;
- spatial organization;
- flow/mechanical cues;
- organ cross-talk.

Can increase human relevance for selected mechanisms.

---

## 39. Context-of-use principle

Current MPS guidance emphasizes defining:

> what decision/question the system must support.

Examples:

- disease phenotype;
- mechanism;
- toxicity;
- drug ranking;
- PK;
- patient stratification.

### Project rule

Model complexity should be justified by **fit-for-purpose performance**, not visual sophistication.

# Part Y — Mechanical environment in exercise biology

## 41. Critical relevance

Muscle/tendon/bone mechanobiology depends on:

- load magnitude;
- strain;
- frequency;
- contraction;
- substrate stiffness;
- 3D geometry.

Standard static 2D culture can distort mechanotransduction.

### Project rule

For exercise mechanism claims, ask whether the in vitro model reproduces the **relevant mechanical input**, not merely the cell type.

---

# Part Z — Dose translation

## 42. Concentration ≠ in vivo exposure automatically

In vitro drug/nutrient concentration may differ from human:

- plasma concentration;
- free fraction;
- tissue concentration;
- exposure duration;
- metabolic conversion.

### Project rule

Do not call an in vitro concentration “physiological” without exposure justification.

---

# Part AA — Timescale translation

## 43. Acute cellular perturbation vs chronic adaptation

Minutes-hours signaling experiment:

> acute cellular mechanism.

Months of human training:

> repeated integrated adaptation.

Need bridge through:

- repeated exposure;
- persistence;
- tissue remodeling;
- systemic integration.

### Project rule

Acute mechanistic sufficiency in cells does not establish chronic training mechanism.

---

# Part AB — Computational mechanistic models

## 44. Types

Examples:

- ordinary differential-equation pathway models;
- musculoskeletal/biomechanical models;
- cardiovascular models;
- metabolic flux models;
- finite-element tissue models;
- multiscale models.

---

## 45. Mechanistic simulation differs from statistical prediction

Mechanistic model encodes assumed governing relations/processes.

Statistical/ML model can predict well without representing causal mechanism.

### Project rule

Good predictive accuracy of an ML model is not the same as mechanistic simulation validity.

---

# Part AC — Verification vs validation

## 46. Code verification

Question:

> was the mathematical model correctly implemented in software?

---

## 47. Calculation verification

Question:

> is numerical solution sufficiently accurate/stable for this use?

---

## 48. Calibration

Question:

> were uncertain parameters fit to data?

Calibration data are not independent validation evidence for those same outputs.

---

## 49. Validation

Question:

> does model reproduce relevant real-world/experimental behavior in data not merely used to calibrate it?

### Project rule

“Model calibrated to experiment” ≠ “model validated”.

---

# Part AD — Computational model context of use

## 50. Credibility is use-specific

FDA current framework explicitly requires credibility evidence proportionate to:

- context of use;
- decision consequence/risk.

A model can be credible for:

> ranking relative trends

but not for:

> exact patient-specific absolute predictions.

### Project rule

Do not ask:

> “Is this simulation validated?”

without:

> “validated for what question/decision/range?”

---

# Part AE — Uncertainty and sensitivity

## 51. Computational mechanism claim depends on inputs/parameters

Need examine:

- parameter uncertainty;
- structural assumptions;
- initial conditions;
- boundary conditions;
- numerical uncertainty.

### Project rule

A simulated mechanism that appears only under one arbitrary parameter set is weaker than a robust phenomenon across plausible parameter ranges.

---

# Part AF — Emergent behavior

## 52. Useful credibility evidence

A mechanistic model reproducing an independently known phenomenon that was not explicitly fitted can strengthen plausibility.

But:

> reproducing one emergent pattern does not prove all encoded causal relations are true.

---

# Part AG — Model calibration circularity

## 53. Circularity risk

If model parameters are chosen so output Y matches experiment E, then showing model matches E is:

> calibration fit,

not independent confirmation.

### Project rule

Strong mechanistic validation benefits from:

- new conditions;
- perturbation predictions;
- out-of-sample behaviors;
- experimentally testable predictions.

# Part AH — Translational chain

## 54. Canonical chain

`molecule/cell → tissue → organ → animal → human physiology → human training/performance`

Evidence can move:

- forward;
- backward;
- iteratively.

### Project rule

Each transition is a separate extrapolation problem.

Do not leap:

> cell finding → training prescription

without intermediate support.

4. anatomy;

16. environmental context.

# Part AJ — Humanized models

## 55. Value

Examples:

- human cells in animal host;
- humanized immune systems;
- patient-derived xenografts;
- human iPSC models.

Can reduce selected species mismatch.

---

## 56. Limitation

Humanized component still exists inside:

- nonhuman physiology;
- artificial microenvironment;
- incomplete immune/endocrine context.

### Project rule

“Humanized” ≠ human-equivalent.

---

# Part AK — Convergent evidence

## 57. Stronger mechanism inference

Confidence increases when different systems converge:

- human perturbation;
- human observational/omics;
- animal knockout;
- primary human cells;
- organoid;
- computational model.

Especially when biases differ.

---

## 58. Exact replication vs conceptual convergence

### Exact replication

Same manipulation/system repeated.

Supports reproducibility.

### Conceptual convergence

Different systems/manipulations support same causal relation.

Supports broader mechanism plausibility.

### Project rule

Both matter, but they answer different questions.

---

# Part AL — Discordant evidence

## 59. Animal positive, human negative

Possible reasons:

- species biology differs;
- human dose inadequate;
- model phenotype wrong;
- endpoint mismatch;
- disease stage differs;
- human measurement insensitive.

### Project rule

Do not automatically choose the “more mechanistic” study.

Investigate model-target mismatch.

---

## 60. Cell positive, animal negative

Possible:

- systemic compensation;
- bioavailability;
- cell model artifact;
- pathway redundant in vivo.

This is often informative about the limits of reductionist sufficiency.

---

# Part AM — “Necessity” in model systems

## 61. Model-specific necessity

Knockout/blockade can support:

> M is required for Y in model system S under condition C.

### Project rule

Do not silently translate this into:

> M is necessary for human Y.

Human necessity requires human-relevant evidence or a very strong cross-system bridge.

---

# Part AN — “Sufficiency” in model systems

## 62. Model-specific sufficiency

Overexpression/activation can show:

> M is capable of inducing Y in system S under protocol P.

But:

- overexpression can be supraphysiological;
- system may lack competing pathways;
- target tissue/context may differ.

### Project rule

Sufficiency is system- and protocol-specific.

---

# Part AO — Rescue and orthogonal evidence

## 63. Strong mechanistic package

Especially persuasive within a model:

1. loss-of-function changes phenotype;
2. independent pharmacological perturbation agrees;
3. rescue restores phenotype;
4. pathway downstream readout changes;
5. effect reproduced in another model/human system.

### Project rule

No single element is a universal proof; convergence matters.

# Part AP — Exercise-specific example: rodent resistance training

## 64. Model choices

Potential models:

- ladder climbing;
- weighted wheel;
- squat-like apparatus;
- electrical stimulation;
- synergist ablation/overload.

These do **not** model the same biological exposure.

## 65. Synergist ablation overload

Can produce robust hypertrophy and enable mechanism study.

But the stimulus may be:

- extreme;
- continuous;
- surgically induced;
- biomechanically unlike voluntary human resistance training.

### Project rule

Mechanistic findings can be valuable for muscle growth biology while direct training-program translation remains limited.

# Part AQ — Exercise-specific example: electrical stimulation

## 66. Strength

Allows controlled contractions:

- intensity;
- frequency;
- duration.

Useful for local muscle mechanism.

---

## 67. Limitation

Bypasses or alters:

- voluntary motor recruitment;
- central drive;
- behavior;
- coordination;
- motivation.

### Project rule

It models contraction biology more directly than whole voluntary resistance training.

# Part AR — Exercise-specific example: treadmill rodent model

## 68. Translational value

Can standardize:

- speed;
- duration;
- incline;
- physiological intensity.

Current work increasingly uses human-like physiological anchors such as:

- lactate;
- oxygen uptake.

## 69. Limitations

May include:

- forced-running stress;
- different gait/biomechanics;
- species-specific thermoregulation;
- different motivation.

### Project rule

Translate the physiological construct, not the apparatus label.

# Part AS — Exercise-specific example: cultured myotubes

## 70. Candidate use

Can test:

- signaling;
- substrate metabolism;
- gene regulation;
- contraction-related molecular responses.

---

## 71. Limitation

Often missing:

- intact innervation;
- circulation;
- immune cells;
- tendon/bone interaction;
- whole-body endocrine effects.

### Project rule

Cultured myotube evidence can strongly inform cell-autonomous mechanism, but not full training adaptation by itself.

# Part AT — Exercise-specific example: ex vivo muscle

## 72. Value

Can isolate:

- contractile properties;
- substrate utilization;
- local signaling.

Preserves more architecture than dissociated cells.

---

## 73. Limitation

No intact whole-body regulation.

Thus:

> local mechanism ≠ whole-organism adaptation.

# Practical rules produced by PASS-06C5

1. A biological model is valid only relative to a specified target question/context of use.
2. Greater experimental control can increase internal mechanistic specificity while reducing external human relevance.
3. Animal studies are model-system labels layered on ordinary experimental designs, not one atomic design family.
4. Species, strain, sex, age and environment are mechanistically relevant model attributes.
5. NIH continues to require consideration of sex as a biological variable in vertebrate animal research.
6. Exercise modality/intensity/duration must be translated through physiological constructs rather than apparatus labels.
7. Forced treadmill, voluntary wheel, swimming and rodent resistance paradigms model different exposures.
8. Forced exercise can introduce stress/coercion as co-exposures.
9. Voluntary wheel running improves behavioral naturalism but reduces dose standardization.
10. Rodent time-to-exhaustion is not automatically equivalent to VO₂max.
11. Outcome-construct correspondence is required for animal-to-human translation.
12. Knockout evidence supports gene/pathway dependence only if functional loss is actually verified.
13. CRISPR knockout can escape through alternative splicing, residual protein or genetic compensation.
14. Germline knockout is vulnerable to developmental compensation and lifelong network adaptation.
15. Conditional/tissue-specific/inducible knockout can improve localization/timing but does not remove all compensation/off-target issues.
16. Partial knockdown estimates a partial perturbation, not complete loss-of-function.
17. Overexpression/gain-of-function supports inducing capability within the model, not necessarily physiological mediation.
18. Rescue strengthens specificity but depends on restoration level, location and timing.
19. Genetic epistasis can help pathway ordering but phenotype interactions alone rarely identify a unique causal network.
20. Pharmacological perturbation in animal/cell models requires target engagement, exposure and selectivity just as in human mechanistic studies.
21. Primary cells improve native/donor relevance but lose systemic context and can change in culture.
22. Immortalized cell lines improve scalability/control but may have altered genotype, signaling and metabolism.
23. Human-cell origin does not make an in vitro system equivalent to the intact human organism.
24. Ex vivo tissue preserves local architecture but removes much systemic regulation.
25. Organoids reproduce more architecture/cell diversity than simple cell culture but remain reductionist.
26. Organoid limitations include maturation, batch variability, vascular/immune/neural/systemic incompleteness.
27. MPS/organ-on-chip validity must be tied to predefined context of use.
28. More complex model ≠ automatically better model; fit-for-purpose validity matters.
29. GIVIMP-style quality control applies to the full in vitro chain from test system/reagents/SOPs through performance/reporting.
30. Exercise mechanobiology requires attention to substrate/load/strain geometry; static 2D culture may distort mechanical signaling.
31. In vitro concentration should not be called physiologic without exposure/tissue justification.
32. Acute cell signaling cannot automatically explain months-long human training adaptation.
33. Mechanistic computational models differ from purely statistical/ML prediction models.
34. Code verification asks whether software implements equations correctly; it is not biological validation.
35. Calibration uses data to tune parameters and is not independent validation.
36. Computational validation must be tied to context of use.
37. Model credibility depends on verification, validation and uncertainty appropriate to intended decision.
38. Simulation matching calibration data is not independent mechanism confirmation.
39. Out-of-sample perturbation predictions provide stronger computational validation than calibration fit alone.
40. Every step cell→tissue→animal→human physiology→training is a separate extrapolation problem.
41. Humanized models reduce selected species gaps but remain embedded in artificial/nonhuman context.
42. Model-specific necessity is not automatically human necessity.
43. Model-specific sufficiency is not automatically human sufficiency.
44. Convergent evidence across systems with different biases strengthens mechanism inference.
45. Exact replication and conceptual cross-system convergence answer different questions.
46. Discordance between cell/animal/human evidence should trigger model-target mismatch analysis, not automatic preference for the “more mechanistic” model.
47. Synergist-ablation overload can reveal muscle-growth mechanisms but poorly resembles ordinary voluntary human resistance training exposure.
48. Electrical stimulation is informative for contraction biology but bypasses substantial voluntary neural/behavioral components.
49. Cultured myotubes are useful for cell-autonomous mechanism but cannot establish whole-body training mechanism alone.
50. Nonhuman/translational mechanistic evidence should always record the intended **human extrapolation claim** explicitly.

---

# Evidence limitations

1. ARRIVE and PREPARE improve reporting/planning but do not by themselves establish translational validity.
2. Classical face/construct/predictive validity terminology is useful but definitions vary across fields.
3. Predictive validity can only be known after comparison with target-system evidence.
4. Animal exercise paradigms are heterogeneous and not standardized universally.
5. Rodent exercise stress, thermoregulation, gait and motivation differ from humans.
6. Genetic perturbations can cause compensation, developmental adaptation or off-target effects.
7. In vitro systems trade organism-level complexity for control; no single model maximizes both.
8. Organoid/MPS methods are rapidly evolving, so model maturity and standards are context-specific.
9. FDA computational credibility guidance is device-regulatory guidance; its verification/validation principles are used here as a strong general framework, not a universal legal standard for exercise models.
10. Computational models can be non-identifiable: different parameter sets/mechanisms may reproduce the same output.
11. Human translation requires independent human evidence whenever practical claims are made.
12. Current FDA moves toward NAMs improve availability of human-relevant systems but do not create an automatic evidence hierarchy where all non-animal models outrank animal experiments.
13. Exercise-specific translation can be particularly fragile when model stimulus is surgically induced, electrically imposed or behaviorally unlike human training.
