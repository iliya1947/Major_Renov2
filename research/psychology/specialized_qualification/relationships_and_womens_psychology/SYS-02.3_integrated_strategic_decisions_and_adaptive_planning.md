## 3. Integrated decision model
Define decision-maker, actual choice, time horizon, feasible actions, fundamental goals, intermediate outcomes, constraints, state uncertainty, preference uncertainty, consequences and available evidence. Other persons' independent decisions are not owned action variables. Hard constraints and actual consent precede compensatory scoring. Respect a legitimate refusal; do not optimize ways to circumvent it.

Compare options in a consequence matrix by ultimate and intermediate goals, immediate and delayed effects, alternative costs, reversibility and possible side effects. Distinguish sunk cost from future consequences of past expenditure. Distinguish desired result from indicator, predicted action from causal mechanism, and welfare from satisfaction with the choice. Elicit actual priorities rather than impose weights. If a quantitative weighting method is appropriate, state scale, tradeoffs and assumptions, check rank sensitivity and permit additional relevant attributes. Preference clarity, confidence, decisional conflict, values alignment, regret and final outcomes remain distinct; do not use a single composite as proof of decision quality. A decision aid is a support tool, not an authority or a guarantee of improved outcomes.

Where credible probabilities and utilities exist, expected utility and information-value comparisons are conditional on those inputs. Where probabilities are not defensible, compare scenario-specific consequences, hard acceptability conditions and sensitivity to risk criterion; do not use scenario frequencies as probabilities. Retain nondominated options when priorities are unresolved. Do not automatically choose maximin, minimax regret or a preferred strategy based on speculative consequences.

Sequential strategy is a contingent policy of feasible actions under observable conditions, not a fixed narrative of another person's reactions. Consider act now, feasible reversible or provisional action, wait, decline, stop or revise. Account for time, opportunity and acquisition costs and changing feasibility. The value of additional information depends on whether it can improve the choice; intrinsic curiosity or emotional benefit may be separate objectives. One-step information value need not capture complementary later information. Define review triggers and time horizon from actual decision costs and constraints, not invented universal deadlines. Avoid indefinite search, sunk-cost escalation and moving the criteria after an outcome. Preserve uncertainty where new evidence is inaccessible without violating autonomy or privacy.

## 4. Executed synthetic sequential check
Four equally likely independent pairs of binary equipment settings (00,01,10,11). A final binary choice earns one utility unit if it equals parity (exclusive OR) of the two settings, zero otherwise. No private material or empirical probabilities. JavaScript enumerated all four states and maximized conditional expected payoff. No information: 0.5. Observe first bit only: 0.5. Observe both: 1. Each optional observation costs .1. A one-step terminal-payoff comparison gives first-observation net change -.1; a planned two-observation policy yields net payoff 0.8, improvement 0.30000000000000004 over immediate choice. Because information is complementary, a myopic one-step stopping criterion can fail. This is a constructed counterexample, not an argument for acquiring more real-world information indiscriminately. With changing costs, availability or dependencies the result can reverse. It complements previous SYS-02.1 multiobjective and SYS-02.2 EVSI/regret arithmetic; not an external research replication.

## 5. Reusable decision protocol
1. Specify the actual decision, feasible alternatives including no change, goals and horizon. Preserve stated values and hard constraints separately from inferred preferences.
2. Establish known facts, uncertainty and competing causal/dynamic models. Do not promote a coherent explanation into an identified mechanism.
3. Compare all materially relevant consequences, tradeoffs, opportunity costs, intermediate effects and feasibility. Remove a dominated option only for the defined attributes and constraints.
4. Test robustness to uncertain weights, scenarios, costs and decision rules. When rankings are conditional, show the conditions rather than choosing arbitrary inputs.
5. Determine whether legitimately obtainable information could change an action; compare immediate choice, feasible interim action, waiting and stopping. Include complementary future information only when realistically available and worth its costs.
6. Record contingent actions and observable revision criteria prospectively. Assess ex ante decision quality separately from ex post outcomes; use new evidence to revise, not to rationalize a favored conclusion.
7. Protect voluntary choice and privacy. No fabricated evidence, deceptive tests, pressure, surveillance or use of inferred vulnerabilities. Explicit safety constraints and actual harm do not require resolution of every causal uncertainty.
