E1. Jodouin et al. (2021), Discrepancy in Dyadic Sexual Desire Predicts Sexual Distress over Time in a Community Sample of Committed Couples: A Daily Diary and Longitudinal Study. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-021-01967-0 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34426897/ . Complete indexed author abstract inspected; direct PubMed open blocked. 229 same-sex/gender and mixed-sex/gender couples, 35-day diaries and 12-month survey. Absolute difference between partners' reported desire predicted subsequent sexual distress at both intervals in autoregressive models; reverse paths nonsignificant. No significant moderation by partner/participant gender, couple type or relative desire level. This is not a causal intervention, nor proof of equal subgroup effects. Absolute differences discard direction and common level; exact coefficients, attrition and subgroup precision not verified.

E2. Fischer et al. (2021 issue), Sexual Satisfaction in Older Heterosexual Couples From Four European Countries: Exploring the Roles of Actual and Perceived Discrepancy in Sexual Interest. https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2020.1809615 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32852228/ . Full PubMed author abstract directly inspected. 677 couples aged 60–75 in Norway, Denmark, Belgium and Portugal. Actor-partner structural model: perceived mismatch negatively associated with both partners' satisfaction, while discrepancy computed from their separate reports was not a significant predictor after adjustment for emotional intimacy. Cross-sectional and outcome-specific; the computed mismatch is based on self-report, not objective ground truth. Distinguish this null result from E1's prospective association with distress in a different population. Adjustment does not establish direct effects; no causal or subgroup estimates independently checked.

E3. Degree and Direction of Sexual Desire Discrepancy are Linked to Sexual and Relationship Satisfaction in Couples Transitioning to Parenthood (2017). https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2017.1321732 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28524698/ . Indexed author abstract extracts; direct open blocked. Polynomial regression/response surface analysis distinguished mismatch and direction. Reported greater mismatch associated with lower sexual satisfaction for both partners but not overall relationship satisfaction. Sample count, estimates and detailed response surface not independently verified; supporting evidence only. Parenthood context restricts generalization.

E4. Park et al. (2023), Sexual Satisfaction Predicts Future Changes in Relationship Satisfaction and Sexual Frequency: New Insights From Within-Person Associations Over Time. https://doi.org/10.5964/ps.11869 . Directly inspected publisher abstract, overview, method excerpt, discussion and limitations. Four annual waves of the US CREATE study, 2,104 mixed-gender newlywed couples. Latent curve model with structured residuals separates stable individual differences and growth from within-person fluctuations. Sexual satisfaction predicted subsequent relationship satisfaction and frequency; reverse paths not significant in reported models. Gender differences were not supported. Partner paths largely disappeared when frequency was included. Authors note weak evidence for differences in magnitudes of some directional effects and limits of annual lag and newlywed sample. Temporal association remains observational; time-varying common causes and modeling assumptions remain. No universal causal direction, frequency prescription or individual coefficient. Detailed estimates, missingness and sensitivity analyses not independently inspected.

E5. McNulty et al. (2016), Longitudinal Associations Among Relationship Satisfaction, Sexual Satisfaction, and Frequency of Sex in Early Marriage. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25518817/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4472635/ . Indexed author abstract and result excerpts; direct PubMed open blocked. Two eight-wave studies spanning four to five years of 207 marriages reported bidirectional associations between sexual and marital satisfaction. Difference from E4 requires comparison of within/between separation, time lag, cohort and estimands; no assumption of contradiction at identical estimand. Exact effects not verified.

E6. Zhao et al. (2022), Evidence of a Bidirectional Association Between Daily Sexual and Relationship Satisfaction That Is Moderated by Daily Stress. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36066683/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9447985/ . Indexed author abstract and full-text excerpts; direct PMC blocked. Twelve-day diary, 287 married individuals (not 287 dyads). Daily sexual and marital satisfaction showed reciprocal next-day prediction; stronger associations under higher reported stress in several analyses. Observational time-lagged results, not a stress manipulation; exact moderator effects and sample overlap with other research not established. Compare daily with annual estimands rather than equating them. No recommendation to induce stress.

E7. Toward an Integrative Model of Intimacy, Sexual Satisfaction, and Relationship Satisfaction: A Prospective Study in Long-Term Couples (2022 online). https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2022.2129557 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36205506/ . Indexed author abstract, direct PubMed blocked. 145 long-term mixed-gender/sex couples; cross-lagged model reported bidirectional associations of intimacy behaviors with sexual and relationship satisfaction. Supporting evidence for candidate feedback paths, not identified causation. Exact lags, effects, within/between separation and attrition not independently checked.

E8. Byers (2005), Relationship satisfaction and sexual satisfaction: a longitudinal study of individuals in long-term relationships. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16123841/ . Indexed abstract extract, direct open blocked. Authors reported limited exploratory evidence for either directional account. Counterevidence to universal reciprocal or unidirectional dynamics; exact sample, time interval and estimates not verified. Do not read null as equivalence.

E9. Sexual Desire Discrepancy: A Position Statement of the European Society for Sexual Medicine (2020). https://academic.oup.com/smoa/article/8/2/121/6956574 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32192965/ . Directly inspected publisher conceptual/methodological sections and indexed abstract. Distinguishes perceived mismatch, separately reported desire, actual versus preferred frequency, duration and distress. Authors explicitly classify the statement as opinion-based owing to limited evidence. Use construct guidance, not an efficacy claim or current clinical treatment protocol. Frequency is not a direct measure of desire, and mismatch by itself does not establish individual disorder.

## 3. Integrated analytical model
Track separate actor-specific constructs and a mutually defined relationship state:
- target-specific romantic attraction, affection and general or partner-specific sexual desire;
- experienced closeness, disclosure, enacted and perceived responsiveness, including provider intent as a distinct unknown;
- sexual and broader relationship satisfaction, distress and actual behavior/frequency;
- desired bond type, dedication, feasible opportunities, investments, perceived alternatives and constraints;
- mutual choice, relationship status, maintenance, transitions and safety.

No scalar score represents all these constructs. Discrepancy D = |A-B| for two self-reported levels does not distinguish (high, high) from (low, low) when both match, or (high, low) from (low, high) when mismatch is equal. Record level, signed direction where meaningful, each person's perceived mismatch, distress, timing, desired activity and feasible opportunities separately. For quantitative research use appropriately validated measures and model both actors' levels, their interaction and nonlinear terms rather than assuming a difference score is sufficient. No individual scores inferred from casual observation.

Hypothesis map: actor/partner characteristics and context -> experiences and appraisals -> multiple feelings and intentions -> autonomous choices and actions -> further experiences. Feedback may occur between intimacy and satisfaction, between perceived responsiveness and desire, and between persistence and investments. Shared context affects both actors. This is a candidate process map, not an identified DAG. Reciprocal cross-lags can reflect common causes, measurement overlap or stable differences; even models separating within-person variation cannot identify causal direction without additional assumptions. Same-day, daily, annual and multi-year dynamics need not have the same signs or strengths.

## 4. Practical inference and revision procedure
1. Specify target: understanding the existing bond, predicting maintenance, estimating change or evaluating a possible mutually desired transition. Do not substitute contact frequency or a temporary response for the actual desired outcome.
2. Record the observable event, voluntary self-report, actor, recipient, context and time separately. Distinguish a person's desire to maintain one kind of relationship from a desire to transform it.
3. Generate competing explanations at the right level: affection without romantic intention; attraction with incompatible priorities or limited opportunity; autonomous interest versus felt obligation; relationship-specific versus generalized changes; mismatch between action and perception; persistence due to dedication, constraints or both.
4. Identify naturally occurring observations that would discriminate rather than merely fit each account. Compare voluntary statements and repeated behavior in relevant feasible contexts; specify predicted direction and time window before updating. If competing models predict the same observation, do not count it as diagnostic. Missing data leave the hypothesis open.
5. Account for interdependence and evidence dependence. Multiple observations within one exchange or multiple studies of a shared cohort are not independent replications. Distinguish future level from change, actor/partner effects, and reported preference from actual mutual choice.
6. Preserve explicit autonomy and relationship-specific consent. Neither closeness, support, prior intimacy, inferred attraction, mismatch nor relational history grants permission. No coercion, engineered jealousy, distress or constraints. No diagnosis or treatment advice from discrepancy alone.
7. Update only the supported proposition; revise predictions rather than retrofitting them. Use verbal uncertainty if individual probabilities are not calibrated. Reopen evidence when consequential transfer exceeds studied populations or constructs.

## 5. Internal transfer checks
Rubric fixed for this exercise: construct separation, competing explanation, distinguishing evidence, inference boundary. Critical errors: unsupported diagnosis/motive, ignoring contrary evidence, deterministic transfer, causal inflation, coercion or privacy breach. Self-authored neutral cases, not held-out or independent.

A. Two couples have equal absolute desire discrepancy, but different mean levels and different reported distress. Response: mismatch, common level and distress differ; expectations, timing and context are alternatives. Compare each actor's voluntary reports, perceived mismatch and repeated experiences; no diagnosis or common prediction from D alone.
B. A couple reports matched interest but one partner perceives mismatch. Response: computed and perceived discrepancy are distinct; definitions, timing, communication and reporting differences may contribute. Clarify constructs voluntarily; no inference of deception or whose report is objective truth.
C. Daily cross-lags are reciprocal, annual within-person paths are predominantly one-way. Response: distinguish time scale, estimand and sample; rapidly decaying effects and different modeling assumptions compete. Matched multiscale designs could discriminate; no universal causal arrow.
D. Two people maintain frequent friendly exchanges, one does not want a romantic bond. Response: maintenance, affection and desired relationship type differ; friendship, routine or other priorities may explain contact. Respect the stated preference; only freely expressed change and mutual agreement establish a transition. No hidden romantic motive inferred.
E. A partner reports reduced desire during a high-demand month while broad relationship satisfaction remains stable. Response: separate desire, opportunity, distress and satisfaction; fatigue and preference change may coexist. Compare ordinary later contexts and voluntary accounts; no deterministic or hormonal inference.
F. A model predicts future contact well but cannot predict changes in commitment. Response: outcome-specific accuracy, baseline persistence and change are different; shared routines or context can predict contact. Compare frozen commitment forecasts with independent later observations; no transfer of validated accuracy between endpoints.
