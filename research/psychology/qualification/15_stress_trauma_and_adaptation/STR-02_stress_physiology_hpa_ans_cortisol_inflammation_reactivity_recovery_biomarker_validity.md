# STR-02 — Stress physiology and temporal measurement: HPA axis, autonomic regulation, cortisol/catecholamines, inflammation, reactivity/recovery, and biomarker validity

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public stress-physiology evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

STR-01 established that stressor exposure, appraisal, response, recovery, coping, allostatic burden, and resilience are distinct. STR-02 addresses the next dependency:

**How should acute and repeated stress physiology be measured over time, and what biological conclusions are actually justified by HPA, autonomic, inflammatory, wearable, and composite biomarkers?**

High-risk errors to prevent:
- "cortisol = stress";
- "higher cortisol = more harmful stress";
- "lower cortisol response = resilience";
- "nonresponse = no stress";
- "HRV = sympathetic/parasympathetic balance";
- "low HRV = psychological stress";
- "salivary alpha-amylase = direct norepinephrine concentration";
- "EDA = emotional stress";
- "CRP = acute stress response";
- "one cytokine = inflammation";
- "stressor offset = start of recovery in every system";
- "return to prestressor baseline = optimal adaptation";
- "habituation = resilience";
- "blunting = resilience";
- "sensor classifier accuracy = validated stress measurement";
- "one biomarker phenotype = diagnosis".

## Core rule

**Stress physiology is multi-system and multi-timescale. HPA, autonomic, cardiovascular, immune, metabolic, and subjective responses have different latencies, peaks, recovery kinetics, confounds, and measurement requirements. A biomarker is evidence about a specific biological process, not a direct meter of psychological stress or adaptation.**

## 1. Stress physiology requires a temporal model

A valid stress-physiology design separates at least:
1. baseline/reference period;
2. anticipation;
3. stressor onset;
4. active stressor period;
5. system-specific peak;
6. early post-stressor transition;
7. recovery/resolution;
8. delayed downstream effects;
9. repeated-exposure adaptation where relevant.

Different systems can occupy different phases at the same clock time.

## 2. Objective stressor offset is not a universal recovery onset

A 2026 structured narrative review on post-stressor transition emphasizes that:
- cardiovascular activation may already be declining;
- cortisol may still be rising;
- threat-related cognition may persist;
- immune/transcriptional effects can emerge later.

Therefore the sentence "recovery began when the task ended" is usually too coarse.

The 2026 Post-Stressor Transition Model is an organizing hypothesis, **not a newly established biological system or validated clinical profile**.

## 3. Baseline is not a physiological zero

A prestressor measurement reflects:
- circadian phase;
- anticipatory stress;
- posture;
- respiration;
- recent movement;
- food intake;
- sleep;
- medication;
- laboratory context;
- prior stress.

Repeated prestressor observations are preferable when precise reactivity/recovery estimation is important.

A single baseline can be noisy or already stress-elevated.

## 4. Reactivity and recovery are different estimands

**Reactivity**: change following stressor exposure.
**Recovery**: post-peak/post-stressor trajectory toward an appropriate reference or stable state.

Two individuals can have:
- equal peak with different recovery;
- different peak with equal recovery;
- similar area under curve with different temporal shape.

Do not rank "stress sensitivity" from one summary statistic without defining the estimand.

## 5. Peak amplitude, peak timing, and area under the curve

Common summaries include:
- maximum change from baseline;
- peak value;
- time to peak;
- area under curve with respect to ground (AUCg);
- area under curve with respect to increase (AUCi);
- recovery slope;
- time to defined reference band.

These answer different questions.

AUCg is strongly influenced by baseline/total exposure.
AUCi emphasizes change relative to baseline and can behave differently when baseline varies.

No summary eliminates the need to inspect the trajectory.

## 6. HPA axis architecture

A simplified HPA sequence:
\`hypothalamic CRH/AVP -> pituitary ACTH -> adrenal cortisol -> distributed glucocorticoid/mineralocorticoid receptor effects and feedback\`.

This is a functional scaffold, not a single linear switch.

Cortisol production, distribution, binding, metabolism, receptor sensitivity, and feedback all shape peripheral measurements.

Peripheral cortisol alone cannot identify which upstream or receptor-level mechanism produced the trajectory.

## 7. Cortisol response is delayed relative to rapid autonomic change

Acute psychosocial stress can produce rapid cardiovascular/autonomic changes before salivary cortisol peaks.

Consequently:
- sampling only during the stressor can miss HPA peak response;
- sampling only after cortisol peak can miss rapid ANS reactivity;
- one synchronized "stress time point" is physiologically inappropriate.

Multi-rate sampling should follow system kinetics.

## 8. Cortisol matrices are not interchangeable

Cortisol can be assessed in:
- saliva;
- serum/plasma;
- urine;
- hair;
- sometimes other matrices.

These differ in:
- biological fraction represented;
- temporal integration;
- invasiveness;
- contamination/confounds;
- collection/storage requirements.

Salivary free cortisol is useful for repeated acute sampling.
Hair cortisol aims at longer integration windows but does not identify exact timing of exposures.

## 9. Salivary cortisol preanalytics matter

Interpretation can be affected by:
- exact sampling time;
- food/drink;
- smoking;
- vigorous exercise;
- oral blood contamination;
- collection device;
- storage;
- assay method;
- adherence to home-sampling schedule.

A nominal timestamp without adherence verification may misclassify circadian or CAR data.

## 10. Cortisol awakening response (CAR)

CAR is the increase in cortisol following awakening, typically studied using tightly timed samples during the first ~30–45 minutes.

It is shaped by:
- awakening time;
- circadian phase;
- anticipation of the coming day;
- sleep;
- light;
- sampling adherence.

CAR is **not** the same construct as cortisol reactivity to an acute laboratory stressor.

## 11. Diurnal cortisol profile

Common diurnal measures:
- awakening level;
- CAR;
- daytime slope;
- total output/AUC;
- bedtime level.

A 2024 meta-analysis of 12 studies found no significant overall associations of total diurnal output, slope, or CAR with TSST cortisol reactivity.

Lower total diurnal output related to better stress recovery in that evidence set; CAR remained unrelated to recovery.

Thus:
**diurnal HPA function ≠ laboratory stress reactivity**.

## 12. Cortisol nonresponse

Some participants show little measurable cortisol increase to a psychosocial stressor despite:
- cardiovascular activation;
- subjective distress;
- other biological responses.

Possible explanations include:
- true low HPA response;
- anticipation-elevated baseline;
- timing mismatch;
- insufficient stressor potency;
- prior exposure/habituation;
- biological state differences;
- measurement noise.

"Cortisol nonresponder" is a task/threshold classification, not a complete physiological phenotype.

## 13. Hyperreactivity and hyporeactivity have no universal adaptive sign

Large responses can reflect:
- effective mobilization;
- strong threat appraisal;
- metabolic/endocrine state;
- insufficient regulation.

Small responses can reflect:
- weak exposure;
- habituation;
- blunting;
- high baseline;
- illness/medication effects;
- altered receptor feedback.

Therefore:
**higher is not automatically worse; lower is not automatically better**.

## 14. 2026 depression meta-analysis: blunting is not resilience

A 2026 Psychological Bulletin meta-analysis of 126 studies found depression was associated with a small blunting of overall physiological response to active mental stress, driven mainly by:
- cardiac measures;
- hemodynamic measures.

No significant overall depression associations were found for:
- HRV reactivity;
- neuroendocrine reactivity.

This directly demonstrates why a reduced response cannot be equated with resilience.

## 15. Metabolic state changes cortisol reactivity

A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of 21 studies (N=1,216) found glucose administration was associated with a modest increase in cortisol responses to acute stress compared with fasting/non-glucose controls (d≈0.30).

Evidence for progesterone/estradiol effects was weaker and inconsistent.

Implication:
**cortisol reactivity is partly conditional on metabolic/endocrine state**.

## 16. HPA feedback and "recovery"

Post-peak cortisol decline reflects:
- reduced upstream drive;
- glucocorticoid feedback;
- distribution/metabolism;
- receptor-mediated processes;
- ongoing cognitive/context signals.

The 2026 post-stressor review argues against treating decline as passive washout or the action of one "stress-off center."

No universal cortisol-recovery threshold is clinically validated.

## 17. Mineralocorticoid/glucocorticoid receptor models are mechanistic hypotheses

MR/GR balance is often used to reason about:
- early appraisal/mobilization;
- later feedback/adaptation.

But peripheral cortisol cannot establish:
- receptor occupancy;
- receptor sensitivity;
- cell-specific signaling;
- neural locus.

Do not infer receptor dysfunction from one salivary trajectory.

## 18. Cortisol and DHEA/DHEA-S

DHEA/DHEA-S can be studied alongside cortisol because adrenal steroid pathways interact.

Ratios such as cortisol:DHEA are sometimes proposed as stress/adaptation indices.

However ratios can be difficult to interpret:
- numerator and denominator have separate determinants;
- age/sex/circadian effects differ;
- assay and timing matter.

No universal cortisol:DHEA resilience ratio is established.

## 19. Sympathetic-adrenomedullary and autonomic responses

Acute stress commonly changes:
- heart rate;
- blood pressure;
- cardiac contractility;
- vascular resistance;
- electrodermal activity;
- catecholamine release;
- salivary alpha-amylase.

These variables do not all index the same autonomic branch or central mechanism.

## 20. Heart rate is not a pure sympathetic measure

Heart rate reflects combined influences including:
- sympathetic activation;
- parasympathetic withdrawal/re-engagement;
- respiration;
- posture;
- physical movement;
- baroreflexes;
- thermoregulation;
- drugs and fitness.

An increased heart rate during a task does not uniquely identify sympathetic stress.

## 21. Blood pressure is not interchangeable with heart rate

Blood pressure depends on:
- cardiac output;
- vascular resistance;
- arterial properties;
- autonomic and hormonal regulation.

Two people can have different BP responses with similar HR responses.

Hemodynamic stress physiology should not be reduced to "pulse increased."

## 22. Heart rate variability (HRV)

HRV describes beat-to-beat variation in cardiac intervals.

Common metrics include:
- RMSSD;
- SDNN;
- high-frequency spectral power;
- other frequency/nonlinear indices.

Different metrics capture different properties and require different recording conditions.

## 23. HRV does not equal "sympathovagal balance"

2026 cardiovascular-methodology guidelines explicitly caution that HRV is not appropriate as a specific marker of:
- cardiac sympathetic outflow;
- simple sympathovagal balance.

Resting time-domain/HF measures can reflect vagal modulation under defined conditions, but dynamic stress contexts complicate interpretation.

The LF/HF ratio should not be treated as a validated universal autonomic balance meter.

## 24. HRV measurement conditions are part of the result

Important factors:
- ECG vs PPG;
- recording duration;
- posture;
- respiration rate/depth;
- speech;
- movement;
- time of day;
- recent exercise;
- food;
- caffeine/alcohol/nicotine;
- medication;
- disease state.

The 2026 guidelines emphasize standardized conditions and ≥5-minute recordings where suitable for resting HRV reliability.

## 25. Relative versus absolute HRV reliability

A measure can show acceptable rank-order reliability across people while having lower absolute reliability for within-person change.

Therefore:
- "reliable biomarker" at group ranking level;
does not automatically imply:
- precise detector of small individual day-to-day stress changes.

This distinction is especially important for interventions and wearables.

## 26. Vagal re-engagement is not identical to psychological recovery

Post-stressor vmHRV increase may reflect changing cardiac parasympathetic influence.

But:
- subjective threat can persist;
- cortisol can still rise;
- cognition can remain engaged.

Autonomic recovery is one response channel.

## 27. Catecholamines

Epinephrine and norepinephrine can provide information about rapid sympathetic/adrenomedullary activation.

Measurement issues include:
- short half-lives;
- sampling stress from venipuncture;
- source of release;
- posture/activity;
- timing;
- receptor sensitivity;
- plasma versus urinary integration.

There is no single gold-standard sympathetic biomarker that resolves all these problems.

## 28. Salivary alpha-amylase (sAA)

sAA is widely used as a noninvasive marker associated with sympathetic/noradrenergic activation.

Advantages:
- fast responsiveness;
- repeated saliva collection;
- useful temporal complement to slower cortisol.

Limitations:
- salivary gland biology;
- flow rate;
- oral conditions;
- food;
- assay method;
- imperfect correspondence with catecholamines.

Thus:
**sAA ≠ direct salivary norepinephrine concentration**.

## 29. sAA has its own diurnal pattern

sAA exhibits strong time-of-day variation.

Acute reactivity should therefore be analyzed against appropriate baseline/time controls.

A post-task increase cannot be compared naively across morning and afternoon protocols.

## 30. Electrodermal activity (EDA)

EDA reflects changes in skin conductance driven by sympathetic sudomotor activity.

It is sensitive to:
- orienting;
- cognitive load;
- thermal conditions;
- movement/electrode contact;
- novelty;
- emotional arousal.

EDA is useful for sympathetic arousal measurement but **not specific to psychological stress**.

## 31. Pupil size and other rapid markers

Pupil responses can reflect:
- arousal;
- cognitive effort;
- luminance;
- attention;
- pharmacology.

Respiratory rate, skin temperature, and peripheral vascular signals can also change during stress.

Each requires specific confound control.

No rapid peripheral signal uniquely identifies a stressor appraisal.

## 32. Acute stress and inflammation

Acute psychosocial stress can transiently alter circulating inflammatory mediators.

Common targets include:
- IL-6;
- TNF-α;
- IL-1-related pathways;
- CRP in some studies.

Inflammatory kinetics are generally slower and more heterogeneous than immediate ANS responses.

Sampling too early can miss delayed changes.

## 33. Circulating versus stimulated cytokine responses

Circulating cytokine concentrations and ex-vivo stimulated cytokine production are different outcomes.

The 2026 preregistered meta-analysis on distress and acute inflammatory reactivity found:
- no general association of overall continuous/categorical distress with circulating cytokine reactivity;
- small associations for depressive symptoms/depression with greater circulating inflammatory reactivity;
- limited and different evidence for stimulated cytokine responses.

Therefore:
**"inflammatory stress response" must specify the assay and response type**.

## 34. CRP is not just a chronic marker

High-sensitivity CRP is commonly used as a systemic inflammation marker and often interpreted over longer timescales.

A 2026 experimental study found a transient hsCRP increase after acute psychosocial stress in healthy adults.

This demonstrates that biomarker timescale labels are not absolute.

However an acute CRP change does not turn CRP into a specific stress marker.

## 35. Inflammation has many non-stress causes

Interpret cytokines/CRP with:
- infection;
- adiposity/BMI;
- recent exercise;
- sleep;
- smoking;
- medication;
- inflammatory disease;
- menstrual/endocrine factors;
- time of sampling.

In the 2026 meta-analysis, sex composition, age, and covariate use moderated some estimates.

## 36. Stress physiology across systems can be weakly coupled

The 2025 TSST meta-analysis of 171 studies and 8,452 adults found:
- physiological responses larger on average than psychological responses;
- ANS response effects larger than HPA effects;
- strong correlations within some ANS measures;
- weak/non-significant salivary cortisol–heart-rate association;
- generally weak/non-significant physiology–psychology associations.

Thus a multi-system profile can be coherent without every channel moving together.

## 37. Cross-system dissociation is not automatically measurement error

Possible reasons:
- different latency/peak;
- distinct appraisals/mechanisms;
- measurement noise;
- baseline differences;
- pharmacology/health state;
- habituation histories.

Do not discard a valid subjective report because cortisol is flat, or vice versa.

## 38. Repeated stress and habituation

Habituation means reduced response to repeated exposure under specified conditions.

A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of 47 repeated-TSST studies found:
- robust, relatively rapid HPA habituation in many studies;
- less uniform ANS habituation;
- less uniform immune habituation.

Therefore habituation is **system-specific**, not one general stress-learning coefficient.

## 39. Habituation is not the same as recovery

Recovery:
- within one stress episode after response.

Habituation:
- reduced response across repeated exposures.

A person can recover quickly after each episode but show little habituation across days, or vice versa.

## 40. Habituation is not automatically resilience

Reduced reactivity can reflect:
- learned safety/predictability;
- reduced novelty;
- fatigue;
- endocrine blunting;
- altered appraisal.

A 2026 study of 120 adults found self-reported resilience measures did not significantly relate to biological habituation or cumulative HPA output, despite measurable HPA/cardiovascular habituation.

Thus:
**habituation ≠ validated resilience biomarker**.

## 41. Sensitization

Repeated exposure can sometimes increase rather than decrease response.

A 2025 repeated social-stress study found system-dependent habituation and sensitization patterns across anxiety, IL-6, blood pressure, pulse, cortisol, and conflict behavior.

Repeated exposure history must therefore be measured rather than assuming monotonic habituation.

## 42. Cognitive processes can alter repeated-stress adaptation

A 2026 randomized study with repeated TSST exposure found:
- overall negative affect and cortisol habituation;
- experimentally induced rumination prevented some cortisol/heart-rate habituation;
- reappraisal effects differed by sex/marker and included prolonged sAA reactivity in one subgroup.

This supports causal modulation of repeated-stress physiology without implying a universal coping strategy effect.

## 43. Chronic dysregulation does not equal chronic hypercortisolism

Repeated/chronic stress can be associated, depending on context, with:
- higher integrated glucocorticoid exposure;
- blunted reactivity;
- altered CAR;
- flatter diurnal slope;
- altered ultradian dynamics;
- context-specific hypocortisolism/hypercortisolism.

Therefore one direction of cortisol change cannot define chronic stress.

## 44. Blunted reactivity needs competing explanations

Blunting can reflect:
- adaptation/habituation;
- depression-related physiological profile;
- chronic exposure;
- high baseline;
- medication/health effects;
- motivational disengagement;
- measurement timing.

Do not label low reactivity as "burnout physiology" or resilience without independent evidence.

## 45. Hair cortisol/cortisone

Hair glucocorticoids can provide longer-term integrated information compared with saliva/plasma.

Important caveats:
- hair treatment/coloring;
- wash frequency;
- growth rate;
- segment assumptions;
- local metabolism;
- ethnicity/hair characteristics;
- medication;
- assay methods.

Hair cortisol is not a calendar of specific stressful events.

## 46. Telomeres

Telomere length is studied in relation to cumulative biological aging and chronic stress exposure.

It is influenced by:
- age;
- genetics;
- cell composition;
- disease;
- lifestyle;
- assay variation.

Telomere length is not a specific or rapidly responsive stress biomarker.

## 47. DNA methylation and epigenetic markers

Stress-related epigenetic studies examine loci/pathways including glucocorticoid signaling.

A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of DNA methylation and cortisol stress response included 39 studies and concluded evidence was mixed/low certainty; an NR3C1 effect was supported only in infants in pooled analyses.

Thus epigenetic associations do not yet provide a validated adult acute-stress readout.

## 48. Multi-omics

Transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and epigenomics can reveal broader biological response profiles.

Challenges:
- high dimensionality;
- batch effects;
- multiple testing;
- small samples;
- tissue specificity;
- temporal alignment;
- replication/generalization.

Omics signatures are hypothesis-generating unless externally validated for the exact stress construct.

## 49. Allostatic load is not an acute-response biomarker

STR-01 established that allostatic-load indices combine multisystem markers to estimate cumulative burden.

STR-02 adds the temporal boundary:
- acute reactivity parameters;
- diurnal function;
- cumulative multisystem status

are different measurement classes.

An AL score cannot replace a trajectory study of acute stress recovery.

## 50. Wearables: measurement versus psychological inference

Wearables can measure or estimate:
- heart rate;
- pulse intervals;
- activity;
- skin conductance;
- temperature;
- sleep-related variables.

A device can measure a physiological signal accurately while its derived "stress score" has weak construct validity.

**Sensor validity ≠ stress-label validity**.

## 51. 2026 wearable reviews: dataset and validation limitations

A 2026 scoping review of college-student wearable stress studies included 134 studies and found heavy reuse of a small benchmark dataset: about 80% of studies using preexisting datasets relied on WESAD, which contains only 15 participants.

Other concerns:
- inconsistent demographics;
- limited sample-size justification;
- limited temporal modeling.

High algorithm performance on reused small datasets does not establish real-world stress detection.

## 52. Noncontact stress AI

A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of noncontact physiological stress recognition included 21 studies; 8 contributed binary-classification meta-analysis.

Pooled accuracy was about 81%, but heterogeneity was substantial and most studies were laboratory/controlled.

Label source affected performance.

Thus classification accuracy remains conditional on:
- ground truth;
- context;
- sensing method;
- validation split;
- population.

## 53. Remote PPG/rPPG limitations

2026 scoping review evidence indicates:
- heart-rate estimation can be robust under favorable conditions;
- HRV/PRV quality degrades with motion, illumination variation, and short windows;
- many studies lack high-quality ECG/contact-PPG agreement.

A camera-derived stress estimate must not be treated as equivalent to validated ECG-based autonomic measurement.

## 54. Wearable HRV should not be reverse-engineered into psychological diagnosis

Consumer-device HRV can support longitudinal lifestyle tracking.

But 2026 HRV guidelines caution against interpreting wearable HRV as:
- exact cardiac sympathetic activity;
- sympathovagal balance;
- diagnostic psychological stress level.

Physiological tracking can be useful without supporting mental-state decoding.

## 55. Physiological stress phenotype is not a diagnosis

A profile such as:
- low HRV;
- blunted cortisol;
- high IL-6;
- high perceived stress

can occur through many pathways.

A 2024 stress-phenotyping framework explicitly noted that no validated biomarker of "toxic stress physiology" currently exists.

Use biological profiles as research variables, not diagnostic shortcuts.

## 56. Minimum acute-stress physiology design

At minimum, report:
1. stressor and timing;
2. baseline timing;
3. anticipation period if relevant;
4. exact sample times;
5. expected latency for each biomarker;
6. posture/activity/respiration;
7. food/caffeine/nicotine/exercise restrictions;
8. medication/illness exclusions or covariates;
9. menstrual/hormonal/metabolic considerations where relevant;
10. assay/device method;
11. reactivity definition;
12. recovery definition;
13. repeated exposure history;
14. subjective appraisal/affect;
15. missing/nonresponse handling.

## 57. Multi-system temporal design

When combining systems, do not force all signals onto one peak time.

Example design logic:
- HR/BP/EDA: seconds-to-minutes;
- sAA/catecholamine-related measures: rapid repeated samples;
- cortisol: delayed repeated samples;
- cytokines: delayed samples;
- later recovery/follow-up: system-specific.

The exact schedule must follow the task and prior evidence.

## 58. Stress Physiology Inference Gate — 58 questions

### A. HPA target
1. Is cortisol the target or a broader HPA mechanism?
2. Which matrix is used?
3. Is baseline measured more than once?
4. Is exact clock/circadian time known?
5. Is awakening relevant?
6. Is acute reactivity distinguished from CAR/diurnal output?
7. Are food/exercise/sleep/medication/metabolic confounds controlled?
8. Is cortisol nonresponse defined transparently?
9. Are peak timing and recovery both measured?
10. Could high baseline mask response?

### B. Autonomic/cardiovascular
11. Is heart rate separated from blood pressure?
12. Is HRV metric named?
13. Is HRV being misread as sympathovagal balance?
14. Is respiration measured/controlled?
15. Are posture and movement standardized?
16. Is ECG distinguished from PPG?
17. Is recording duration adequate?
18. Are catecholamine measurements time-matched?
19. Is sAA treated as a surrogate rather than direct norepinephrine?
20. Is EDA interpreted as sympathetic arousal rather than stress-specific?

### C. Inflammation/slow biology
21. Which cytokine/CRP target is measured?
22. Is circulating concentration separated from stimulated production?
23. Is the sampling window long enough for the target kinetics?
24. Are infection/BMI/sleep/exercise/smoking confounds considered?
25. Is one inflammatory marker being treated as "inflammation"?
26. Is acute change separated from chronic low-grade inflammation?
27. Are epigenetic/telomere markers being misclassified as acute measures?
28. Are omics results externally replicated?

### D. Repeated/chronic physiology
29. Is within-episode recovery separated from across-session habituation?
30. Is habituation system-specific?
31. Is sensitization possible?
32. Is chronic stress defined by exposure rather than biomarker direction alone?
33. Is blunting being misread as resilience?
34. Is hyperreactivity being misread as pathology?
35. Is repeated-stressor history measured?
36. Are hair/diurnal markers interpreted at their actual integration window?

### E. Cross-system timing
37. Is objective stressor offset separated from physiological peak?
38. Are system-specific extrema measured?
39. Are channels allowed to diverge?
40. Is cross-system correlation expected for a theoretical reason?
41. Is recovery defined separately per channel?
42. Is return to original baseline actually the desired adaptive endpoint?
43. Are anticipatory and post-event cognition measured where relevant?
44. Is adaptation distinguished from physiological resolution?

### F. Wearables/classifiers
45. Does the device validly measure the raw physiological signal?
46. Does the derived algorithm validly measure the stress construct?
47. What is the ground-truth label?
48. Is validation participant-independent?
49. Is external/out-of-dataset validation available?
50. Is the dataset large/diverse enough?
51. Are motion/light/activity artifacts handled?
52. Is a consumer stress score being treated as clinical evidence?

### G. Inference limits
53. Is one biomarker being substituted for stress as a whole?
54. Could metabolic, circadian, health, or medication factors explain the signal?
55. Is a group-average biomarker relation being assigned to an individual?
56. Is association being converted into a causal stress mechanism?
57. Which competing physiological model remains?
58. What is the narrowest inference supported by timing, channel, and design?

## Canonical operating rule

**Define stressor and exact physiological target -> establish repeated baseline and timing -> match sampling rate to HPA/ANS/immune kinetics -> separate peak/reactivity/recovery -> model circadian/metabolic/respiratory/health confounds -> distinguish within-episode recovery from repeated-exposure habituation -> validate raw sensor before derived stress label -> preserve cross-system dissociation -> infer only the measured biological process.**

Never:
**biomarker = stress; less response = resilience; more response = damage; HRV = autonomic balance; offset = recovery; habituation = health; classifier = diagnosis.**
