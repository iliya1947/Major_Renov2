## 1. Qualification purpose / Question Card

A fitness trainer needs this model to avoid:

- calling resistance exercise “purely anaerobic”;
- assuming one universal phosphagen/glycolytic/oxidative percentage split;
- assuming %1RM alone determines metabolic cost;
- assuming repetition count alone determines set metabolism;
- assuming time under tension alone determines metabolic demand;
- assuming slower tempo is automatically more metabolically costly in every metric;
- assuming more blood lactate means proportionally more glycolytic ATP;
- assuming training to failure is required to create an effective metabolic stimulus;
- assuming shorter rest only changes “cardio” rather than the metabolic state of the next set;
- assuming PCr is fully restored after one fixed rest duration;
- assuming identical external volume load produces identical internal metabolic strain;
- treating supersets/circuits as metabolically equivalent to traditional sets;
- inferring hypertrophy causality from burn, lactate, swelling or “metabolic stress” alone.

Primary later decisions supported:

- understand why two sets with the same load or reps can have different energetic cost;
- understand why short rest changes later-set power and pathway contribution;
- distinguish load, reps, set duration, tempo, effort and density as interacting constraints;
- choose set configuration without mistaking acute metabolic strain for adaptation quality;
- interpret supersets/circuits as time-efficient but internally different structures;
- preserve volume/effort/mechanical-output trade-offs when programming.

Question type:

- **mechanism + acute-response mapping + chronic-adaptation boundary**.

Target context:

- healthy adults;
- resistance-trained and untrained adults where evidence exists;
- free-weight, machine and multi-joint/single-joint resistance exercise;
- traditional sets, cluster/rest-redistribution, supersets and circuits.

Closure criterion for PASS-01:

- all 13 exact Section 12 items covered at content/scope level;
- acute energetic effects separated from chronic adaptation;
- measurement limitations explicit;
- no content-level blocker preventing independent closure audit.

## 2. Core resistance-training energetic model

Resistance exercise is:

- intermittent;
- non-steady-state;
- mechanically heterogeneous;
- locally and systemically variable;
- composed of repeated transitions between work and recovery.

Within and across sets, ATP resynthesis can involve overlapping:

- phosphagen / PCr;
- glycolytic substrate-level phosphorylation;
- oxidative phosphorylation.

Canonical rule:

**resistance exercise is metabolically mixed; pathway contribution changes continuously with set structure and recovery state.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 3. Energy demand within a set — content PASS

The energetic demand of one resistance set depends on more than load.

Relevant variables include:

- external load;
- repetitions performed;
- repetition duration / velocity;
- set duration;
- concentric/eccentric/isometric time;
- active muscle mass;
- ROM;
- exercise mechanics;
- proximity to failure;
- starting PCr/metabolite state;
- prior sets;
- rest interval;
- blood-flow/perfusion constraints.

Canonical rule:

**the metabolic demand of a set is a state-dependent product of mechanical work, contraction pattern, duration, effort and recovery history—not one scalar prescription variable.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 4. Resistance-exercise energy expenditure is difficult to measure

2024 systematic scoping review PMID **38896201**:

- 166 resistance-exercise studies included;
- indirect calorimetry was the most common method;
- blood lactate, wearables and MET-based approaches were also used;
- reported energy expenditure varied widely;
- major challenge: substantial anaerobic contribution is not fully captured by indirect calorimetry alone.

Canonical rule:

**resistance-exercise energy cost is method conditioned; indirect calorimetry alone can underrepresent non-oxidative contribution.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

Measurement detail remains TST.

---

## 5. Integrated energy cost ≠ instantaneous pathway flux

During a set:

- PCr can provide rapid ATP buffering;
- glycolytic contribution can increase with sustained high ATP turnover;
- oxidative ATP production is active from exercise onset;
- relative contribution changes within seconds.

After the set:

- oxidative metabolism supports PCr resynthesis and homeostatic restoration.

Canonical rule:

**one post-set lactate or VO2 value cannot reconstruct instantaneous phosphagen/glycolytic/oxidative ATP flux across every repetition.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 6. Effect of repetition duration — content PASS

Repetition duration changes:

- total time under tension;
- number of repetitions possible at a load;
- mechanical power;
- total work/volume load;
- local oxygenation;
- cardiovascular/metabolic responses.

Current 2026 crossover study PMID **42319447**:

- 30 resistance-trained adults;
- three squat sets to failure;
- traditional tempo: 2-s eccentric + 1-s concentric;
- slow tempo: 4-s eccentric + 2-s concentric;
- traditional tempo produced greater total volume load and heart rate;
- slow tempo produced longer TUT, greater nVCO2 and greater accumulated muscle deoxygenation;
- no significant difference in measured total energy expenditure or RQ.

Canonical interpretation:

**slower repetitions can extend local metabolic/oxygenation stress while faster repetitions can permit more external work; “slower = universally higher metabolic cost” is false.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 7. Tempo depends on what is matched

PMID **35394146**:

- when time under tension was matched,
- faster repetition tempo produced greater total volume load, blood lactate, heart rate and RPE;
- slower tempo allowed greater external load in that protocol;
- muscle oxygenation did not differ.

Together with PMID **42319447**:

**tempo effects change depending on whether failure, TUT, external load, repetitions or work are matched.**

Canonical rule:

**repetition duration cannot be interpreted without the matching constraint.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual / MODERATE-HIGH empirical**.

---

## 8. Tempo ≠ hypertrophy mechanism

2025 tempo systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **42709703**:

- 14 studies;
- commonly studied faster vs slower repetition durations produced broadly similar hypertrophy;
- pairwise difference was trivial on average.

ACSM 2026 Position Stand PMID **41843416** likewise found time under tension did not consistently alter primary adaptations.

Canonical rule:

**acute tempo-related metabolic differences do not establish slower tempo as an inherently superior hypertrophy strategy.**

Confidence: **HIGH chronic-outcome boundary**.

---

## 9. Effect of set duration — content PASS

Longer continuous sets generally increase cumulative ATP turnover and can increase:

- PCr depletion;
- glycolytic flux;
- metabolite/ion disturbance;
- oxygen demand;
- local perfusion constraints;
- fatigue.

However set duration is not independent of:

- load;
- repetition duration;
- reps;
- proximity to failure.

Canonical rule:

**set duration is an important energetic variable but cannot be treated as an isolated dose.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 10. Longer set ≠ same metabolism at same external work

PMID **22808209**:

- six trained men;
- same initial leg-press load;
- same total 50 repetitions;
- same inter-set rest;
- 5×10 vs 10×5 configuration;
- 5×10 produced larger reductions in power, PCr and ATP;
- higher muscle lactate and IMP.

Canonical rule:

**the distribution of the same repetition total across sets can materially change intramuscular energetic disturbance.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**, with small male sample limitation.

---

## 11. Effect of load — content PASS

Load influences:

- force requirement;
- attainable repetition count;
- contraction velocity;
- set duration;
- recruitment;
- local blood-flow mechanics;
- total work.

But load alone does not determine metabolic disturbance.

2026 cross-load failure study PMID **41841121**:

- 10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 90% 1RM conditions;
- 30–90% 1RM taken to failure produced broadly similar local/whole-body metabolic perturbation in the studied protocol;
- 10% 1RM did not reach comparable failure/metabolic disturbance.

Canonical rule:

**%1RM is an important mechanical constraint, not a standalone metabolic dose.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**, pending exact population/protocol audit in PASS-02.

---

## 12. Load interacts with effort and repetition number

2026 randomized crossover study PMID **42458937**:

- 18 participants;
- squat and prone row;
- 65% vs 85% 1RM;
- failure, 1-RIR and 3-RIR;
- metabolic stress and fatigue increased closer to failure;
- 65% 1RM often elicited greater fatigue/metabolic stress than 85% 1RM;
- total repetitions differed by load/exercise/effort.

Canonical interpretation:

**a lighter load can be more metabolically demanding when it permits more repetitions and is taken sufficiently close to failure.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 13. Heavy load ≠ automatically greater total metabolic stress

The same high external load may produce:

- high instantaneous force demand;
- fewer repetitions;
- shorter set duration;
- lower total glycolytic/metabolite accumulation than a lighter, longer set.

Conversely, low/moderate load near failure may produce:

- longer duration;
- more repetitions;
- larger local metabolic disturbance.

Canonical rule:

**mechanical intensity and metabolic stress are related but non-equivalent dimensions.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 14. Effect of repetitions — content PASS

Increasing repetitions within a continuous set generally increases:

- set duration;
- cumulative work;
- PCr use;
- glycolytic contribution;
- metabolite accumulation;
- velocity/power loss;
- local fatigue.

PMID **22808209** directly supports larger PCr/ATP disruption and muscle lactate in 10-rep vs 5-rep set structures with matched total repetitions across the workout.

PMID **36812922** found protocols with more repetitions per set and shorter rests produced higher velocity loss, effort index and lactate.

Canonical rule:

**more repetitions per set usually increase continuous metabolic disturbance, but the effect depends on load, tempo, rest and effort.**

Confidence: **HIGH direction / MODERATE exact magnitude**.

---

## 15. Repetition count ≠ metabolic dose

Ten repetitions can differ metabolically depending on:

- 30% vs 80% 1RM;
- 1-RIR vs failure;
- 1-s vs 6-s repetition;
- 30-s vs 3-min prior rest;
- squat vs curl;
- first vs fifth set.

Canonical rule:

**rep count without load, tempo, effort and rest context is metabolically incomplete.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 16. Effect of proximity to failure — content PASS

Closer proximity to failure usually increases acute:

- repetitions completed;
- velocity loss;
- mechanical fatigue;
- perceived exertion;
- metabolic stress markers.

PMID **42458937** supports greater metabolic and neuromuscular fatigue closer to momentary failure.

Velocity-loss systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **36178597** similarly found higher blood lactate and RPE as velocity-loss thresholds increased, with exercise/load moderation.

Canonical rule:

**closer-to-failure sets generally produce greater acute metabolic disturbance, but the magnitude depends on load and exercise.**

Confidence: **HIGH direction**.

---

## 17. Failure ≠ required metabolic or chronic endpoint

2026 failure vs non-failure systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **42410632**:

- 20 studies;
- 556 participants;
- non-failure training was not inferior for hypertrophy, endurance or power;
- dynamic strength slightly favored non-failure training.

ACSM 2026 PMID **41843416**:

- training to momentary muscular fatigue did not consistently impact primary chronic training outcomes.

Canonical rule:

**momentary failure is not required to obtain resistance-training adaptations and must not be treated as a mandatory metabolic target.**

Confidence: **HIGH chronic-outcome boundary**.

---

## 18. Proximity to failure and hypertrophy boundary

2024 meta-regression PMID **38970765** suggests hypertrophy may improve as sets terminate closer to failure.

But:

- RIR values were estimated from study descriptions;
- model fit was modest;
- exact dose-response remains uncertain;
- this does not prove metabolic stress is the causal mediator.

Canonical rule:

**sufficiently close proximity to failure can matter for hypertrophy, but this does not establish lactate, H+, “burn” or other metabolic stress as the causal driver.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 19. Effect of rest interval — content PASS

Rest changes the starting state of the next set.

Longer rest generally allows more:

- PCr resynthesis;
- metabolite/ion restoration;
- mechanical performance recovery;
- repetitions / volume maintenance.

Shorter rest generally increases:

- session density;
- residual metabolic disturbance;
- lactate/RPE in many protocols;
- probability of reduced later-set performance.

Canonical rule:

**rest interval is part of the metabolic prescription because it determines the pre-next-set state.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 20. Rest interval ≠ fixed PCr percentage

PCr recovery is oxidative and often rapid, but BIO-02/08/10 established:

- no universal full-repletion time;
- recovery depends on depletion, pH, O2 availability, muscle and method.

Canonical rule:

**“X minutes rest = Y% PCr restored” is not a universal resistance-training law.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 21. Rest interval and external work

PMID **36812922** showed that shorter-rest/higher-density configurations can reduce the repetitions/volume actually completed under heavy-load conditions.

2024 hypertrophy rest meta-analysis PMID **39205815**:

- nine studies;
- central estimates modestly favored >60-s rest;
- no appreciable hypertrophy differences detected when resting >90 s;
- authors proposed volume-load preservation as a plausible mediator.

Canonical rule:

**short rest can increase metabolic strain while simultaneously compromising later-set work; those are separate consequences.**

Confidence: **HIGH acute direction / MODERATE chronic exact optimum**.

---

## 22. Repeated-set PCr recovery — content PASS

Between sets:

- oxidative ATP production supports PCr resynthesis;
- later sets may start with incomplete PCr restoration;
- exact recovery depends on the prior set's depletion/acidosis and rest conditions.

PMID **22808209** demonstrates that repeated-set structure materially alters PCr/ATP/lactate state.

Upstream 31P-MRS systematic review PMID **40189235** confirms heterogeneous PCr recovery kinetics.

Canonical rule:

**repeated resistance sets are performed from evolving phosphagen states; identical later-set prescriptions do not imply identical starting PCr.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual / MODERATE-HIGH direct RT evidence**.

---

## 23. PCr recovery and later-set performance are non-equivalent

More PCr recovery can support high-rate force/power.

But later performance also depends on:

- Pi/ions/pH;
- E–C coupling;
- neural factors;
- glycogen;
- mechanics;
- task strategy.

Canonical rule:

**PCr restoration contributes to repeated-set performance but does not certify complete recovery.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 24. Glycolytic contribution in moderate/high-rep sets — content PASS

Moderate/high-repetition continuous sets can create large glycolytic demand because:

- ATP demand remains high after initial PCr use;
- set duration is longer;
- local oxygen delivery can be constrained during contraction;
- glycogen-derived glycolysis supports ATP resynthesis.

Evidence:

- PMID **22808209**: 10-rep sets produced more muscle lactate and greater PCr/ATP disturbance than 5-rep sets in the studied protocol;
- PMID **36812922**: more reps/set + shorter rest increased lactate;
- PMID **42458937**: lower load / closer failure increased metabolic stress in several conditions.

Canonical rule:

**glycolytic contribution often becomes more prominent in longer moderate/high-rep sets, but no rep-range boundary defines a “glycolytic set.”**

Confidence: **HIGH direction / MODERATE exact partition**.

---

## 25. Blood lactate boundary

Blood lactate reflects:

- production;
- transport;
- oxidation;
- redistribution;
- sampling timing.

Project rule:

**blood lactate ≠ direct glycolytic ATP production and ≠ hypertrophic stimulus dose.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 26. Oxidative contribution to resistance exercise — content PASS

Oxidative ATP production is active:

- during the set;
- between sets;
- after the session.

Its relative role increases with:

- longer set duration;
- repeated sets;
- incomplete recovery;
- prolonged sessions;
- circuit-like density.

Canonical rule:

**resistance exercise is not “anaerobic only”; oxidative metabolism contributes both to ongoing work and especially recovery.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 27. Oxidative recovery between sets

Oxidative ATP production supports:

- PCr resynthesis;
- ATP-dependent ion homeostasis;
- continued substrate oxidation;
- other recovery processes.

Project rule:

**between-set rest is metabolically active, not an energetic zero.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 28. Longer sets / circuits can increase cardiorespiratory demand

High-density/circuit resistance exercise can increase:

- VO2;
- heart rate;
- blood lactate;
- total session energy cost.

PMID **33171830**:

- high-intensity resistance circuit vs traditional strength protocol;
- HRC had ~75% higher VO2, ~39% higher heart rate and ~66% higher energy cost in the studied amateur soccer players;
- blood lactate and EPOC were also higher.

Canonical rule:

**as local rests shrink and session density rises, resistance exercise can become increasingly cardiorespiratory/oxidative demanding.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 29. Training density — content PASS

Training density is the amount of work/exposure completed per unit time.

Density rises with combinations of:

- shorter rest;
- longer sets;
- more reps;
- more exercises;
- supersets/circuits;
- less transition time.

Higher density can increase:

- acute metabolic stress;
- cardiorespiratory demand;
- perceptual effort;
- velocity/power loss.

But it may reduce:

- repetitions per set;
- load maintained;
- total high-quality mechanical work.

Canonical rule:

**density is a trade-off variable, not a one-direction “more stimulus” variable.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 30. Equal volume load ≠ equal internal stress

PMID **36812922**:

- protocols designed around similar/equated volume-load structures produced different lactate, velocity loss and effort responses depending on load, reps/set and rest.

PMID **22808209**:

- same total repetitions with different set distributions produced different intramuscular energetic disturbance.

Canonical rule:

**volume load is a mechanical bookkeeping metric; it does not uniquely specify metabolic stress.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 31. Set configuration / rest redistribution

Cluster and rest-redistribution approaches break continuous work into shorter segments.

Current evidence supports that they can:

- preserve velocity/power better in some contexts;
- reduce lactate/metabolic disturbance;
- reduce perceived exertion;
- change total session duration.

PMID **37649783** in young women:

- 5×10 traditional vs 10×5 with shorter/frequent rest;
- traditional configuration produced larger lactate response and MVIC decrement.

PMID **41369549** in postmenopausal women:

- shorter set configurations reduced lactatemia and preserved mechanical output relative to longer configurations.

2026 rest-redistribution meta-analysis PMID **42032773** supports selected power/torque and RPE advantages but not uniform superiority across all outcomes.

Canonical rule:

**redistributing rest can reduce continuous metabolic disruption without necessarily reducing total work.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 32. Supersets — content PASS

2025 systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **39903375**:

- 19 studies;
- 313 participants;
- supersets produced similar total repetitions and volume load on average;
- shorter session duration / greater time efficiency;
- higher blood lactate;
- higher estimated energy cost;
- higher RPE;
- chronic strength, endurance and hypertrophy outcomes broadly similar on average;
- similar-biomechanical supersets could reduce volume load.

Canonical rule:

**supersets generally trade higher internal/metabolic load for time efficiency; their mechanical cost depends on superset type.**

Confidence: **HIGH for acute direction / MODERATE-HIGH chronic equivalence**.

---

## 33. Superset type matters

Distinguish:

- agonist–antagonist;
- alternate peripheral;
- same/similar biomechanical muscle-group supersets.

PMID **39903375**:

- agonist–antagonist supersets could preserve/increase repetitions;
- similar-biomechanical supersets reduced volume load relative to traditional sets.

Canonical rule:

**“superset” is not one physiological intervention class.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 34. Circuits — content PASS

Circuit-like structures reduce transition/rest time and can increase whole-body cardiorespiratory/metabolic demand.

But circuit effects depend on:

- exercise sequence;
- local muscle overlap;
- load;
- reps;
- work:rest;
- active muscle mass;
- exercise skill.

Canonical rule:

**circuit resistance training can become strongly cardiorespiratory/metabolic, but no universal “circuit metabolism” exists.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual / MODERATE-HIGH empirical**.

---

## 35. Superset/circuit metabolic cost ≠ superior adaptation

2025 superset meta-analysis PMID **39903375** found higher lactate/energy cost/RPE without superior average hypertrophy.

2024 RCT PMID **39072654** found whole-body multi-joint supersets roughly halved session duration but somewhat reduced strength gains for some pulling movements, while body composition changes were similar.

Canonical rule:

**greater acute metabolic demand from supersets/circuits does not prove superior hypertrophy or strength adaptation.**

Confidence: **HIGH boundary**.

---

## 36. Metabolic stress vs hypertrophy causality — content PASS

Resistance exercise can acutely produce:

- lactate accumulation;
- H+/pH disturbance;
- Pi accumulation;
- cell swelling;
- metabolite-rich “pump” sensations;
- hypoxia/deoxygenation.

These responses can correlate with hard work.

They do not by themselves prove a causal hypertrophy mechanism.

2025 review PMID **41276164** concludes that claims for acute hormonal responses, metabolic stress, cell swelling or pump as meaningful independent hypertrophy drivers are not supported by current scientific evidence.

Canonical rule:

**metabolic stress is an acute exercise state/response, not a validated standalone causal dose for hypertrophy.**

Confidence: **HIGH current synthesis**.

---

## 37. ACSM 2026 chronic-outcome boundary

ACSM Position Stand PMID **41843416** synthesized 137 systematic reviews (>30,000 participants).

Relevant BIO-12 boundary:

- momentary muscle fatigue did not consistently alter primary outcomes;
- set structure did not consistently alter primary outcomes;
- time under tension did not consistently alter primary outcomes;
- hypertrophy was more consistently related to weekly volume and loading exposure than to deliberately maximizing acute metabolic strain.

Canonical rule:

**acute metabolic intensity and chronic training efficacy are non-equivalent.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 38. Failure / burn / pump boundary

Reject:

- more burn = more hypertrophy;
- more lactate = more hypertrophy;
- failure = required hypertrophy signal;
- slow tempo = superior because it “creates more metabolic stress”;
- supersets = superior because lactate is higher.

Retain:

- hard sets can be effective;
- sufficiently close proximity to failure can matter, especially with lighter loads;
- mechanical loading, weekly exposure and recoverability remain central.

Canonical rule:

**metabolic responses can accompany effective hypertrophy training without being the established independent causal reason it works.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 39. Resistance set energy-system profile — trainer-level model

### Very short/heavy sets
Likely:
- high phosphagen reliance;
- meaningful oxidative contribution already present;
- relatively short time for large glycolytic accumulation.

### Moderate-length sets
Likely:
- phosphagen + substantial glycolytic contribution;
- increasing oxidative contribution;
- rising Pi/ion/H+ disturbance.

### Long/light sets near failure
Likely:
- prolonged glycolytic/oxidative contribution;
- high metabolite/perceptual strain;
- PCr repeatedly buffered/resynthesized during continuing contractions depending on contraction pattern.

Canonical rule:

**these are profiles, not percentage laws or fixed rep-range energy-system categories.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual / MODERATE exact partition**.

## 40. Same external prescription can produce different internal metabolism

Two athletes performing:

- same %1RM;
- same reps;
- same rest

can still differ due to:

- fibre composition;
- training status;
- actual RIR;
- repetition velocity;
- ROM/technique;
- muscle mass;
- glycogen/PCr state;
- oxidative capacity;
- temperature.

Canonical rule:

**external prescription is an input; internal metabolic dose is an individual response.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 41. Measurement boundaries

### VO2 / indirect calorimetry
Useful for oxidative component but incomplete for total resistance-exercise energy cost.

### Blood lactate
Systemic concentration; not direct glycolytic ATP flux.

### Muscle biopsy
Local biochemical state; not every fibre/muscle.

### 31P-MRS
Local PCr/Pi/pH with acquisition/model constraints.

### NIRS
Local oxygenation/hemodynamic proxy; not direct ATP flux.

### Velocity loss
Mechanical fatigue/performance proxy; not a direct metabolite measurement.

### RPE
Perceptual response; not a biochemical assay.

Canonical rule:

**no single common field measure directly quantifies total resistance-set bioenergetics.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 43. Practical trainer implications — non-prescriptive layer

1. Describe a resistance set with load + reps + tempo + effort + rest + exercise, not reps or %1RM alone.
2. Expect shorter rest to alter the next set's phosphagen/metabolite state.
3. Use longer continuous sets as a likely route to greater glycolytic/metabolite disturbance, not as an automatic hypertrophy upgrade.
4. Recognize lighter loads can become highly metabolically demanding near failure.
5. Recognize heavy low-rep sets can be mechanically severe without maximal lactate/metabolic accumulation.
6. Treat blood lactate as contextual, not as a target.
7. Use cluster/rest redistribution when preserving mechanical quality matters, without assuming it is chronically superior.
8. Use supersets for time efficiency with awareness of higher internal load and configuration-specific volume trade-offs.
9. Treat circuits as combined resistance + cardiorespiratory/metabolic exposure when density is high.
10. Do not maximize burn, pump or lactate as primary programming goals.
11. Separate acute fatigue/metabolic cost from chronic hypertrophy/strength efficacy.
12. Exact load/reps/rest/density decisions remain PRO.

## 46. Evidence-quality interpretation

### Stronger trainer-level evidence

- resistance exercise is metabolically mixed;
- set structure alters energetic disturbance;
- reps/set and short rest increase acute metabolic/fatigue responses in many protocols;
- proximity to failure increases acute metabolic stress;
- momentary failure is not required for hypertrophy/strength adaptation;
- rest changes later-set performance and metabolic starting state;
- supersets increase time efficiency and acute internal/metabolic load;
- higher acute metabolic stress does not prove greater hypertrophy;
- energy expenditure measurement in resistance exercise is method limited.

### Translation cautions

- many direct metabolic studies have small samples;
- several classic biopsy studies are male-only;
- blood lactate is indirect;
- 31P-MRS studies are muscle/protocol specific;
- velocity-loss evidence does not directly measure ATP pathway contribution;
- tempo studies differ in what they match;
- supersets differ by configuration;
- circuit studies differ strongly in exercise order/rest/muscle overlap;
- chronic adaptation evidence should outrank acute metabolite differences for hypertrophy/strength decisions.

## 47. Established content-level findings

### HIGH

1. Resistance exercise is metabolically mixed rather than purely anaerobic.
2. Energy-system contribution varies within and across sets.
3. Set metabolic demand depends on load, reps, duration, tempo, effort, exercise and recovery state.
4. Energy expenditure in resistance exercise is difficult to quantify because anaerobic energy is incompletely captured by indirect calorimetry.
5. More repetitions per continuous set generally increase metabolic disturbance.
6. Shorter rest changes later-set metabolic starting state and often increases acute metabolic strain.
7. PCr recovery between sets is oxidative and variable; no fixed rest→PCr rule is retained.
8. Moderate/high-rep sets can have substantial glycolytic contribution.
9. Oxidative metabolism contributes during resistance exercise and between sets.
10. Higher density can increase metabolic/cardiorespiratory strain.
11. Supersets generally increase time efficiency and acute internal/metabolic load.
12. Blood lactate is not direct glycolytic ATP flux.
13. Acute metabolic stress is not a validated independent causal hypertrophy dose.
14. Momentary failure is not required for hypertrophy/strength adaptation.

### MODERATE-HIGH

15. Slower tempo can increase TUT/local deoxygenation while not necessarily increasing total EE.
16. Faster tempo can permit more external work and higher lactate/HR when TUT is matched.
17. Lighter/moderate loads near failure can produce metabolic stress comparable to heavier failure sets.
18. Rest redistribution/shorter set configurations can reduce metabolic disturbance and preserve mechanical output.
19. Superset chronic hypertrophy outcomes are broadly similar to traditional sets on average.
20. High-density circuit resistance work can substantially increase VO2/energy cost.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT DEPENDENT

21. Exact phosphagen/glycolytic/oxidative percentage in one set.
22. Exact PCr fraction restored after a given rest.
23. Exact metabolic consequence of a specific repetition tempo.
24. Exact metabolic difference between loads when effort/work are differently matched.
25. Exact RIR at which metabolic stress rises meaningfully.
26. Exact density threshold where cardiorespiratory limitation dominates.
27. Exact metabolic cost of one superset configuration.
28. Exact transfer of small lab-study metabolic responses to an individual's program.

### NOT RETAINED AS UNIVERSAL RULE

30. Heavy sets are always more metabolically demanding than light sets.

32. Slow tempo is always more metabolic.
