## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a bounded endocrine/immune model sufficient for later programming, recovery, nutrition, testing and safety decisions without:

- one hormone concentration treated as the state of the whole endocrine system;
- acute catecholamine/cortisol/testosterone/GH responses treated as chronic adaptation;
- acute systemic “anabolic hormone” spikes treated as a causal hypertrophy mechanism;
- circulating IGF-1 treated as equivalent to local muscle IGF signaling;
- insulin treated only as an anabolic hormone rather than a regulator of glucose/substrate homeostasis;
- glucagon treated independently of hepatic glucose production and counter-regulation;
- chronic training assumed to shift all resting hormones in one universally favorable direction;
- cortisol or testosterone:cortisol ratio used as a stand-alone readiness/overtraining score;
- one acute cytokine rise interpreted as harmful systemic inflammation;
- muscle-derived IL-6 treated as biologically identical to chronically elevated inflammatory IL-6;
- postexercise lymphopenia automatically labeled immunosuppression;
- habitual exercise described as simply “boosting immunity”;
- high training load alone equated with infection or overtraining;
- sleep loss, low energy availability and psychosocial stress ignored as systemic modifiers;
- infection/pathology diagnosed from training biomarkers.

### Question type

Integrative endocrine and immune physiology of acute exercise, repeated training and recovery context.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 18 exact Section 17–18 items must be covered at content level; acute-vs-chronic and systemic-vs-local boundaries must be explicit; metabolic hormones must be separated from hypertrophy surrogates; immune-cell redistribution must be separated from immunosuppression; inflammatory biomarkers must be separated from clinical inflammation/infection; sleep/energy/stress modifiers and MED boundaries must remain explicit.

## 3. Hormonal regulation at rest

Resting endocrine physiology is organized through interacting axes and feedback loops rather than one “anabolic/catabolic balance.”

Important regulators include:

- hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis;
- hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis;
- GH/IGF axis;
- pancreatic insulin/glucagon regulation;
- sympathoadrenal catecholamine system;
- thyroid and energy-balance signaling;
- circadian and sleep-wake regulation.

Resting concentrations depend on:

- time of day;
- recent food intake/fasting;
- sleep;
- energy availability;
- acute stress;
- recent exercise;
- sex/age;
- menstrual/reproductive status;
- assay/sample matrix.

Project rule:

**one resting hormone value is not a complete endocrine-state measure.**

---

## 4. Catecholamines

Norepinephrine and epinephrine rise during dynamic exercise in relation to workload/intensity.

Broad roles include:

- cardiovascular support;
- redistribution of blood flow;
- hepatic glucose output;
- lipolysis;
- modulation of pancreatic hormone secretion;
- immune-cell mobilization.

Norepinephrine reflects predominantly sympathetic neural activity; epinephrine is the major adrenomedullary hormonal catecholamine.

High-intensity exercise can produce steep catecholamine increases.

Project rule:

**catecholamines are acute integrative stress/metabolic regulators, not direct chronic adaptation scores.**

---

## 5. Cortisol

Cortisol participates in:

- glucose/substrate availability;
- vascular/stress regulation;
- immune modulation;
- adaptation to energetic/psychological challenge.

Acute exercise can increase cortisol, especially when exercise stress is sufficiently intense/long, but response depends strongly on:

- time of day/circadian phase;
- intensity;
- duration;
- prior training;
- energy status;
- psychological stress;
- sampling timing and matrix.

Training or a single low cortisol measurement is not universally “better.”

Project rule:

**cortisol is a context-sensitive stress-regulation hormone, not a stand-alone training quality or overtraining marker.**

---

## 6. Insulin

Insulin regulates glucose uptake/storage and suppresses hepatic glucose output in the fed/resting state.

During exercise, skeletal-muscle glucose uptake can rise through contraction-mediated pathways that are partly independent of insulin.

This allows active muscle to increase glucose uptake even when circulating insulin falls during prolonged exercise.

After exercise, insulin sensitivity can be enhanced.

Chronic exercise robustly improves insulin action in many insulin-resistant populations, but magnitude depends on baseline metabolic state and training mode.

Project rule:

**exercise glucose uptake is not simply “more insulin”; contraction-mediated and insulin-mediated pathways interact but are distinct.**

---

## 7. Glucagon

Glucagon supports blood-glucose homeostasis through hepatic glucose production, especially glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.

During exercise, the insulin:glucagon balance shifts to support substrate availability, particularly as duration/intensity and glycogen demand increase.

Glucagon acts together with catecholamines and other counter-regulatory signals rather than as a stand-alone “exercise hormone.”

Current human infusion studies confirm dose-dependent glucagon effects on glucose and broader substrate metabolism.

Project rule:

**glucagon is a hepatic substrate-regulation signal; its exercise role must be interpreted together with insulin, catecholamines, liver glycogen and nutritional state.**

---

## 8. Testosterone

Acute exercise can transiently alter circulating testosterone.

A 2026 systematic review of RCTs found:

- resistance exercise tends to produce larger transient increases than aerobic exercise;
- response varies by age, sex, mode, intensity and sampling method;
- recovery kinetics vary substantially.

This does not establish that the size of the acute systemic testosterone spike drives local muscle hypertrophy.

Physiological androgen signaling is biologically relevant to muscle, but normal within-person acute exercise fluctuations are not interchangeable with pharmacological androgen exposure.

Project rule:

**acute endogenous testosterone response ≠ hypertrophy dose.**

---

## 9. Growth hormone

GH can rise markedly after metabolically demanding exercise.

Magnitude depends on:

- exercise intensity;
- work duration/volume;
- rest intervals;
- metabolic stress;
- training status;
- age/sex;
- sampling timing.

Acute GH elevations can reflect exercise stress and metabolic regulation.

They do not serve as a validated surrogate for later muscle hypertrophy.

Project rule:

**large postexercise GH response does not mean a more hypertrophic workout.**

---

## 10. IGF-related signaling

IGF physiology must be separated by compartment.

Relevant compartments include:

- circulating IGF-1;
- interstitial IGF-1;
- locally expressed skeletal-muscle IGF-1 and receptor signaling.

Human resistance-training work demonstrates divergent regulation across serum, interstitial fluid and muscle and supports local muscle IGF-related signaling as more mechanistically relevant to hypertrophic tissue adaptation than a systemic postexercise IGF-1 spike.

Chronic exercise can change circulating IGF-1, but direction/magnitude varies with health status and energy state.

Project rule:

**circulating IGF-1 ≠ local muscle IGF signaling.**

---

## 11. Acute systemic hormonal response vs local adaptation

Resistance and endurance exercise can acutely alter:

- catecholamines;
- cortisol;
- testosterone;
- GH;
- insulin/glucagon balance;
- other endocrine/exerkine signals.

These responses describe whole-body regulation of exercise stress and substrate supply.

Current hypertrophy syntheses do not support acute systemic testosterone/GH/IGF-1 elevations as the primary causal mechanism for resistance-training hypertrophy.

Local adaptation depends more directly on:

- mechanical loading;
- intracellular mechanotransduction;
- local signaling;
- protein turnover;
- tissue-specific remodeling;
- repeated exposure and recovery.

Project rule:

**systemic acute hormone response is not a proxy for local chronic adaptation.**

---

## 12. Chronic endocrine adaptation

Chronic exercise does not produce one universal resting “athlete hormone profile.”

More reproducible chronic changes include:

- improved insulin sensitivity/glucose regulation in appropriate populations;
- altered hormonal response to the same absolute workload because relative stress is lower;
- context-dependent changes in IGF-1, cortisol, sex hormones and energy-regulatory signals.

Resting testosterone, GH, cortisol or IGF-1 can remain unchanged despite meaningful fitness/muscle adaptation.

Project rule:

**absence of a resting hormone change does not imply absence of training adaptation.**

---

## 13. Sleep, energy availability and stress

Endocrine background is modified by non-training stressors.

### Energy availability

Short-term experimental LEA can alter:

- leptin;
- testosterone in some studies;
- IGF-1;
- T3;
- bone-turnover signals;
- broader metabolic regulation.

Current evidence shows heterogeneity and carbohydrate availability can modify some responses.

### Sleep

Repeated sleep restriction can alter metabolic/endocrine and inflammatory physiology.

Experimental sleep-deprivation meta-analysis supports increases in IL-6 and CRP after multiple nights of partial restriction, while individual short protocols can show minimal inflammatory change.

### Psychological / life stress

Stress load can modify HPA/sympathoadrenal activity and therefore alter endocrine measurements independently of training adaptation.

Project rule:

**training biomarkers must be interpreted in the context of sleep, energy availability and non-training stress.**

---

## 14. Acute inflammatory response to exercise

A single exercise bout can transiently change:

- cytokines;
- leukocyte counts/distribution;
- acute-phase/inflammatory signaling;
- muscle-tissue immune activity.

This is not automatically pathological inflammation.

Response depends on:

- modality;
- intensity;
- duration;
- active muscle mass;
- glycogen/energy state;
- tissue damage;
- training status;
- sampling window.

Project rule:

**acute exercise inflammation is a regulated stress/repair signal, not automatically harmful systemic inflammation.**

---

## 15. Cytokines / myokines

Contracting skeletal muscle releases signaling molecules with local and systemic actions.

IL-6 is the best-established exercise-responsive myokine.

Recent meta-analyses show acute IL-6 increases after both endurance and resistance exercise, with other cytokines showing more heterogeneous responses.

Important boundary:

Exercise-induced muscle-derived IL-6 can participate in metabolic and anti-inflammatory signaling.

This is not biologically equivalent to chronically elevated IL-6 in inflammatory disease.

Other proposed myokines/exerkines vary greatly in causal certainty and translational readiness.

Project rule:

**“cytokine increased” does not by itself identify pro-inflammatory harm or a chronic adaptation mechanism.**

---

## 16. Acute immune-cell response

Acute exercise causes rapid immune-cell mobilization and redistribution.

Contemporary human data show:

- exercise-induced leukocytosis/mobilization during/just after exercise;
- preferential mobilization of cytotoxic/migratory NK and T-cell phenotypes;
- postexercise return or temporary below-baseline blood counts for some subsets;
- substantial intracellular/proteomic immune-cell changes after acute exercise.

The fall in circulating lymphocytes during recovery should not automatically be interpreted as loss of immune competence.

Project rule:

**blood immune-cell count after exercise reflects trafficking/redistribution as well as function; low circulating count ≠ automatic immunosuppression.**

---

## 17. Habitual exercise and immune/inflammatory adaptation

Regular exercise can modify immune regulation and chronic low-grade inflammatory burden.

Current meta-analyses/umbrella reviews show reductions in some circulating inflammatory markers in multiple populations, but:

- effects vary by population and modality;
- heterogeneity is substantial;
- certainty for many biomarker outcomes is low or very low;
- biomarker change is not equivalent to proven infection protection.

Project rule:

**habitual exercise generally supports healthier immune/inflammatory regulation, but “exercise boosts immunity” is too nonspecific.**

---

## 18. Exercise stress and immune function

Training stress exists on a continuum.

One demanding session, repeated heavy training, functional overreaching, nonfunctional overreaching and overtraining syndrome are not equivalent exposures.

Modern exercise-immunology evidence does not support a simple universal “open window” in which one hard workout causes immune shutdown.

At the same time, high cumulative training/competition exposure can coexist with:

- travel;
- crowd/pathogen exposure;
- sleep disruption;
- low energy availability;
- psychological stress;
- tissue damage;
- insufficient recovery.

These factors can contribute to illness susceptibility in athletes.

A 2025 meta-analysis reported more acute respiratory illnesses in athletes than non-athlete controls, but the evidence base was small and cannot isolate training physiology from exposure/context.

Project rule:

**infection risk is an integrated load + recovery + exposure problem, not a direct function of one postexercise immune marker.**

---

## 19. Insufficient recovery

Insufficient recovery is not one endocrine or immune laboratory state.

Potential contributors include:

- repeated high training load;
- insufficient sleep;
- low energy/carbohydrate availability;
- psychosocial stress;
- illness;
- inadequate rest between sessions.

These can alter cortisol, sex/energy-regulatory hormones, inflammation and immune function, but no single hormone/cytokine is a validated universal readiness or overtraining diagnostic.

Project rule:

**recovery status must be inferred from multiple longitudinal signals, not one cortisol/testosterone/cytokine value.**

Detailed monitoring → REC/TST/IND.

---

## 20. Infection / pathology boundary

PHY-16 covers expected endocrine/immune physiology around exercise.

It does not diagnose:

- endocrine disease;
- hypogonadism;
- adrenal disorders;
- diabetes;
- thyroid disease;
- REDs;
- autoimmune disease;
- immunodeficiency;
- acute infection;
- chronic inflammatory disease;
- overtraining syndrome as a medical diagnosis.

Fever, systemic illness, persistent unexplained endocrine abnormalities or clinical immune symptoms belong to MED.

Project rule:

**exercise biomarker physiology cannot substitute for clinical diagnosis.**

## 22. Established project-level model

### Acute endocrine regulation

`exercise demand`
`→ sympathetic/adrenomedullary + HPA + pancreatic + other endocrine responses`
`→ substrate mobilization + cardiovascular support + stress regulation`.

### Local adaptation boundary

`acute circulating hormone change`
is not equivalent to
`local muscle/tissue signaling → repeated remodeling → chronic adaptation`.

### Acute immune/inflammatory response

`exercise stress / muscle contraction`
`→ immune-cell mobilization/redistribution + myokine/cytokine signaling`
`→ transient surveillance/metabolic/repair response`.

### Chronic context

`repeated exercise + adequate recovery`
can support metabolic and immune regulation.

`training load + sleep/energy/stress/pathogen exposure`
jointly determine whether the total system remains adaptive or becomes maladaptive.

## 23. Training-relevant implications

1. **Do not optimize workouts for acute testosterone/GH spikes.**
2. **Do not use cortisol alone as a readiness or overtraining score.**
3. **Do not treat testosterone:cortisol ratio as a stand-alone adaptation metric.**
4. **Separate systemic IGF-1 from local muscle IGF signaling.**
5. **Interpret insulin/glucagon primarily in substrate-homeostasis context.**
6. **Expect catecholamines to scale with acute exercise stress, not chronic training quality.**
7. **Do not equate an acute cytokine rise with harmful inflammation.**
8. **Do not equate postexercise lymphopenia with immune shutdown.**
9. **Habitual exercise can improve inflammatory/metabolic regulation, but biomarker effects are heterogeneous.**
10. **Interpret endocrine/immune data together with sleep, energy availability, illness exposure and training load.**
11. **No single endocrine/immune biomarker diagnoses under-recovery or overtraining.**
12. **Clinical endocrine/immune abnormalities remain MED-owned.**

## 25. Confidence / unresolved issues

### HIGH

- exercise acutely activates catecholamine/endocrine responses;
- contraction-mediated muscle glucose uptake is not dependent solely on insulin;
- glucagon supports hepatic glucose production;
- acute systemic testosterone/GH/IGF-1 spikes are not validated hypertrophy surrogates;
- systemic vs local IGF biology must be separated;
- acute exercise changes circulating cytokines and immune-cell trafficking;
- postexercise circulating immune-cell decline can reflect redistribution rather than simple suppression;
- clinical endocrine/immune disease cannot be diagnosed from normal exercise physiology.

### MODERATE-HIGH

- chronic exercise improves insulin sensitivity in metabolically impaired populations;
- habitual exercise can lower selected chronic inflammatory markers;
- LEA can suppress/alter multiple endocrine signals;
- repeated partial sleep loss can increase selected inflammatory markers;
- heavy cumulative load plus poor recovery/exposure can increase illness risk.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- exact cortisol/GH/testosterone response to a given session;
- exact chronic resting hormone shift in a healthy trained individual;
- causal importance of circulating IGF-1 change for muscle adaptation;
- exact role of individual myokines beyond established IL-6 biology;
- mapping of acute cytokine changes to long-term outcomes;
- exact infection-risk contribution of training stress independent of travel/exposure/sleep/energy status;
- biomarker signatures of nonfunctional overreaching/overtraining.
