# MOT-02 — Reward valuation and effort allocation: probability, delay, uncertainty, effort discounting, opportunity cost, and measurement validity

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: generic population-level research and inference methodology, without user-derived observations.

## Purpose

MOT-01 established that motivation is not a single quantity and cannot be inferred from inaction or apparent effort. The next inferential problem is whether experimentally observed decisions are driven by:
- outcome magnitude or utility;
- probability and uncertainty;
- delay to outcome;
- physical or cognitive demand;
- anticipated errors and performance probability;
- effort timing;
- alternative options and opportunity costs;
- context, boredom or intrinsic value;
- estimation, measurement and model confounds.

A fitted "effort sensitivity" or "discount rate" is not a general-purpose individual motivational trait without extensive validation.

## Core rule

**A lower willingness to work or wait for a reward does not uniquely identify the psychological cost of effort, reward sensitivity, time preference, capacity, fatigue, impulsivity, apathy, or an underlying neural deficit.** These explanations must be separated by tasks, controls and model comparison.

## 1. NIMH construct scaffold

Current NIMH RDoC Positive Valence Systems distinguishes:
- **Reward (probability):** valuation under prospective reward probability;
- **Delay:** valuation as a function of time until expected reward;
- **Effort:** valuation accounting for perceived physical or cognitive effort cost;
- all under **Reward Valuation**.

These categories support distinct operationalization but do not establish three separable neural modules or assume the exhaustive universe of economic valuation.

NIMH: https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/constructs/reward-valuation

## 2. Outcome utility vs objective reward

Objective magnitude can be money, points, food or another laboratory reinforcer. The subjective utility of the outcome can depend on:
- current goals;
- internal need state, satiation and identity;
- baseline resources or reference points;
- task framing, social and moral context;
- probability, uncertainty, delay and expected control.

Equal objective reward does not imply equal experienced utility. A behavioral coefficient for magnitude may mix reward encoding, attention and information.

## 3. A schematic valuation architecture

\`SV(a|s,g) = expected subjective outcome benefit − anticipated physical/cognitive effort cost − time/delay costs − opportunity costs\`.

This is a **conceptual decomposition**, not a verified brain computation. Some models combine terms multiplicatively, others additively; utility curves may be nonlinear and reference-dependent.

A researcher must specify which variables are observed, manipulated, assumed and inferred.

## 4. Probability, expected value and risk

Expected value of a numeric reward:
\`EV = Σ_i P_i × V_i\`.

This computes an objective weighted mean if probabilities and numeric values are defined. It is not by itself human choice probability.

Separate:
- expected objective outcome;
- subjective probability belief;
- probability weighting;
- risk preference;
- outcome variance and skew;
- ambiguity about unknown probabilities;
- exploration/information-seeking.

Choice of a low-probability option need not show "irrationality" or a global sensation-seeking trait.

## 5. Risk versus ambiguity versus volatility

**Risk:** probability distribution is specified or learnable.
**Ambiguity:** relevant outcome probabilities are imprecise or unknown.
**Volatility:** the generating contingencies change over time.
**Stochasticity:** unpredictable outcomes arise from stable distributions.

These differ in what adaptive updating should do. Volatility may favor faster adaptation; noise may favor longer integration. A stated probability task differs from a task requiring probabilistic learning.

Cross-reference: LEA-03, state representation and adaptive learning-rate limits.

## 6. Probability discounting is not delay discounting

Probability-discounting tasks vary likelihood of reward; delay-discounting tasks vary when the reward is delivered.

The same person may show different choice signatures across probability and delay manipulations. A single "discounting tendency" is not guaranteed to generalize across both.

The 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis of test–retest studies estimated moderate aggregate reliability around r ≈ .66 across delay/probability tasks but found substantial methodological variation. A meta-analytic average is not a person's trait reliability.

## 7. Delay discounting

In intertemporal tasks, a choice is made between a smaller-sooner and a larger-later outcome.

A common illustrative hyperbolic formulation:
\`SV = A/(1 + kD)\`,

where A is outcome amount, D is delay and k is a fitted discounting coefficient.

This is a model family, not proof that subjective human values follow exactly this equation in every situation.

Alternative models include exponential, hyperboloid, quasi-hyperbolic, and models with interval and timing effects. Model/parameter recovery and held-out prediction are necessary for strong interpretation.

## 8. Delay of reward is not delay of effort

A task can require immediate effort for a later reward, or future effort for a later reward. These are distinguishable:
- delay from choice to effort;
- duration spent working;
- delay from work to payment;
- delay from choice to payment.

A task that does not orthogonalize these timings may not isolate temporal discounting versus effort discounting.

## 9. Effort discounting

In effort-discounting tasks, a more demanding option competes with a less demanding option; reward magnitudes and sometimes probabilities differ.

A model may reduce subjective value as anticipated effort rises. But behavioral choices also depend on success probability, perceived competence, expected failures, alternative value, speed and reward delivery delay.

Do not classify all aversion to a challenging task as a pure, stable "effort cost".

## 10. Physical and cognitive effort are distinct measurements

Physical effort may involve grip, button presses, force, motion or endurance.

Cognitive effort may involve working-memory updates, task switching, conflict resolution, sustained attention or policy abstraction.

These create different:
- motor/neuromuscular constraints;
- cognitive capacity requirements;
- chance of success;
- duration and fatigue;
- boredom and task engagement;
- subjective experiences.

There is no validated universal conversion factor from physical to cognitive effort.

## 11. Task difficulty versus subjective cost

Difficulty is an objective feature relative to a specified task and ability. Subjective effort cost is a preference or experienced aversiveness.

Difficulty can affect:
- error likelihood;
- completion time;
- opportunity to achieve reward;
- fatigue;
- confidence;
- uncertainty;
- perceived control.

If harder tasks increase error probability, the probability of success changes even if reward conditional on success is fixed. Apparent effort discounting may partly be probabilistic discounting.

## 12. Ability–motivation confound

Two decision-makers with different working-memory capacity may assign different expected success probability to identical n-back demands.

A person may refuse a high-load option because it is less achievable, not because effort is aversive.

Measure ability with a separate baseline, performance accuracy, success expectations, instruction comprehension and individualized demand calibration where suitable.

A 2024 study reported a link between short-term memory capacity and willingness to expend cognitive effort. This is evidence of a confound or joint process, not a one-way causal statement that capacity determines motivation.

## 13. Effort–delay confound

Higher effort levels often take longer to complete. A decision-maker may avoid an "effortful" option because:
- reward arrives later;
- total task duration is longer;
- additional effort is unpleasant;
- long exposure increases fatigue;
- alternatives are unavailable longer.

A 2024 validation study created a typing-based effort-discounting assessment explicitly examining this effort–delay confound.

**A pure effort interpretation requires time-matched tasks or joint modeling of delay and effort.**

## 14. Temporal dynamics of prospective effort

Effort performed now and effort scheduled later may differ in subjective value. An empirical 2026 cognitive-effort discounting study found time-dependent effort valuation and interactions with demand, while keeping reward payment timing fixed in a task condition.

Do not collapse:
- delaying payment;
- delaying work;
- postponement to escape immediate demand;
- preference for eventual future self.

Temporal effort preferences can be state- and task-dependent; they are not diagnostic of a universal procrastination mechanism.

## 15. Interacting effort, delay and probability

Realistic choices often trade all three:

\`SV(a) ≈ p_success(a) × U(reward_at_delay) − C_effort(a)\`.

This is useful as an explanatory sketch only; it assumes a chosen utility function and an additive effort cost. Human estimates may treat failure, reward timing and effort as interacting rather than independent.

To infer components, factorially manipulate effort, reward amount, reward probability and relevant intervals; fit and recover competing models.

## 16. Effort sequence and outcome type

A 2026 laboratory study on effort discounting examined effects of effort sequence and consequence type. That protocol demonstrates the importance of response sequence and task structure; the finding should not be generalized to all effort domains without replication.

Keep earned reward after effort separate from reward promised before effort, and effort measured as button pressing separate from cognitive control costs.

## 17. Opportunity costs

An opportunity cost is the value of the best forgone alternative when choosing an action.

Relevant alternatives may include:
- other paid tasks;
- intrinsically enjoyable activity;
- rest;
- information gathering;
- switching to other goals;
- avoiding boredom.

Opportunity cost is a plausible component of subjective mental effort and control allocation, **but not an empirically sufficient explanation of all demand avoidance**.

## 18. Evidence against a strong universal opportunity-cost account

A 2025 three-experiment study manipulated extrinsic and intrinsic values of alternative tasks and found **no strong evidence** for the prediction that opportunity costs dominated effort avoidance. On-task errors were more influential in that design.

This directly limits a theory that treats cognitive effort cost as purely forgone alternative value.

Interpretation: an alternative-value account can work in some settings without being a universal causal law. Competing on-task error and performance-experience models must be evaluated.

Source: "On-task errors drive effort avoidance more than opportunity costs" (2025).

## 19. Context and boredom

A 2024 study found demand preference changed with alternative available tasks and lab versus online context. In some conditions participants chose a harder activity partly to avoid boring alternatives.

This indicates that:
- easy is not always preferred;
- harder can be intrinsically preferable;
- subjective cost is relative to context;
- demand-avoidance rates are not simple indices of global aversion to mental effort.

A context effect does not prove that opportunity costs explain all effort aversion.

## 20. Decomposing cognitive effort costs

Master, Curtis and Dayan (2024), *Wagers for work*, analyzed effort-cost signals across working-memory conditions and distinguished components that tracked different task demands and subjective choice.

Inference principle: cognitive effort costs may be multidimensional and partly dependent on prior task experience. Do not use one fitted slope to label a person's overall cognition, endurance or executive capacity.

## 21. Effort as desirable

Effort can be:
- an instrumental price to pay;
- an indication of competence or progress;
- intrinsically engaging/challenging;
- an identity-affirming activity;
- a value signal learned from rewards.

MOT-01's effort paradox remains a boundary: high-demand selection is not always irrational; refusing high demand does not prove low self-discipline.

## 22. Expected Value of Control and reward choice

EVC models ask whether deploying cognitive control is worthwhile under expected benefits and its control cost.

Their mathematical variables may overlap with effort-discounting measures, but:
- choice among effortful tasks;
- executive control intensity after task choice;
- subjective cognitive effort during execution

are different dependent variables.

A normative model is not direct proof of a unique region-specific neural mechanism.

## 23. Prediction errors and valuation updating

After an outcome, subjective values may change because of:
- new information about payoff;
- new beliefs about success probability;
- learning about state/contingency;
- altered value of effort;
- changes in outcome identity or goal;
- prediction error.

A before/after choice change does not identify an RL learning rate; see LEA-03.

**Valuation at choice time is not the same as learning an outcome value.**

## 24. Neural and dopamine boundary

Reward valuations correlate with neural activity in several regions, including ventral striatum, vmPFC and circuits linked to cognitive control. Yet signals are often multiplexed and task-dependent.

Do not infer a dopamine deficiency or a specific neurobiological state from:
- preferring small immediate rewards;
- avoiding demanding tasks;
- questionnaire reports of low motivation;
- one discounting parameter.

Neural measurement/circuit causality belongs in NEU.

## 25. Test-retest reliability of delay/probability tasks

The 2024 meta-analysis estimated moderate average reliability (roughly r=.66) across discounting tasks, with differences across task versions, populations, intervals and scoring.

A stable within-task score is useful, but:
- it does not make the model mechanistically correct;
- it does not ensure cross-task validity;
- it does not imply a domain-general trait.

Cross-retest precision must be evaluated separately from between-person rank stability.

## 26. Cross-task generalization can be weak

Pike et al. (2026) evaluated five reward-related tasks. Winning models could show excellent recovery and good-to-excellent test–retest reliability, while similarly named parameters generally failed to correlate strongly across distinct tasks.

Some symptom associations were weak and concentrated in a subset of tasks.

This is crucial for effort and delay valuation:

**recoverable + reliable within task ≠ a general value/effort trait or clinical biomarker.**

## 27. Different domains may not share one discount factor

A November 2026 investigation explicitly examined relationships among cognitive effort, physical effort and delay discounting while trying to address previous methodological problems.

The existence of that study does not by itself establish a unified discount factor; evaluate its exact methods and effect estimates before making such a claim.

Evidence basis here: bibliographic/indexed preview only; full paper not accessible in this audit. **Results not independently verified.**

## 28. Context versus individual differences

Effort preference can be reshaped by:
- alternative activities;
- expected success;
- current workload;
- cognitive/physical ability;
- payment delay;
- effort timing;
- reward certainty;
- boredom;
- perceived control;
- incentives and social task framing.

Therefore a high measured effort cost may be **conditional on that state and task**, not proof of personality stability.

## 29. Reverse inference from nonaction

Refusing an option could indicate:
1. effort cost;
2. reward too small;
3. low success confidence;
4. long delay;
5. low subjective value;
6. uncertainty or ambiguity;
7. competing task value;
8. fatigue or lack of opportunity;
9. instructions misunderstood;
10. adaptive nonparticipation.

Without discriminating manipulations, "lack of motivation" is a summary label, not a mechanism.

## 30. Behavior and subjective reports are complementary

Choices, reaction times, wager amounts and self-report effort/fatigue can diverge.

One subject may report high effort but perform; another may avoid the task while describing little effort due to low expectancy or boredom.

Subjective perceived effort is a measured experience; performance errors are observed output; discounting estimates are inferred latent parameters. None subsumes the others.

## 31. Appropriate experimental design

At minimum, specify:
- outcome magnitude and identity;
- objective vs believed probabilities;
- delay until effort and until reward;
- effort type, level, duration, sequence;
- success probability and skill calibration;
- exposure/practice and task learning;
- alternatives and opportunity costs;
- environmental and social context;
- task comprehension and choice noise;
- delayed or repeated measurement;
- model competition and parameter recovery.

A good design selectively manipulates candidate costs, not just maximum task load.

## 32. Statistical model checks

For model-derived discounting parameters:
1. inspect raw choice distributions;
2. compare candidate functional forms;
3. use posterior predictive checking;
4. check parameter recovery on simulated data;
5. check model recovery to discriminate alternatives;
6. test sensitivity to missed trials and outliers;
7. assess tradeoffs between reward sensitivity, temperature, bias and cost parameters;
8. use held-out prediction;
9. evaluate retest and cross-task convergence;
10. check parameter scale and interpretation under new reward units.

A mathematically estimable k is not automatically psychologically identifiable.

## 33. Ecological validity

Real-world effort decisions often include:
- different reinforcement histories;
- open-ended choices and uncertain opportunity sets;
- delayed feedback;
- social costs;
- moral goals;
- adaptive task switching;
- fluctuating states;
- plans and commitments.

Lab discounting tasks can explain local choices without being reliable predictors of life outcomes. Demonstrate transport rather than assuming it.

## 34. Evidence confidence tiers

### High confidence for practical inference
- Reward probability, delay and effort are distinct task dimensions;
- effort and delay can be confounded;
- capacity and expected success can affect choice;
- discounting curves are model-dependent;
- contextual alternatives matter in at least some tasks;
- reliability does not imply cross-task construct equivalence.

### Supported but task-dependent
- opportunity cost can influence effort valuation;
- boredom can lead to preference for demanding options;
- on-task error experience can shape demand avoidance;
- timing of future effort changes valuation;
- reward/effort preferences may have meaningful within-task retest reliability.

### Not established as universal
- all cognitive effort costs are opportunity costs;
- physical and cognitive effort discounting share one fixed latent trait;
- fitted k determines impulsivity or pathology;
- dopamine uniquely explains effort avoidance;
- every low-effort choice reflects low motivation;
- one task score generalizes across real-world contexts.

## 35. Reward Valuation and Effort Inference Gate — 50 questions

### A. Outcome and probabilities
1. What is the reward type and identity?
2. Is objective magnitude defined?
3. What is subjective utility?
4. Is probability specified, learned or ambiguous?
5. Is subjective probability measured?
6. Are risk and ambiguity distinguished?
7. Is environmental volatility distinguished from noise?
8. Is outcome uncertainty distinct from uncertainty in effort completion?

### B. Timing
9. Is reward delay specified?
10. Is effort delay specified?
11. Is effort duration specified?
12. Can effort alter outcome waiting time?
13. Are immediate and future effort conditions compared?
14. Is temporal discounting model choice justified?
15. Are reward time and work time held constant where necessary?

### C. Effort and ability
16. Is effort physical or cognitive?
17. What cognitive/physical operation is required?
18. Is task difficulty calibrated to ability?
19. Could errors reduce expected reward?
20. Is perceived success probability measured?
21. Is effort aversion confounded with low competence?
22. Are fatigue and state controlled?
23. Is effort measured as intensity, duration, choice or report?
24. Could learning/familiarity change perceived demand?

### D. Alternatives and control
25. Which alternative task is available?
26. What is the best forgone alternative?
27. Is intrinsic enjoyment of alternatives measured?
28. Could boredom drive high-demand choice?
29. Does on-task error history explain avoidance?
30. Is effort sometimes positively valued?
31. Is control allocation separated from option choice?
32. Is reward-value change separated from policy selection?

### E. Psychometrics
33. Does the task isolate the intended construct?
34. Are raw choices stable enough for model fitting?
35. Are competing discounting models compared?
36. Are parameter recovery and model recovery adequate?
37. Are reward scale and inverse temperature identified?
38. Is test–retest reliability known?
39. Is cross-task convergence established?
40. Are parameters stable across incentive magnitudes?
41. Is subjective effort report validated against choices?
42. Does the score predict behavior outside the task?

### F. Inference boundary
43. Could nonaction reflect constraints rather than valuation?
44. Could option timing produce an apparent effort effect?
45. Does a state effect masquerade as a trait?
46. Is a neural claim supported by neural evidence?
47. Is a clinical claim supported by clinical validation?
48. Do results generalize to another age/population/task?
49. What competing mechanisms remain plausible?
50. What is the narrowest warranted conclusion?

## 36. Common failure modes

A. Avoid high effort -> laziness: **unsupported moral leap**.

B. Low willingness to work -> low reward sensitivity: **invalid without discriminating reward and effort parameters**.

C. Effort > delay -> pure effort discounting: **invalid if effort changes duration/payment**.

D. Hard task rejected -> high effort cost: **invalid if success probability fell**.

E. Easy task preferred -> always wants low cognitive demand: **context/boredom may reverse preference**.

F. Opportunity cost theory -> unique cause: **direct evidence against a strong universal claim**.

G. Immediate reward preferred -> stable impulsivity trait: **unsupported cross-context inference**.

H. Fitted hyperbolic k -> actual neuronal computation: **model-to-mechanism leap**.

I. Cognitive effort coefficient -> physical effort coefficient: **invalid without cross-domain evidence**.

J. Reliable score -> universal construct: **reliability and validity separate**.

K. Physiological signal -> motivation trait: **reverse inference**.

L. Delayed effort -> delayed reward: **different manipulated quantities**.

M. Low effort choice -> clinical syndrome: **diagnostic overreach**.

## Canonical inference rule

**Define outcome/subjective utility -> probability and uncertainty -> timing of effort and reward -> physical/cognitive demand and ability -> success probability -> available alternatives, boredom and on-task errors -> separate valuation from choice/control -> test model recoverability, reliability and ecological validity -> restrict inference.**

Never:
**refusal -> laziness; delay coefficient -> impulsivity; effort coefficient -> trait; opportunity cost -> all demand avoidance; cross-task same parameter name -> same mechanism; lab choice -> clinical diagnosis.**
