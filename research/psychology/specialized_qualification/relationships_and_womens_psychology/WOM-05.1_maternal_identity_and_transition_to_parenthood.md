# WOM-05.1 — Maternal identity, motivation and psychological adjustment during transition to parenthood

Date: 2026-11-26. Status: bounded research unit COMPLETE_FOR_SPECIFIED_SCOPE (internal review only); WOM-05 remains IN_PROGRESS. This is generic scientific material, not clinical qualification or an individual assessment.

## 1. Question and dependency gate
How can a change in a mother's roles and daily actions be distinguished from change in identity, goal valuation, personality and well-being? Practical gap: inferring durable motives or trait change from behavior after a major role transition. Target: adult women around pregnancy and first parenthood; other parities, adoptive/non-gestational motherhood, cultural and structural settings require separate applicability checks. Reopened relevant excerpts of WOM-02.1, DEV-03, REL-01 and WOM-04.3. Retain IND-03 and INT-02. Distinguish selection, anticipation, transition, adaptation, ordinary age-related development and measurement/reference shifts. Closure for this unit: directly inspected relevant scientific evidence, counterexamples, concrete analytic rules, synthetic transfer checks and limitations; not an exhaustive specialist audit.

## 2. Evidence and provenance
Targeted public search on 2026-11-26: maternal identity qualitative synthesis, motherhood well-being longitudinal experience sampling, personality changes with comparison groups, life-goal stability and self-control. Selected evidence, not systematic/exhaustive. No individual data in search. No full article read end-to-end; source access and limits below.

E1. Finlayson, Crossland, Bonet & Downe (2020), What matters to women in the postnatal period: A meta-synthesis of qualitative studies. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0231415 . Publisher HTML study-selection, appraisal, synthesis, sample and findings sections inspected. 36 papers, 15 countries, >800 women; 33 papers from high/upper-middle income countries; 19 papers first-time mothers only. Some papers share underlying studies. Thematic synthesis with CERQual: 22 descriptive findings, generally moderate/high confidence. Accounts distinguish integration of maternal identity, competence and confidence, changes in relationships, mixed emotional experiences, bodily adjustment, and practical/emotional support. Qualitative themes establish reported experiences and meanings, not their prevalence, universal sequence, or effects of support interventions. Representation and translation limits; paper count not independent sample count. No normative requirement to feel fulfilled or sacrifice other identities.

E2. van Scheppingen et al. (2016), Personality Trait Development During the Transition to Parenthood: A Test of Social Investment Theory. https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550616630032 . Complete final publisher abstract inspected, full text restricted. Representative first-parent vs nonparent comparison did not find stronger average growth in emotional stability, agreeableness or conscientiousness among parents. Agreeableness and openness varied with duration in role; extraversion and openness predicted role entry across sexes, conscientiousness additionally in women. No sample size, exact effect, precision or attrition independently verified here. Null result is not equivalence, and aggregate stability is compatible with individual change. Predictors of role entry do not establish voluntary choice or causal mechanisms.

E3. Wehner, van Scheppingen & Bleidorn (2022 issue), Stability and change in major life goals during the transition to parenthood. https://doi.org/10.1177/0890207021996894 . Publisher sample excerpt, discussion/limitations/conclusion inspected; previously covered in WOM-02.1. Preregistered longitudinal parent/nonparent comparison, N=542 initial, 248 in parent subsample, 53.2% women. Expecting mothers reported lower achievement and variety goal importance than comparison women, little evidence for mean-level change in goals following transition. Prenatal measurement once, after pregnancy began; cannot distinguish selection from earlier anticipatory change. Group age imbalance, modest subgroup power, selective attrition and power-goal model-fit concern. Importance is not actual goal effort; preserve possibility of stable values under altered constraints. Do not double-count as new independent evidence relative to WOM-02.1.

E4. van Scheppingen, Denissen & Bleidorn (2018), Stability and Change in Self-Control during the Transition to Parenthood. https://doi.org/10.1002/per.2172 . Publisher discussion/results and limitations inspected. Four-wave prospective Dutch parent/nonparent comparison (initial total >500; exact group sample not verified here). New mothers initially higher in reported dispositional self-control; nonlinear declines, strongest early, continuing during follow-up to 18 months. Daily vs trait measure differed for fathers. Concurrent stress associations did not establish moderation of maternal change. No prepregnancy measurement, attrition, self-report, six-month spacing, possible response-standard shift. Counterexample to invariant trait growth; decline relative to initial score does not establish impaired capacity (authors note final maternal average did not fall below overall sample mean). No inference to brain or hormonal mechanism.

E5. Tran, Ivanova, Stavrova & Reitz (online 2025), Changes in psychological well-being across the transition to motherhood: Combining longitudinal and experience sampling methods. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000573 . Complete author-institution abstract and publication metadata inspected; PubMed browser verification; full paper not inspected. Preregistered 3-wave longitudinal study with 7-day experience sampling per wave, 161 first-time Dutch mothers, 20 weeks pregnant to 6 months postpartum. Positive affect and meaning increased, while life and relationship satisfaction declined from pregnancy to 2–3 months postpartum; no further significant mean changes reported thereafter. No significant change in intraindividual variability. Exploratory daily predictor patterns (e.g. sleep, infant crying) not causal proof. Pregnancy baseline and absence of a nonparent comparator in inspected description limit causal attribution; exact estimates, attrition and measures not verified. Well-being is multidimensional; opposite mean trajectories do not imply that every participant had both changes.

E6. Transition to Parenthood and Changes in Wellbeing and Relationship Quality: A Propensity Score Matching Approach (2026), https://doi.org/10.1177/02654075261499759 . Indexed publisher extracts only; both direct opens failed (cache miss). New Zealand data 2012–2024, 1,260 matched participants becoming parents and 1,260 controls; authors report small positive life satisfaction effects and relationship-quality challenges. Matching observed sociodemographics and baseline outcomes helps address measured selection; residual confounding, missingness, overlap, exact estimates and maternal-specific effects not verified. Not a contradiction of E5 until outcome, reference time, population and design align; supporting evidence only.

## 3. Operational distinctions
Maternal identity = self-concept/role meaning; distinguish centrality, integration, commitment, perceived competence, enacted caregiving, child bond, satisfaction with care, external recognition, and autonomy. These are not interchangeable measures. Parenthood status is an event/role indicator, not an intrinsic psychological type. Motherhood is not synonymous with pregnancy or giving birth. Distinguish wanting a child, valuing parenthood, wanting particular caregiving arrangements, actual available choices, and enacted responsibilities. Ambivalence and multiple identities can coexist; neither implies lack of care.

Model as interacting but not identified causal variables:
- prior traits, values, resources, relationships and intentions -> selection into transition and baseline;
- anticipation and pregnancy-related changes -> prenatal measurements;
- care demands, child needs, sleep, health, paid work, social expectations, support availability and division of labor -> current opportunity and effort constraints;
- role appraisals, identity integration, perceived competence, goal conflict and emotion -> possible subjective adaptation;
- outcomes feed back into relationships and future choices.

Do not infer mediation from this diagram. Support expected vs received vs perceived, and shared demands vs actor/partner effects, require separate evidence. A role can be valued and burdensome; reduced participation can result from time or opportunity rather than reduced interest; distress may coexist with positive meaning. Changes in trait questionnaires can reflect reference shifts, repeated states or durable trait change; distinguish with temporal coverage, measurement comparability, informants, behavior and controls. A single postpartum observation cannot distinguish these models.

## 4. Transfer and revision gate
1. Establish exact transition, timing and target construct; avoid universal stage timelines.
2. Specify observed behavior and voluntary self-report separately; compare available pretransition evidence, not imagined baseline.
3. Assess realistic alternatives: stable preference under constraints, anticipatory reprioritization, role learning, lasting value change, temporary exhaustion or relationship context. Consider coexistence.
4. Evaluate naturally occurring variation: opportunity to pursue old goal, rest/support changes, and comparable contexts over time. Stable importance with restored activity supports opportunity explanation; sustained changed valuation across feasible alternatives supports a value-change hypothesis, not a unique cause.
5. Differentiate mean-level outcome, person trajectory, state variability, and individual-specific predictor. No group-average-to-person conclusion.
6. Treat postpartum distress, impairment or safety concerns as separate clinical/safety questions under CLN/SAF; no diagnosis from ordinary transition, no dismissal of serious symptoms as normal motherhood. Respect autonomy, voluntary disclosures and preferences.
7. Keep model open where discriminating evidence is absent. No covert testing, pressured disclosures, stereotyped maternal duties, or inferred consent.

## 5. Internal transfer checks
Rubric before applying examples: correct construct, at least two compatible alternatives, discriminating evidence, limits; critical errors include essentialism, diagnosis without grounds, causal inflation, privacy breach or coercion. Original synthetic examples, unrelated to any real person.
A. A new mother pauses a ceramics class but reports unchanged enthusiasm. Expected: distinguish time allocation from value and trait change; compare feasible access and repeated reports. Result: retained both competing constraints and possible evolving interests; no causal verdict.
B. A mother describes more purpose but lower satisfaction with daily routines. Expected: separate meaning from satisfaction, examine support and demands; do not collapse into global happiness. Result: compatible dimensions, no diagnosis.
C. A cohort shows mothers differ before birth and have stable average goals after. Expected: prebirth difference may reflect anticipation or selection; null change is not universal stability. Result: both alternatives and individual variation retained; earlier repeated measures and comparable groups needed.
D. A self-control questionnaire falls after birth while actual task performance is unmeasured. Expected: do not infer neurological impairment; consider demands, response standards, transient states and trait change. Result: multiple hypotheses preserved; repeat measurements and observable task/context data required.
Internal checks met specified rubric on these four authored cases; not independent tests or validated forecast calibration. No assertion of professional licensure or model-weight learning.
