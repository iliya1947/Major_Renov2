# PHY-14 — Cardiovascular exercise physiology

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-18**  
Область: 03 — Physiology

## 0. Core decision

Exercise cardiovascular physiology is an integrated pressure-and-flow system.

Canonical model:

`exercise task demand`
`→ autonomic + vascular + mechanical + respiratory adjustments`
`→ HR/SV/Q + arterial pressure + regional flow + venous return`
`→ acute oxygen delivery and pressure regulation`
`→ repeated exposure`
`→ mode-specific central + vascular/microvascular adaptation`.

Resistance and aerobic exercise use overlapping cardiovascular machinery but do not produce identical acute responses or chronic adaptation phenotypes.

## 1. Heart rate

HR generally rises with exercise demand.

It is influenced by:

- autonomic drive;
- active muscle mass;
- work rate;
- set duration/repetition accumulation;
- fatigue;
- breathing/strain;
- modality.

Project rule:

**HR is a cardiovascular response variable, not a universal stand-alone intensity measure across exercise modes.**

## 2. Stroke volume

SV is blood ejected per beat.

During dynamic aerobic exercise it commonly rises from rest through interacting changes in:

- ventricular filling/preload;
- contractility;
- end-systolic volume;
- afterload.

The exact trajectory is context dependent.

Project rule:

**SV does not obey one universal moderate-intensity plateau rule.**

## 3. Cardiac output

`Q = HR × SV`.

Both aerobic and resistance exercise can substantially increase Q.

Project rule:

**Q cannot be inferred from HR alone; HR, SV and Q are separate constructs.**

## 4. Blood pressure

Arterial pressure reflects the interaction of flow, vascular resistance/compliance and the exercise task.

### Dynamic aerobic exercise
Typical healthy pattern:
- SBP rises;
- DBP often changes little or falls slightly;
- MAP rises less than SBP because systemic vasodilation offsets part of the Q increase.

### Resistance exercise
BP can rise markedly during active sets.

Response depends on:

- load;
- muscle mass;
- repetitions/set duration;
- fatigue/proximity to failure;
- mechanical compression;
- breathing/Valsalva;
- measurement timing/method.

Project rule:

**exercise BP is protocol dependent; there is no single fixed “RT blood pressure response.”**

## 5. Systemic vascular resistance

Total peripheral/systemic resistance is a whole-circulation construct.

During dynamic aerobic exercise:

- active-muscle vasodilation increases;
- Q rises;
- systemic resistance commonly falls.

During resistance exercise, local mechanical compression, sympathetic vasoconstriction, metabolic vasodilation and postcontraction hyperemia coexist.

Project rule:

**systemic TPR ≠ local exercising-muscle vascular resistance.**

## 6. Blood-flow redistribution

Exercise redistributes cardiac output.

Typical changes include:

- increased active-muscle flow;
- increased coronary flow;
- relative reduction of renal/splanchnic flow;
- increased skin flow when thermoregulatory demand rises.

Project rule:

**redistribution is graded and regional, not complete on/off shutdown of inactive tissues.**

## 7. Venous return

Venous return during exercise depends on interacting mechanisms:

- skeletal-muscle pump;
- respiratory pump;
- venous valves;
- venous tone;
- pressure gradients/cardiac suction;
- blood volume and venous compliance.

Project rule:

**venous return cannot be reduced to the muscle pump alone.**

## 8. Acute resistance-exercise response

Integrated model:

`contraction + central/autonomic drive + reflexes + breathing/strain`
`→ HR/Q response + BP response + phase-dependent vascular behavior`.

Longer/fatiguing sets can create larger cardiovascular stress even when external relative load is unchanged.

Project rule:

**%1RM alone does not describe acute RT cardiovascular load.**

## 9. Chronic resistance-training adaptation

RT can produce:

- lower resting/ambulatory BP in some populations;
- selected vascular/endothelial improvements;
- selected central/hemodynamic changes;
- improved functional capacity.

Effects vary by baseline BP, population and protocol.

Project rule:

**RT has real cardiovascular adaptation, but it is not assumed to reproduce the full endurance-training phenotype.**

## 10. Acute aerobic-exercise response

Canonical response:

`metabolic demand ↑`
`→ HR ↑ + SV commonly ↑`
`→ Q ↑`
`+ active-muscle vasodilation`
`→ systemic resistance ↓ relative to rest`
`→ regional oxygen delivery ↑`.

Project rule:

**acute aerobic exercise is an integrated flow-delivery/pressure-regulation response, not simply a higher HR.**

## 11. Chronic aerobic adaptation

Central adaptations can include:

- blood/plasma-volume expansion;
- greater ventricular filling;
- greater maximal SV;
- greater maximal Q;
- lower HR at the same absolute submaximal workload;
- cardiac remodeling with sustained training.

Peripheral adaptations can include:

- vascular remodeling/function;
- capillarization;
- mitochondrial/oxidative changes;
- altered oxygen extraction.

Project rule:

**aerobic adaptation combines central and peripheral changes; their relative contribution is protocol- and person-dependent.**

## 12. Postexercise hypotension

BP can remain below pre-exercise values after some aerobic and resistance sessions.

Magnitude and mechanism depend on baseline BP, protocol and hemodynamics.

Project rule:

**acute PEH ≠ validated stand-alone surrogate for chronic BP adaptation.**

## 13. Capillary adaptation

Exercise can alter skeletal-muscle capillarization.

Important metrics are different:

### Capillary-to-fiber ratio
Capillaries associated with fibers.

### Capillary density
Capillaries per tissue area.

### Fiber CSA
Changes tissue-area denominator and therefore affects density.

Endurance/HIT/SIT evidence supports increases in capillaries per fiber, while density behaves differently across protocols.

RT-related capillary responses are less uniform and can be obscured by fiber hypertrophy.

Project rule:

**capillary density ≠ capillary number; interpret capillarization together with fiber size.**

## 14. Normal physiology vs clinical abnormality

PHY-14 covers expected exercise physiology.

It does not diagnose/manage:

- hypertension;
- abnormal exercise BP;
- arrhythmia;
- ischemia;
- syncope/presyncope;
- heart failure;
- valvular disease;
- abnormal recovery;
- individual Valsalva risk.

Project rule:

**clinical cardiovascular interpretation belongs to MED.**

## 15. Canonical project rules

1. HR, SV and Q are distinct.
2. `Q = HR × SV`.
3. HR alone is not a universal exercise-intensity metric.
4. SV does not follow one universal plateau.
5. BP response depends on exercise task, fatigue and breathing.
6. Systemic TPR is not local muscle vascular resistance.
7. Blood-flow redistribution is graded and regional.
8. Venous return is multi-mechanistic.
9. Resistance exercise is a genuine cardiovascular stressor.
10. %1RM alone does not define RT cardiovascular stress.
11. RT cardiovascular adaptation is not identical to endurance adaptation.
12. Aerobic adaptation combines central and peripheral components.
13. Acute PEH is not a chronic surrogate.
14. Capillary density is not capillary number.
15. Normal exercise physiology cannot diagnose cardiovascular disease.

## 16. Training-relevant implications

1. **Do not use HR alone to compare internal intensity across RT and aerobic exercise.**
2. **Interpret RT cardiovascular load from the whole set structure, fatigue and breathing, not just %1RM.**
3. **Do not infer SV or Q from HR without measurement/appropriate model.**
4. **Do not interpret one BP value outside protocol and measurement context.**
5. **Separate systemic resistance from local vascular behavior.**
6. **Treat venous return as an integrated pump/volume/pressure system.**
7. **Expect mode-specific chronic adaptation rather than one generic “cardio adaptation.”**
8. **Do not rank chronic BP programs from one acute PEH response.**
9. **Interpret capillary metrics with fiber CSA.**
10. **Route abnormal/symptomatic cardiovascular findings to MED.**

## 17. What PHY-14 does not prescribe

Not owned here:

- individual cardiovascular screening;
- BP/ECG diagnostic thresholds;
- Valsalva contraindications;
- exact aerobic dose;
- exact RT dose for BP;
- exercise selection;
- HR-zone prescription;
- concurrent-training design;
- personal cardiovascular risk.

These remain MED, TEC, PRO, TST, GPP and IND.
