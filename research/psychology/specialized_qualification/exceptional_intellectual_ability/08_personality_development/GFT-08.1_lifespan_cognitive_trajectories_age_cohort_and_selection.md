## 2. Evidence cards
E01 — Breit, Scherrer, Tucker-Drob & Preckel (2024), The stability of cognitive abilities: A meta-analytic review of longitudinal studies, https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000425 ; indexed published text https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11626988/ and author abstract PMID38330347. 205 samples, 1288 manifest correlations, N87508 across abilities per indexed text. Model-estimated mean rank-order stability rho=.76 at age20, five-year interval; rises rapidly through childhood, stays relatively high from late adolescence to late adulthood. This is a group correlation, not percent of IQ preserved or probability of an individual's stability. Four distinct questions: rank-order, mean-level, individual-level and ipsative/profile continuity. No significant moderation by *sample mean* ability level in analyzed subset, not within-person slope, not direct high-tail evidence, not equivalence. Mean IQ available for 79 samples/456 effects (indexed methods); mean of sample levels ~104.19, SD10.37. Scope and sparse moderator data, range restriction, interval and tests differ. Includes Lothian, VCAP, BETULA, LOGIC among cohorts; subsequent studies are not all independent evidence. No full model or supplement reread.
E02 — Hartshorne & Germine (2015), https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614567339 , indexed author full-text excerpts https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4441622/ and APS abstract. Cross-sectional age profiles from online tasks and Wechsler normative data show nonuniform peak ages across abilities; vocabulary often later than speed, some memory tasks later than other speed/fluid tasks. Not a longitudinal curve for each individual; cohort, sampling, platform familiarity, domain and test matter. No exact universal peak ages assigned. No evidence that a peak in one task is peak of total intelligence. 
E03 — Salthouse (2010), Influence of age on practice effects in longitudinal neurocognitive change, PMID20804244, indexed author abstract and results https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/2933088/ : comparison of longitudinal participants and first-time testers of corresponding ages supports retest contribution to discrepancy, not all of discrepancy explained. Practice effects often larger at younger ages in study. Salthouse (2016), Continuity of cognitive change across adulthood, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4740316/ PMID26238759; 2330 adults 18–80, abstract and indexed discussion: cross-sectional decline often earlier than longitudinal, previous test experience can bias apparent longitudinal improvement. Trajectories of normal cognitive aging (2019 issue), PMID30211596: >5000 cross-sectional and ~1600 three-occasion longitudinal adults. No new exact slope estimates, no independence assumed within VCAP reports. Practice can be real learning but invalid as generalized ability inference without transfer; matching age does not eliminate selection or historical differences.
E04 — Tucker-Drob et al. (2022), A strong dependency between changes in fluid and crystallized abilities in human cognitive aging, https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abj2422 ; indexed author abstract/results https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8809681/ . VCAP and BETULA growth models show positive associations between individual rates of Gf and Gc change even when mean-level trajectories differ. Some people gain knowledge despite declining fluid performance; association does not prove single common mechanism or immutable decline. Distinguish relative slope correlation, average slope and individual compensation; not infer knowledge is unaffected by aging. VCAP overlaps family of E03 studies; meta-analysis E01 includes both cohorts. No exact slope-correlation estimate extracted.
E05 — Gow et al. (2011 issue), Stability and change in intelligence from age11 to ages70,79,87, PMID20973608 indexed author abstract: Moray House Test raw correlations .67/.66/.51, respectively, cohort 1936 at70 and cohort1921 at79/87; cannot attribute difference solely to interval or age, cohorts differ. Authors distinguish high later-life level from slower decline. Conte et al. (2022), Cognitive change before old age (11 to70) predicts cognitive change during old age (70 to82), PMID36113037 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9660354/ indexed author abstract: LBC1936 recruitment N1091, standardized beta=.163 (p=.001) for prior gain and later slower g decline after specified adjustments. Not evidence that high initial IQ causally slows aging; prior change and initial level are distinct predictors. Salthouse (2012), Does direction and magnitude of cognitive change depend on initial level of ability?, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3375710/ indexed results: little indication of ability-dependent change in five abilities when accounting for regression to mean. Different populations/designs and predictors. LBC1936 repeated publications not independent cohorts; E01 includes both LBC cohorts. Selection/survival, measurement error and latent change modeling assumptions remain.
E06 — Schneider, Niklas & Schmiedeler (2014), https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2014.02.001 ; LMU institutional author abstract opened https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/68559/ : ~200 LOGIC participants tested 4–12,17,23, initially high/average/low subgroups; high initial group less rank-stable than low, yet maintained average advantage and more often pursued higher educational tracks. Exact group cutoffs and conditional estimates not extracted; grouping by noisy baseline restricts range and induces regression; no causal education effect from this study. The influence of preschool IQ on the individual-order stability of intelligence into adulthood (2023), https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.16925 ; indexed published HTML methods/results https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/apa.16925 (direct 403). Lower rank stability for high-initial group, 9–14 interval compared with medium/low significant; change of test, baseline selection/range, missing data and population composition complicate interpretation. Not contradiction of E01 sample-level null moderator (different question). Neither substantiates lower absolute intellectual growth of gifted adults. 2021 Temporal stability of specific ability scores and intelligence profiles in high ability students, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0160289621000222 indexed author text, 114 students in gifted classes, mean age14.11, six-month BIS-HB; mean IQ116.72 to125.48 in reported sample, high specific score rank stability. Not causal growth, cannot generalize to adults or infer fixed profile; full profile metrics and design not retrieved.

## 3. Developmental inference gate (PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC)
Separate four questions: absolute/equated mean-level change, rank-order continuity in a reference population, reliable individual change, within-person profile continuity. An age-standardized IQ score is relative to age and norm version; the same age-standardized score can coexist with absolute gain or loss. Raw scores on differing tests are not automatically comparable; use linked/equated or invariant scales with precision, conditions, and version. A profile difference at one occasion is not developmental asynchrony. 
Illustration only: age-specific reference mean 40 and SD10, score60 -> z2; later reference mean50, SD10, score70 -> z2, both correspond to IQ130 on illustrative linear scale, raw gain10 if same equated scale. Later score65 -> z1.5 (IQ122.5), still absolute gain5 while relative rank falls. Do not use hypothetical numbers as norms or infer exact percentile at tail. Conversely constant rank at older age can coexist with absolute decline. 
Age = calendar period minus birth cohort (same time units). Free additive linear effects of all three cannot be identified from data alone; e.g. adding d*age - d*period + d*cohort leaves fitted prediction unchanged. Cross-sectional age differences combine cohort and age; longitudinal observations include age and period, repeated testing, selective attrition, mortality and changing measurement. Cohort-sequential designs add overlaps but need substantive restrictions/sensitivity checks. Do not infer causal mechanism from chronological age. Separate initial level and change slope, within-person vs between-person association. Higher initial ability may give higher later level even if slope same; no universal protection or accelerated decline. 
Baseline selection: conditional expectation in a toy standardized bivariate normal with equal means/variances and correlation .8, selected observed z=3 at T1 -> expected T2 z=2.4 absent group mean change. This is conditional regression of observed scores, not necessarily true latent decline, not individual prediction or empirical tail estimate. Ceiling, initial group restriction, reliability, practice, context and cohort confound apparent differences; use independent repeated baselines, comparable scales and local precision where possible; check change-score coupling (T2−T1 includes T1). Reliable change needs conditional measurement error, practice adjustment and appropriate norms/assumptions; do not apply a generic SEM at extreme IQ or presume test equivalence (GFT-02). 
| Observation | Narrow inference | Necessary check |
|---|---|---|
| High IQ in childhood | Relative measured performance then | Test version, age norms, independent adult measurement |
| Higher raw performance | Observed improvement on same or linked task | Equating, practice, error, transfer |
| Stable adult IQ | Relative score continuity | Absolute/linked score and reference norms |
| Older group scores lower | Between-group difference | Cohort, period, selection and measurement |
| Repeated lower score | Observed difference | Reliable change, task/health/context, practice, alternate explanations |
| High later-life level | Measured level, not slower decline | Multiwave slopes and baseline levels |
| More domain experience | Exposure/knowledge evidence | Distinguish expertise from broad ability and selection |
General framework integrates DEV/PER, GFT-01/02/05/06 and GFT-07. No clinical diagnosis; persistent concerning functional changes are not inferred or dismissed by group averages.
