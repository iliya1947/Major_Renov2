# 2. Canonical sampling principle

Qualitative sampling is not primarily a smaller version of probability sampling.

Its central question is:

**Which people, cases, settings, events, interactions or materials can provide the information needed for this specific qualitative question and methodology?**

The target is generally information relevance, depth, conceptual variation or theoretical development rather than estimation of population parameters.

### Critical rule

A qualitative sample should be evaluated against:

- scientific question;
- methodology;
- unit of analysis;
- inferential target;
- sampling strategy;
- information richness;
- variation required;
- analytic strategy.

Not against a universal N.

---

# 3. Sampling unit is not always a participant

Possible qualitative sampling units include:

- person;
- dyad;
- family;
- team;
- case;
- organization;
- site;
- community;
- event;
- interaction;
- time period;
- document;
- media item;
- narrative;
- conversation sequence;
- observation episode.

### Fitness example

Question:
How is coaching authority enacted during strength-training feedback?

Possible sampling units:

- coach-athlete sessions;
- correction sequences;
- feedback episodes;
- coaches/athletes;
- training phases.

A participant-only sampling record would miss the actual interactional units.

---

# 4. Selection vs recruitment

These are separate methodological processes.

## Selection logic

Who or what should enter the sample and why?

Examples:

- maximum variation;
- criterion;
- theoretical;
- critical-case.

## Recruitment mechanism

How are eligible people reached?

Examples:

- clinic invitation;
- email;
- social media;
- participant referral;
- coach referral.

### Critical rule

Snowball referral can be a recruitment mechanism inside a purposive selection strategy.

Do not collapse:

selection logic
with
recruitment channel.

---

# 5. Purposive / purposeful sampling

Purposive sampling is an umbrella logic for deliberately selecting information-rich cases relevant to the study purpose.

It does not specify one exact strategy.

### Canonical project representation

purposeful sampling
→ specify subtype(s)
→ specify eligibility
→ specify dimensions of variation
→ specify recruitment route.

### Critical rule

Writing only "purposive sampling was used" is often insufficient.

Extract what was being purposively optimized.

---

# 6. Criterion sampling

Criterion sampling selects cases that meet predefined conditions important to the question.

Examples:

- experienced ACL reconstruction;
- completed at least 12 weeks of exercise program;
- maintained resistance training for 2 years;
- coached elite athletes for 5+ seasons.

### Strength

Creates direct relevance.

### Risk

Criteria can be too broad or too narrow.

### Rule

Eligibility criterion does not automatically guarantee information richness.

A person can meet the criterion but have little direct experience of the specific phenomenon being studied.

---

# 7. Maximum-variation sampling

Maximum-variation sampling deliberately includes cases differing on characteristics expected to matter.

Potential fitness dimensions:

- age;
- sex;
- training experience;
- injury severity;
- competition level;
- exercise setting;
- adherence trajectory;
- socioeconomic access;
- coach-athlete context.

### Purpose

1. expose important variation;
2. identify patterns that persist across variation;
3. avoid mistaking one narrow subgroup's experience for the whole phenomenon.

### Critical rule

Maximum variation is not probability representativeness.

It is deliberate heterogeneity.

---

# 8. Homogeneous sampling

Homogeneous sampling deliberately narrows variation.

Appropriate when the aim is deep understanding of a specific subgroup.

Examples:

- novice female powerlifters in first competition season;
- elite male sprinters returning from hamstring injury;
- older adults after one specific falls-prevention program.

### Strength

Supports depth and comparability.

### Risk

Cannot automatically support claims beyond the narrow group.

### Methodology link

IPA commonly values relatively homogeneous samples because of its idiographic cross-case logic.

---

# 9. Typical-case sampling

Typical cases are selected as ordinary/normal examples of a setting or phenomenon.

Example:

- average community gym rather than unusually high-performing or failing facility.

### Critical rule

"Typical" is an analytic selection judgment.

It does not mean statistically representative unless population data independently establish representativeness.

---

# 10. Extreme / deviant case sampling

Selects unusually high, low, successful, unsuccessful or otherwise atypical cases.

Examples:

- athlete recovering far faster than expected;
- exercise program with exceptionally low dropout;
- gym with unusually strong long-term adherence.

### Purpose

Rare cases can make hidden mechanisms visible.

### Critical boundary

Extreme-case findings should not be generalized as normal prevalence.

---

# 11. Intensity sampling

Intensity sampling selects strong manifestations of a phenomenon without choosing the most extreme outliers.

Example:

- lifters with substantial but not catastrophic fear of reinjury.

### Purpose

Obtain rich examples while avoiding singular edge cases.

### Distinction

Intensity ≠ extreme/deviant.

---

# 12. Critical-case sampling

A critical case is strategically selected because it is especially informative for a proposition or practical inference.

Potential logic:

- if a process fails even under unusually favorable conditions, this challenges the assumption that the process is easy to implement;
- if a barrier persists in a setting with excellent resources, resource scarcity alone is unlikely to explain it.

### Critical rule

Critical-case logic must be explicit.

Calling an interesting case "critical" is not sufficient.

---

# 13. Confirming and disconfirming case sampling

As analysis develops, researchers may deliberately seek:

- cases supporting an emerging pattern;
- cases contradicting it.

Purpose:

- test boundaries;
- refine explanation;
- identify hidden moderators/context;
- avoid premature closure.

### Example

Emerging finding:
social accountability improves exercise maintenance.

Disconfirming sampling:
seek participants with strong social accountability who nevertheless disengaged.

### Rule

Contradictory data are not noise to discard.

They can improve the explanation.

---

# 14. Negative/deviant case analysis

Negative-case analysis is an analytic quality tactic closely related to disconfirming sampling.

It asks:

> Which observations do not fit the current interpretation?

Possible responses:

- refine the category;
- split a category;
- add contextual conditions;
- reject the explanation.

### Critical rule

A theory/theme that explains only confirming cases is weaker than one refined against meaningful contradiction.

---

# 15. Snowball / chain-referral sampling

Snowball sampling recruits additional participants through referrals.

Useful when:

- population is hard to identify;
- trust matters;
- network access is important.

Potential examples:

- illicit enhancement-drug users;
- marginalized sport communities;
- informal training subcultures.

### Risks

- network homophily;
- overrepresentation of connected people;
- gatekeeper influence;
- hidden isolated cases.

### Critical rule

Snowballing is not automatically purposive depth.

Record:

- referral chain;
- eligibility filtering;
- whether referral created systematic network narrowing.

---

# 16. Convenience sampling

Convenience sampling selects cases because they are easy to access.

Examples:

- members available after one training session;
- students in researcher's own class;
- first clinic attendees agreeing to interview.

### Strength

Feasible.

### Risk

Accessibility replaces scientific information logic.

### Rule

Convenience sampling is not automatically invalid, but limitations must be explicit.

Do not relabel convenience sampling as purposive merely because inclusion criteria existed.

---

# 17. Volunteer / self-selection

Open calls can produce self-selection.

Likely overrepresentation:

- highly engaged people;
- people with strong positive/negative experiences;
- people comfortable discussing the topic.

### Fitness example

An open call asking "Tell us about problems in gym culture" may disproportionately attract people with salient negative experiences.

### Rule

Volunteer sampling affects the range of accounts available and should be recorded separately.

---

# 18. Key informants

Key informants have special contextual/cultural knowledge.

Examples:

- long-serving coach;
- senior physiotherapist;
- community organizer;
- gym manager.

### Critical rule

Expert knowledge ≠ representativeness.

A key informant can reveal system structure while still holding a partial position.

---

# 19. Theoretical sampling

Theoretical sampling belongs specifically to theory development, especially grounded theory.

Data collection is redirected by the emerging analysis.

Canonical cycle:

initial sample
→ analysis
→ conceptual gap
→ select next data/case
→ compare
→ refine category
→ repeat.

### Critical rule

Preplanned purposive sampling based on known demographics is not theoretical sampling.

### Another rule

Theoretical sampling is not:

"we recruited participants with relevant characteristics."

It requires emerging theory/categories to drive later sampling decisions.

---

# 20. Case/site/event/time sampling

Qualitative sampling often occurs simultaneously at multiple levels.

Example ethnography:

- site: two gyms;
- people: coaches and lifters;
- events: max-effort sessions;
- time: weekday evenings + weekend competitions;
- interactions: coach corrections.

### Project rule

Extract each level separately.

Person-level sampling alone may miss systematic temporal/site selection.

---

# 21. Sampling in case study

Case-study sampling has two levels:

1. selecting the case(s);
2. selecting evidence within the case.

Case selection can use:

- critical;
- typical;
- extreme;
- positive-deviant;
- polar/contrasting;
- replication logic.

Within-case data can then use:

- purposive participants;
- key informants;
- documents;
- events;
- metrics.

### Rule

Case N ≠ participant N.

---

# 22. Sampling in ethnography

Ethnographic sampling can include:

- sites;
- actors;
- activities;
- times;
- events;
- documents/artifacts.

Adequacy depends partly on whether fieldwork covers the social situations needed to understand the practice/culture.

### Critical rule

Interview count alone cannot establish ethnographic adequacy.

---

# 23. Sampling in narrative inquiry

Narrative sampling prioritizes access to relevant stories/trajectories.

Adequacy depends on:

- story depth;
- temporal span;
- narrative variation;
- individual vs collective claim;
- analytic tradition.

### Rule

Narrative inquiry does not require a generic saturation claim to justify every sample.

A single deep trajectory can be coherent for an individual narrative question.

---

# 24. Sampling in Conversation Analysis

CA typically samples interactional phenomena and builds collections of instances.

It asks:

- how does the practice work?;
- does the organization recur?;
- what variants and deviant cases exist?

### Critical rule

Generic "data saturation" is not a defining CA stopping rule.

Adequacy is tied to the phenomenon collection and interactional analysis.

---

# 25. Sample size: no universal qualitative N

Current JARS–Qual explicitly states there is no agreed minimum qualitative sample size.

Appropriate size depends on:

- question;
- methodology;
- sampling logic;
- data richness;
- heterogeneity;
- subgroup comparison;
- analysis depth;
- number of data sources;
- resources.

### Critical rule

Larger qualitative N is not automatically stronger.

Too many cases can reduce case-level depth when methodology is idiographic.

---

# 26. Planning N vs final N

At proposal stage, researchers often need an anticipated sample size.

A defensible plan can use:

- prior studies;
- empirical saturation studies;
- information power;
- methodology conventions;
- recruitment feasibility;
- expected attrition;
- planned subgroup structure.

But final adequacy should be reconsidered during data collection/analysis where methodology permits.

### Rule

A priori estimate ≠ proof that final data were adequate.

---

# 27. Empirical saturation ranges

Hennink & Kaiser 2022 systematically reviewed empirical tests of saturation.

For relatively homogeneous samples with narrowly defined objectives, many studies reached:

- 9–17 interviews;
- 4–8 focus-group discussions.

### Critical limitation

These are not universal sample-size prescriptions.

Most empirical studies assessed relatively narrow forms of saturation, often code-level saturation.

Broader, more heterogeneous, multi-country, subgroup-heavy or meaning-focused studies may require more.

---

# 28. Code saturation vs meaning saturation

Hennink, Kaiser & Marconi 2017 provide a crucial distinction.

## Code saturation

No substantial new issues/codes emerge.

Their empirical dataset reached code saturation around 9 interviews.

Meaning:

> heard the range of issues.

## Meaning saturation

Enough depth is collected to understand:

- dimensions;
- nuances;
- variation;
- complexity.

Their empirical dataset required roughly 16–24 interviews for many conceptual issues.

Meaning:

> understand the issues deeply.

### Critical rule

No-new-codes is not equivalent to deep understanding.

---

# 29. Focus-group saturation

Hennink et al. 2019 found in one empirical focus-group dataset:

- roughly four groups could identify a broad range of issues;
- more groups were needed for meaning saturation;
- stratification increased data requirements.

### Critical rule

The number of groups needed depends on:

- study purpose;
- type of code;
- subgroup strata;
- degree/type of saturation sought.

Do not apply "4 groups" generically.

---

# 30. Saturation is not one concept

Saunders et al. distinguish at least:

1. theoretical saturation;
2. inductive thematic saturation;
3. a priori thematic saturation;
4. data saturation.

### Theoretical saturation

Grounded theory:
categories no longer materially develop.

### Inductive thematic saturation

No new inductively derived themes/codes.

### A priori thematic saturation

Predefined categories sufficiently represented.

### Data saturation

General redundancy/no new information.

### Project rule

Every saturation claim must specify:

**what is being saturated?**

---

# 31. Saturation is not a binary magic point

Methodological critiques caution against treating saturation as a perfectly observable moment where knowledge suddenly becomes complete.

A more defensible framing can involve:

- diminishing returns;
- conceptual sufficiency;
- degree of saturation;
- information adequacy.

### Rule

"Data saturation was reached" without method is weak reporting.

Extract:

- definition;
- unit;
- operational rule;
- timing;
- evidence.

---

# 32. Guest–Namey–Chen thematic saturation method

Guest et al. 2020 proposed a transparent empirical procedure for inductive thematic saturation using:

- Base Size;
- Run Length;
- New Information Threshold.

### Value

Makes a particular thematic-saturation decision auditable.

### Limitation

It is not a universal algorithm for:

- phenomenology;
- narrative;
- CA;
- discourse analysis;
- ethnography;
- all thematic-analysis traditions.

### Rule

A quantified saturation procedure is valid only when aligned with the analytic framework.

---

# 33. Information Power

Malterud et al. propose:

> the more information power the sample holds for the actual study, the fewer participants may be needed.

Five factors:

1. study aim;
2. sample specificity;
3. use of established theory;
4. quality of dialogue;
5. analysis strategy.

### Direction of effect

Smaller samples may be defensible when:

- aim is narrow;
- sample is highly specific;
- theory strongly focuses inquiry;
- dialogue is rich;
- analysis is case-oriented/deep.

Larger samples may be needed when:

- aim is broad;
- population is heterogeneous;
- theory is weak/open;
- dialogue is thin;
- cross-case comparison is extensive.

---

# 34. Information Power is not statistical power

Information power is a conceptual model for qualitative sample adequacy.

It does not calculate:

- effect-size detection;
- Type I/II error;
- confidence intervals.

### Critical rule

Do not write:

"qualitative study was powered using information power"

as if it were a statistical power calculation.

Better:

"sample adequacy was guided by information power."

---

# 35. 2026 current synthesis

Roberts et al. 2026 reiterate that:

- quantitative power calculations do not apply to qualitative sample size in the same way;
- saturation is frequently under-operationalized;
- information power is useful for both planning and ongoing assessment.

The current 2026 saturation literature also explicitly warns against one-size-fits-all use.

### Project rule

For Stage 2:

saturation and information power are tools, not universal validity stamps.

---

# 36. Saturation may be inappropriate in some methodologies

## Phenomenology

van Manen explicitly argues generic data saturation does not fit phenomenological meaning because meaning is not a finite container to exhaust.

## Conversation Analysis

Adequacy concerns collections of interactional practices, variants and deviant cases rather than generic no-new-theme saturation.

## Reflexive/interpretive approaches

Some approaches reject an assumption that themes are finite objects waiting to be fully discovered.

### Critical rule

Do not penalize a study for failing to report saturation when its methodology provides a different defensible adequacy logic.

---

# 37. Phenomenology / IPA adequacy

Relevant factors:

- direct lived-experience eligibility;
- depth/richness;
- methodological tradition;
- idiographic commitment;
- experiential specificity;
- within-case analytic depth;
- cross-case convergence/divergence.

### IPA

Small, relatively homogeneous samples may be methodologically appropriate because detailed case-level analysis is central.

### Rule

A larger IPA sample can weaken idiographic depth if cases are reduced to generic themes.

---

# 38. Grounded Theory adequacy

GT adequacy is linked to:

- theoretical sampling;
- category development;
- properties/dimensions;
- category relationships;
- negative/contrasting cases;
- theoretical saturation or sufficiently developed theory.

### Critical rule

GT sample size is particularly difficult to fix completely a priori because emerging theory should guide later sampling.

No-new-interview-topic saturation is not equivalent to theoretical saturation.

---

# 39. Ethnography adequacy

Relevant factors include:

- field engagement;
- social situations observed;
- role/context variation;
- actor perspectives;
- temporal/event coverage;
- contextual depth;
- tacit-practice access;
- triangulation where appropriate.

### Rule

A fixed interview count cannot substitute for field adequacy.

---

# 40. Case-study adequacy

Relevant factors include:

- case-boundary clarity;
- case-selection rationale;
- evidence-source sufficiency;
- within-case depth;
- rival explanations;
- replication logic for multiple cases;
- cross-case coverage.

### Rule

More participants within one case do not compensate for poorly defined case boundaries.

---

# 41. Qualitative Description adequacy

QD may legitimately use:

- data/thematic saturation;
- meaning sufficiency;
- information power;
- pragmatic adequacy.

But the criterion should match the descriptive aim.

Example:

If the aim is:

> identify categories of barriers,

code saturation may be relatively relevant.

If the aim is:

> understand variation and nuance of those barriers,

meaning saturation / information power may be more appropriate.

---

# 42. Interpretive Description adequacy

ID adequacy should support:

- relevant variation;
- conceptual pattern development;
- practice significance;
- interpretive authority.

A superficial no-new-code criterion may be weaker than evidence of:

- variation explored;
- interpretations challenged;
- data richness;
- practice-relevant conceptual depth.

---

# 43. Narrative adequacy

Narrative adequacy depends on:

- narrative depth;
- temporal completeness;
- plot/identity material;
- analytic fit;
- claim level.

### Individual narrative

One or few cases can be coherent.

### Collective/master narrative claim

Requires broader evidence across persons/contexts/materials.

### Rule

Sample adequacy must follow the inferential level.

---

# 44. Discourse-analysis adequacy

Different traditions require different adequacy logic.

## CA

Collection of interactional instances and variants.

## DP/CDP

Discursive practices/repertoires across relevant contexts.

## FDA/CDA

Corpus/context sufficient to support discourse/power claims.

### Rule

"No new themes" is not a universal language-centered stopping criterion.

---

# 45. Longitudinal qualitative adequacy

Relevant dimensions include:

- number/timing of waves;
- continuity of cases;
- attrition;
- event coverage;
- trajectory depth;
- stability/change capture.

### Critical rule

More participants at baseline cannot substitute for missing temporal coverage.

Longitudinal sufficiency has a time dimension in addition to sample size.

---

# 46. Subgroups and stratification

Every planned subgroup can increase required information.

Example:

Study of exercise barriers across:

- men vs women;
- novice vs experienced;
- gym vs home;
- younger vs older.

If claims will be made within each stratum, each stratum needs sufficient evidence.

### Critical rule

A total sample of 20 does not mean four subgroups of 5 are automatically adequately understood.

Adequacy must be considered at the level of the claim.

---

# 47. Focus groups: number of groups vs participants

Focus-group adequacy has at least two levels:

1. participants within group;
2. number of groups / strata.

Interaction among participants is part of the data.

### Rule

Twenty-four people in one huge group are not methodologically equivalent to four groups of six.

The number of independent group discussions matters.

---

# 48. Data richness vs data quantity

Two samples with the same N can differ radically in information.

Example:

N=15:
- 15 five-minute interviews;

versus:

N=15:
- 15 ninety-minute in-depth interviews with highly specific experience.

### Rule

Participant count alone is a poor proxy for data adequacy.

Record:

- depth;
- duration;
- dialogue quality;
- source multiplicity;
- contextual specificity.

---

# 49. Heterogeneity increases information demands

Broader variation can require:

- more cases;
- more sampling strata;
- more field sites;
- more analytical comparisons.

But the goal is not maximum N.

The goal is enough data to understand the relevant variation.

### Rule

Heterogeneity should be intentional.

Unplanned heterogeneity can create a sample too diffuse for deep analysis.

---

# 50. Rare perspectives and low-frequency data

Hennink et al. showed high-frequency codes do not necessarily achieve meaning saturation faster than conceptually complex low-frequency issues.

### Critical rule

Frequency ≠ importance.

Rare perspectives can:

- challenge dominant interpretation;
- reveal boundary conditions;
- expose hidden barriers.

Do not stop sampling only because common themes repeat.

---

# 51. Sample composition vs sample size

Adequacy is two-dimensional:

1. who/what is included?;
2. how much data is included?

A large convenience sample can still omit critical perspectives.

A smaller strategically selected sample can sometimes provide stronger information for a narrow qualitative question.

### Rule

Always evaluate composition before N.

---

# 52. Probability sampling in qualitative research

Probability selection is not prohibited in qualitative work.

A qualitative study can draw interviewees from:

- random trial sample;
- population survey;
- randomly selected records.

But probability sampling does not turn qualitative findings into population estimates automatically.

### Rule

Inference follows:

- data type;
- analysis;
- measurement;
- response/nonresponse;
- sampling design.

Probability selection can improve coverage but does not make narrative/thematic frequencies equivalent to survey estimates.

---

# 53. Sampling from trials/cohorts

Qualitative sub-studies can purposively sample trial/cohort participants by:

- treatment arm;
- responder/nonresponder;
- adherence;
- age;
- site;
- outcome trajectory.

### Strength

Can target explanatory contrasts.

### Critical rule

If selection uses post-treatment outcome, qualitative comparison can illuminate experiences associated with different trajectories but does not independently establish causal moderators or mediators.

---

# 54. Sampling on outcome or trajectory

Examples:

- high vs low adherence;
- improved vs non-improved;
- injury recurrence vs no recurrence.

Useful for:

- contrast;
- mechanism hypotheses;
- process explanation.

### Risk

Retrospective explanations can be conditioned by outcome.

### Rule

Do not convert explanatory qualitative contrasts into quantitative causal effects.

---

# 55. Sampling plans can evolve

Iterative qualitative methodologies may intentionally revise sampling.

Reasons:

- new category emerges;
- missing subgroup identified;
- contradiction discovered;
- site variation matters;
- theoretical sampling needed.

### Critical rule

Changing the sample plan is not automatically protocol failure.

If methodologically justified, it can be a strength.

Report:

- what changed;
- why;
- when;
- based on what analysis.

---

# 56. Pragmatic constraints

Real studies face:

- recruitment difficulty;
- time;
- cost;
- safety;
- access;
- participant burden.

Pragmatic stopping is not the same as saturation.

### Rule

If data collection stopped because resources ended, say so.

Do not retroactively relabel resource exhaustion as saturation.

---

# 57. Ethical adequacy

Too small a sample can waste participant contribution if the question cannot be adequately answered.

Unnecessarily large qualitative samples can:

- burden participants;
- create unused data;
- consume resources;
- reduce analytic depth.

### Rule

"More participants" is not automatically more ethical or more rigorous.

---

# 58. Reporting standards

Current JARS–Qual expects researchers to report:

- participant/data-source selection process;
- inclusion/exclusion criteria;
- how participant number was determined;
- changes through attrition;
- rationale for halting data collection.

COREQ asks for:

- sampling method;
- sample size;
- non-participation;
- data saturation discussion;

for interview/focus-group studies.

SRQR similarly emphasizes transparent sampling and methodological rationale.

### Critical rule

Reporting checklists improve transparency.

# 62. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| purposive sampling vs one single technique | it is an umbrella logic |
| inclusion criteria vs purposive strategy | eligibility does not explain information logic |
| selection vs recruitment | who is needed differs from how they are reached |
| snowball referral vs theoretical sampling | network referral ≠ emerging-theory sampling |
| convenience vs purposive | access alone is not an information-rich rationale |
| homogeneous vs representative | deliberate narrowness is not population representation |
| maximum variation vs probability sampling | purposeful heterogeneity ≠ random coverage |
| typical case vs statistically average case | typicality may be analytic judgment |
| extreme case vs general prevalence | unusual case is intentionally non-typical |
| critical case vs interesting case | criticality requires explicit inferential logic |
| theoretical sampling vs predefined stratification | emerging theory must drive sampling |
| negative case vs bad data | contradiction can refine analysis |
| participant N vs case/site/event N | sampling occurs at multiple levels |
| larger N vs better qualitative study | depth and methodology matter |
| planned N vs final adequacy | a priori estimate is not proof |
| code saturation vs meaning saturation | heard issue ≠ understood issue |
| thematic saturation vs theoretical saturation | different scientific objects |
| saturation vs information power | different adequacy frameworks |
| saturation vs universal rigor criterion | relevance is methodology-dependent |
| 9–17 interviews vs universal rule | empirical range had narrow conditions |
| 4–8 focus groups vs universal rule | strata/meaning depth alter needs |
| information power vs statistical power | no effect-size/error calculation |
| no-new-codes vs complete knowledge | conceptual nuance may still be missing |
| frequency vs importance | rare data can be theoretically crucial |
| pragmatic stopping vs saturation | resource limit is a different reason |
| probability selection vs quantitative inference | analysis and measurement still determine claims |
| trial sampling vs randomized qualitative inference | qualitative sub-study does not inherit randomization |
| subgroup total N vs subgroup adequacy | each intended claim needs support |

# 65. Status after PASS-06D11

## Established

1. Qualitative sampling is information-driven, methodology-specific and claim-specific rather than a small-N version of probability sampling.
2. Sampling can target people, cases, sites, events, interactions, times and materials.
3. Selection logic must be separated from recruitment mechanism.
4. Purposive sampling is an umbrella requiring subtype/goal specification.
5. Criterion, maximum-variation, homogeneous, typical, intensity, extreme, critical and confirming/disconfirming strategies serve distinct purposes.
6. Snowball and convenience recruitment have different limitations and cannot be silently relabelled as theoretical or purposive sampling.
7. Theoretical sampling requires emerging analysis/theory to redirect data collection.
8. There is no universal qualitative N.
9. Empirical saturation ranges are conditional heuristics, not rules.
10. Code saturation and meaning saturation are materially different.
11. Saturation itself has multiple methodology-dependent forms.
12. Information power provides a non-statistical adequacy framework based on aim, sample specificity, theory, dialogue and analysis.
13. Phenomenology, IPA, GT, ethnography, case study, narrative, discourse/CA and longitudinal qualitative research require different adequacy logics.
14. Adequacy must be evaluated at the level of the intended subgroup/case/trajectory claim.
15. Pragmatic stopping must not be falsely reported as saturation.
