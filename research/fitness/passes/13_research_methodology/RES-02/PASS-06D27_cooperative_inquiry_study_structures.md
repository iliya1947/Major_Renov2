# 2. Canonical scientific target

Co-operative Inquiry asks:

**What can a group of people who share a concern, practice or lived situation learn by jointly designing an inquiry, acting within the phenomenon, reflecting together on their own and each other's experience, revising their ideas/actions, and cycling repeatedly between experience and reflection?**

The methodology aims not only to know **about** a phenomenon but to develop knowledge **with and through** those who live or enact it.

### Canonical project representation

shared inquiry question
+
all members as co-researchers
+
all members as co-subjects
+
joint design/management
+
experience/action
+
reflection/dialogue
+
extended epistemology
+
repeated inquiry cycles
+
practice/change/learning outcomes.

### Critical rule

Co-operative Inquiry is not simply:

> “a focus group where participants discuss their experience.”

Participants must have substantive influence over the inquiry and engage in action/experience between reflective phases.

---

# 3. Genealogy

Co-operative Inquiry was developed principally by:

- John Heron;
- Peter Reason.

It belongs to the broader family of:

- participatory inquiry;
- action research;
- human inquiry;
- second-person inquiry.

Current methodology describes it as a **qualitative, participatory methodology** that shifts research from:

> research about people

toward:

> research with people.

### Project rule

Classify CI as:

**distinct participatory/action-oriented qualitative methodology**

while preserving broader PAR/action-research parent relationships where explicitly claimed.

---

# 4. Participatory inquiry paradigm

Heron & Reason proposed a participatory worldview with:

- **subjective–objective ontology**;
- extended epistemology;
- cooperative methodology;
- practical knowing in the service of human flourishing.

### Key challenge to simple constructivism

Heron & Reason argued that purely constructivist accounts understate direct experiential knowing.

Their framework therefore treats reality/knowing as involving both:

- subjective participation;
- encounter with a world/other that is not reducible to private construction.

### Critical rule

Do not classify CI as merely:

> constructivist focus-group research.

Its epistemology is explicitly broader.

---

# 5. Co-researcher and co-subject

This is CI's defining role architecture.

Each inquiry member is:

## Co-researcher

During reflective/design phases, the person helps:

- frame questions;
- choose methods/actions;
- interpret experience;
- decide next cycles;
- draw conclusions.

## Co-subject

During action/experience phases, the same person:

- engages in the phenomenon/practice;
- experiments;
- observes;
- experiences consequences;
- records relevant material.

### Critical rule

The same people alternate between:

> inquiry governance

and

> lived/practical participation.

This is more demanding than ordinary participant involvement.

---

# 6. Co-researcher ≠ participant with extra consultation

A participant who:

- gives feedback on the interview guide;
- attends one advisory meeting;
- checks results;

is not automatically a CI co-researcher.

Canonical CI requires participants to be as fully involved as possible in research decisions concerning:

- content;
- method;
- action;
- interpretation.

### Project extraction

Record actual decision rights.

Do not infer CI fidelity from labels alone.

---

# 7. Co-subject ≠ passive data source

Co-subjects deliberately enter the action/experience being investigated.

Examples:

- clinicians test a new form of reflective practice;
- leaders try new communication actions;
- wheelchair users investigate mobility experiences in everyday contexts;
- practitioners experiment with alternative clinical approaches.

### Critical rule

CI data are not limited to what participants retrospectively say in meetings.

The action phase itself is an epistemic source.

---

# 8. Political participation

CI has a political participation dimension:

> people affected by the inquiry should influence decisions about the inquiry.

This includes:

- question;
- process;
- data generation;
- meaning;
- outputs.

### Critical rule

Political participation does not require everyone to perform identical tasks.

It requires substantive influence rather than researcher monopoly.

---

# 9. Epistemic participation

CI also has an epistemic participation dimension.

Participants are not merely:

> sources of data for expert analysts.

They participate in defining:

- what counts as relevant experience;
- what it means;
- how knowledge is represented;
- what can be acted upon.

### Project rule

Political and epistemic participation should be extracted separately.

A group can have voice in logistics but little role in interpretation, or vice versa.

---

# 10. Extended epistemology

Heron/Reason's CI uses four interdependent ways of knowing:

1. **experiential knowing**;
2. **presentational knowing**;
3. **propositional knowing**;
4. **practical knowing**.

These are not merely four data types.

They constitute a theory of how knowledge is generated and validated.

---

# 11. Experiential knowing

Experiential knowing arises through:

> direct encounter / lived contact with person, place, process, thing or activity.

It can include:

- embodied sense;
- relational experience;
- tacit understanding;
- immediate awareness.

### Critical rule

Experiential knowing is not equivalent to:

> opinion about an event.

It concerns first-hand participation/encounter.

---

# 12. Presentational knowing

Presentational knowing gives expressive form to experience.

Possible forms:

- story;
- image;
- drawing;
- metaphor;
- movement;
- music;
- enactment;
- other expressive representation.

### Scientific role

It can communicate patterns/qualities that are difficult to reduce immediately to abstract propositions.

### Critical rule

Presentational knowing ≠ decorative illustration.

It is a distinct epistemic mode.

---

# 13. Propositional knowing

Propositional knowing is conceptual knowing expressed through:

- ideas;
- statements;
- models;
- theories;
- explanations.

This is the mode most familiar to conventional academic research.

### CI distinction

Propositions should remain grounded in and congruent with:

- experiential;
- presentational;
- practical;

knowing.

---

# 14. Practical knowing

Practical knowing is:

> knowing how to do something.

It is expressed in:

- skill;
- competence;
- action;
- effective practice.

Heron places particular emphasis on the **primacy of practical knowing**.

### Scientific implication

CI can legitimately culminate in:

- improved practice;
- new skill;
- changed organizational action;

not only a conceptual paper.

---

# 15. Four ways of knowing are interdependent

Strong CI does not simply collect one instance of each knowing type.

The aim is **congruence and interaction** among them.

Example:

experience of rehabilitation-team isolation
→ story/image representing it
→ conceptual account of communication barriers
→ new collaborative practice
→ new experience
→ revised understanding.

### Critical rule

A four-column checklist is not enough.

The epistemic value lies in cycling among forms of knowing.

---

# 16. Four-phase inquiry cycle

Reason's canonical CI cycle can be represented as four phases.

## Phase 1 — reflection/design

Co-researchers:

- agree the area/question;
- develop propositions/ideas;
- decide actions/practices to explore;
- agree how experience will be observed/recorded.

## Phase 2 — action / co-subject experience

Members leave reflection and engage in the agreed actions/practices.

They:

- act;
- observe;
- record;
- notice self/others.

## Phase 3 — deeper immersion / experiential engagement

Members become more fully immersed in the action/experience.

This may:

- deepen original questions;
- disrupt prior assumptions;
- produce unexpected directions;
- temporarily draw attention away from formal inquiry awareness.

## Phase 4 — reflection / sense-making

The group reconvenes to:

- share experiential data;
- reconsider initial ideas;
- revise/reject propositions;
- redesign next action cycle.

### Rule

These phases are then repeated.

---

# 17. One cycle is usually insufficient for canonical CI

The value of CI comes from **research cycling**.

Cycle:

reflection
→ action/experience
→ immersion
→ reflection
→ revised action
→ new experience
→ further reflection.

### Scientific function

Repeated cycles allow:

- correction;
- deepening;
- testing in practice;
- theory–action interaction;
- skill development.

### Critical rule

A single workshop with discussion and no subsequent action cycle is generally closer to:

- consultation;
- focus group;
- co-design workshop;

than full CI.

---

# 18. Number and duration of cycles

There is no universal cycle count.

Foundational examples range from:

- multiple rapid workshop cycles;
- cycles lasting weeks;
- inquiries extending months/year.

A classic whole-person medicine inquiry ran for months.

The 2026 rehabilitation-hospital study used:

- six iterative cycles;
- 18 months.

### Rule

Adequacy depends on:

- question;
- practice;
- depth of change;
- repeated learning;

not a fixed number.

---

# 19. Action and reflection must both matter

CI explicitly warns against imbalance.

## Too much reflection

Risk:
> armchair theorizing.

## Too much action

Risk:
> activism without systematic inquiry.

### Validity function

The group needs an appropriate balance between:

- thinking;
- action;
- experience;
- reflection.

---

# 20. Divergence

During divergence, co-researchers may:

- explore different aspects;
- try different actions;
- pursue alternative interpretations;
- surface disagreement.

### Scientific value

Divergence broadens the inquiry and reduces premature closure.

### Critical rule

CI is not designed to force uniform experience.

Difference is epistemically useful.

---

# 21. Convergence

During convergence, the group may:

- focus repeatedly on one issue;
- refine a shared practice;
- test a developing proposition;
- examine a common concern in depth.

### Scientific value

Convergence can deepen and stabilize emerging knowledge.

### Rule

Good inquiry can alternate divergence and convergence according to what the phenomenon requires.

---

# 22. CI does not require consensus on findings

This is a critical distinction.

Co-operative Inquiry requires collaboration in:

- research design;
- action;
- reflection.

It does **not** require erasing genuine disagreement in order to produce one consensus conclusion.

Current methodology explicitly warns that CI should draw out diverse perspectives rather than merely develop consensus.

### Project rule

Store:

- shared conclusions;
- divergent conclusions;
- unresolved tensions.

Do not treat dissent as failed methodology.

---

# 23. Consensus collusion

Heron/Reason explicitly identify **consensus collusion** as a validity threat.

A group may agree because of:

- social pressure;
- politeness;
- hierarchy;
- shared blind spots;
- desire for harmony.

### Critical rule

Agreement is not automatically stronger evidence.

CI requires procedures that actively challenge comfortable consensus.

---

# 24. Unaware projection

Another classic validity threat is **unaware projection**:

> co-researchers may impose unexamined beliefs, fears or assumptions onto experience.

### Countermeasures

- repeated cycles;
- critical attention;
- alternative actions;
- disagreement;
- devil's advocacy;
- reflexive dialogue.

### Rule

Participatory inquiry does not become bias-free because everyone is involved.

---

# 25. Critical subjectivity

CI rejects both:

- naive objectivity;
- unexamined subjectivity.

A key goal is **critical subjectivity**:

> retaining living personal knowledge while developing capacity to inspect and challenge one's own beliefs/experience.

### Project rule

Do not translate CI rigor into:

> remove subjectivity.

The objective is disciplined participation.

---

# 26. Authentic collaboration

Foundational CI treats **authentic collaboration** as a validity issue.

A group is not authentically collaborative if:

- one or two people dominate;
- key voices are excluded;
- formal experts always determine conclusions;
- participants cannot challenge facilitators.

### Possible safeguards

- rotating leadership;
- speaking rounds;
- process reviews;
- shared decision procedures;
- explicit power checks.

---

# 27. Equal influence ≠ identical role

One member may know more about:

- methodology;
- clinical practice;
- lived experience;
- facilitation.

Canonical CI allows differential expertise.

### Critical rule

Expertise should serve the group's inquiry rather than become unilateral authority.

---

# 28. Facilitation

CI may use:

- appointed facilitator;
- rotating facilitator;
- distributed/self-organizing facilitation.

A facilitator can help:

- maintain inquiry structure;
- include quieter voices;
- manage conflict;
- monitor cycles;
- keep focus.

### Risk

Facilitator can become de facto principal investigator who controls:

- question;
- agenda;
- interpretation.

### Project extraction

Record:

- who facilitates;
- whether facilitation rotates;
- who can alter agenda;
- who can veto/change decisions.

---

# 29. Distributed leadership

Current methodology suggests distributed/facilitative leadership can support CI better than rigid hierarchy.

### Critical nuance

“Egalitarian” does not mean:

> absence of leadership.

Groups can fail through:

- domination;
- under-facilitation;
- chaos;
- diffusion of responsibility.

### Rule

Evaluate how leadership functions, not whether a leader exists.

---

# 30. Power asymmetry

Power can enter through:

- clinical hierarchy;
- academic status;
- funding;
- employment;
- lived-experience stigma;
- authorship control;
- institutional authority.

### Critical rule

Calling everyone “co-researcher” does not remove these asymmetries.

Strong CI addresses them explicitly.

---

# 31. Compensation and material participation

Meaningful CI may require:

- protected work time;
- payment;
- travel support;
- accessibility;
- technology;
- childcare;
- training.

### Rule

Formal equality without material capacity can produce unequal participation.

---

# 32. Authorship / dissemination

Current CI examples sometimes make all inquiry members:

- co-authors;
- co-presenters;
- dissemination decision-makers.

This is coherent with full participation but not universally feasible.

### Critical rule

Authorship is evidence of participation only when contributions genuinely meet authorship standards.

It should not be used as a ceremonial marker of equality.

---

# 33. Validity / quality in CI

Heron's validity framework is methodology-specific.

Common procedures include:

- research cycling;
- divergence and convergence;
- balance of reflection and action/experience;
- discriminating/critical awareness;
- falsification/challenge of favored interpretations;
- balance of chaos and order;
- management of unaware projections;
- authentic collaboration;
- open/closed inquiry boundaries;
- coherence in action;
- replication/variation across cycles or contexts.

### Critical rule

These are CI-specific quality resources.

They are not universal qualitative reporting requirements.

---

# 34. Research cycling as validity

Repeated cycles function as corrective feedback loops.

An idea can be:

- tried;
- experienced;
- questioned;
- modified;
- tried again.

### Scientific benefit

This provides a form of practical iterative testing unavailable to one-off interview studies.

### Rule

Cycles should demonstrate real learning/revision, not simply repeated meetings.

---

# 35. Falsification / challenge

CI can deliberately challenge a favored proposition by:

- trying the opposite;
- seeking disconfirming cases;
- using devil's advocate;
- testing whether a proposed practice fails.

### Critical boundary

This is not Popperian experimental falsification in a strict statistical sense.

It is structured participatory challenge to avoid self-confirmation.

---

# 36. Chaos and order

Inquiry needs enough structure to remain research, but enough openness to allow:

- unexpected experience;
- emergent issues;
- creativity.

### Too much order

Risk:
> researcher-controlled protocol suppresses participation.

### Too much chaos

Risk:
> inquiry loses focus and cannot produce accountable knowledge.

---

# 37. Open and closed boundaries

CI groups may need to decide:

- who is inside the inquiry group;
- whether new members can join;
- what information leaves the group;
- what can be public.

### Scientific/ethical importance

Membership changes can alter:

- trust;
- group dynamics;
- data continuity;
- power.

### Rule

Boundary decisions are part of inquiry governance.

---

# 38. Distress management

Foundational guidance recognizes that inquiry into lived/professional experience may uncover:

- grief;
- anger;
- fear;
- conflict;
- shame.

### Critical rule

Distress is not merely an ethics side issue.

Unaddressed distress can distort inquiry through avoidance or group defensiveness.

Strong CI should have processes for:

- emotional safety;
- pausing;
- support;
- referral;
- voluntary withdrawal.

---

# 39. Data in CI

Potential material includes:

- reflection notes;
- journals;
- observations;
- action logs;
- meeting notes;
- recordings;
- drawings/art;
- models;
- action plans;
- feedback loops;
- practice outcomes.

### Critical rule

CI data are often distributed across the whole inquiry process.

A transcript of final meetings alone may not represent the actual epistemic corpus.

---

# 40. Analysis in CI

Analysis is not necessarily a separate researcher-only stage.

It occurs through:

- collective reflection;
- dialogue;
- comparison of experience;
- practical testing;
- conceptual refinement;
- presentational expression.

A later formal thematic or other analysis may also be used.

### Project rule

Store separately:

1. CI participatory sense-making;
2. downstream formal analysis method.

Do not let researcher-only thematic analysis erase the co-inquiry layer.

---

# 41. Action is epistemic, not merely implementation

In CI, action serves as a way to know.

A group may test:

- a new clinical practice;
- a communication routine;
- a leadership action;
- a mobility strategy.

The resulting experience informs further inquiry.

### Critical rule

Action is not simply:

> “apply findings after research.”

It is integrated into knowledge production.

---

# 42. Change may occur during the study

Because CI combines inquiry and action, participants/practice may change before final reporting.

Possible outcomes:

- new skill;
- changed relationship;
- changed service process;
- new organizational practice;
- changed self-understanding.

### Critical boundary

Observed change within CI is not automatically attributable causally to a specific intervention component.

No randomization/control is implied.

---

# 43. Practical knowing as outcome

CI can legitimately claim:

> the group learned how to do X differently.

This may be stronger than merely:

> participants said X was useful.

### Evidence can include

- enacted practice;
- repeated action cycles;
- observed competence;
- organizational change.

### Rule

Still distinguish qualitative/practical demonstration from quantified effectiveness.

---

# 44. CI vs generic action research

## Action research

Broad family:
- inquiry;
- action;
- reflection;
- change.

## Co-operative Inquiry

More specific:
- all members as co-researchers/co-subjects;
- extended epistemology;
- participatory inquiry paradigm;
- repeated experiential/reflection cycles;
- methodology-specific validity procedures.

### Rule

CI is a distinct action-research methodology, not a synonym for all action research.

---

# 45. CI vs Participatory Action Research (PAR)

Both emphasize:

- participation;
- action;
- power;
- reflection.

PAR is broader and often emphasizes:

- collective social change;
- marginalized communities;
- emancipatory/political transformation.

CI has a more specific epistemic architecture:

- co-researcher/co-subject roles;
- four ways of knowing;
- four-phase cycles;
- explicit CI validity procedures.

### Project rule

A study can be:

PAR parent
+
Co-operative Inquiry method/architecture.

Preserve both if explicitly present.

---

# 46. CI vs CBPR

CBPR is a broad community-partnership governance approach.

It can involve:

- organizations;
- community representatives;
- researchers;
- multiple study methods.

CI normally centers a more bounded inquiry group whose members directly participate as:

- co-researchers;
- co-subjects.

### Critical rule

Community partnership ≠ CI unless members themselves cycle through inquiry/action/reflection.

---

# 47. CI vs co-design

Co-design focuses on jointly designing:

- intervention;
- service;
- product;
- process.

CI may produce designs but its epistemic scope is broader.

It can investigate:

- lived experience;
- professional practice;
- theory;
- organization;
- skill.

### Rule

A co-design workshop with shared creation is not automatically CI.

Repeated inquiry cycles and co-subject action are needed.

---

# 48. CI vs co-production

Co-production can describe broad shared power/responsibility across research/service creation.

CI is one methodology that can enact co-production strongly.

### Critical rule

Co-production is a governance/partnership architecture.

CI adds:

- inquiry cycles;
- epistemology;
- methodological validity procedures.

---

# 49. CI vs focus group

## Focus group

Participants discuss a topic.

Researcher generally controls:

- question;
- facilitation;
- analysis.

## CI

Members:

- co-design the inquiry;
- act/experiment between meetings;
- collect/interpret experience;
- revise the inquiry.

### Rule

Repeated focus groups alone ≠ Co-operative Inquiry.

---

# 50. CI vs peer support / community of practice

A group may:

- learn together;
- reflect;
- support each other.

That is not automatically research.

CI additionally requires:

- explicit inquiry question;
- systematic cycles;
- agreed observation/recording;
- critical reflection;
- knowledge claims/output.

### Rule

Learning community ≠ research methodology unless inquiry architecture is explicit.

---

# 51. CI vs PPI advisory group

PPI groups may advise a research team.

In CI, inquiry members are themselves researchers and subjects of inquiry.

### Rule

Advisory participation is structurally shallower than canonical CI.

---

# 52. CI vs heuristic inquiry

Both value:

- direct experience;
- reflection.

But:

## Heuristic inquiry

Primary researcher has personal experience and leads phenomenological self/other inquiry.

## CI

All inquiry members jointly govern inquiry and enter action/experience cycles as co-subjects.

### Rule

Shared lived experience alone does not make heuristic research CI, nor CI heuristic.

---

# 53. CI vs CQR

CQR has:

- analysts;
- participant cases;
- consensus team;
- auditor.

CI dissolves the conventional participant/analyst divide much more radically.

### Critical rule

Analyst consensus in CQR ≠ participatory co-inquiry.

In CI, those living the phenomenon participate in knowledge production.

---

# 54. Claim architecture

CI can support claims about:

- lived/practical experience;
- collectively generated understanding;
- changes in professional/practical knowing;
- how action-reflection changes understanding;
- locally enacted practice improvements;
- group learning;
- relational/organizational change;
- co-created propositions/models.

### Critical rule

The strongest claims often concern:

> this group's inquiry and the practices it enacted.

Broader claims require additional evidence/transferability argument.

---

# 55. Claims CI cannot establish by itself

CI alone does not establish:

- population prevalence;
- incidence;
- representative population proportions;
- randomized causal effect;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism;
- causal mediation.

### Important boundary

If a CI group reports:

> reflective meetings improved leadership,

that is an experiential/practical explanatory claim.

It is not a controlled causal effect estimate.

---

# 56. Generalization / transferability

CI often produces:

- situated practical knowledge;
- transferable process insight;
- practice models.

Its generalization is usually:

- analytic;
- experiential;
- practical;
- transferable by contextual fit.

### Rule

Small inquiry groups do not estimate population frequencies.

---

# 57. Sampling

CI typically recruits people who:

- share the issue/practice;
- can act within it;
- can participate repeatedly;
- are willing to reflect collaboratively.

### Important distinction

Sampling concerns not only:

> who has experience?

but also:

> who can meaningfully co-govern and sustain an inquiry?

### Project extraction

Record:

- inclusion by lived/practice relevance;
- power relationships;
- capacity to participate;
- duration/retention.

---

# 58. Group size

There is no universal N.

Practical constraints include:

- group dialogue;
- shared decision-making;
- action coordination;
- relationship depth.

Current examples range from small professional groups to sessions with variable attendance.

### Rule

Do not apply generic qualitative sample-size thresholds.

Adequacy concerns inquiry functioning and relevant experience.

---

# 59. Attendance/attrition

Long CI can face:

- changing availability;
- role transitions;
- burnout;
- employment changes.

### Scientific consequence

Attrition can change:

- power;
- memory of previous cycles;
- continuity;
- group interpretation.

### Project extraction

Record:

- attendance by cycle;
- membership changes;
- decisions after attrition.

---

# 60. 2026 rehabilitation-hospital exemplar

Carroll et al. conducted a cooperative inquiry with senior medical professionals in an Irish national rehabilitation hospital.

Architecture:

- 18 months;
- six iterative cycles;
- 4–12 participants per session;
- structured reflection;
- collective dialogue;
- co-designed actions;
- meeting notes;
- reflective journals;
- action plans / feedback loops.

Reported actions included:

- new communication platforms;
- embedded reflective practice;
- engagement with executive structures;
- governance innovations.

### Methodological lesson

This is a direct current rehabilitation example of:

reflection
→ collective learning
→ action
→ organizational feedback
→ further inquiry.

### Claim boundary

The study supports a situated account of adaptive/relational leadership development.

It does not isolate the causal effect of CI versus other concurrent organizational changes.

---

# 61. Wheelchair/community-mobility exemplar

A 2024 co-operative inquiry studied community mobility experiences of wheelchair users.

The process included:

- orientation to CI roles;
- informed consent;

|---|---|

| subjective–objective worldview vs simple constructivism | Heron/Reason explicitly extend beyond it |
| experiential knowing vs opinion | direct encounter/experience |
| presentational knowing vs illustration | distinct expressive epistemic mode |
| propositional knowing vs whole knowledge | only one of four modes |
| practical knowing vs implementation after research | knowing-how is an inquiry outcome |
| four ways of knowing vs four phases | epistemology vs process |
| action vs implementation-only | action generates knowledge |
| reflection vs discussion-only | must connect to action/experience |
| one workshop vs full CI | repeated cycling is central |
| divergence vs failed consensus | difference can strengthen inquiry |
| consensus vs validity | agreement can become consensus collusion |
| authentic collaboration vs equal task assignment | equal influence can coexist with differentiated expertise |
| facilitator vs principal authority | facilitation should serve group inquiry |

| participant label “co-researcher” vs actual power | decision rights must be evidenced |

# 69. Adequacy / stopping logic

There is no universal:

- participant N;
- meeting N;
- cycle N.

A strong stopping decision asks whether:

- enough cycles occurred for ideas/actions to be tested and revised;
- experiential/propositional/practical knowledge became sufficiently integrated;
- major disagreements were explored;
- further cycling was producing diminishing useful change;
- the group judged the inquiry question sufficiently addressed.

### Critical rule

Generic thematic saturation is not the defining CI stopping criterion.
