## 2. Axial skeleton — project scope

The full axial skeleton includes:

- skull;
- vertebral column;
- ribs;
- sternum.

For this fitness project, ANA-03 focuses on:

1. vertebral column;
2. sacrum/coccyx;
3. thoracic cage.

Detailed skull anatomy is not a current dependency.

---

## 3. Vertebral column regions

Typical adult regional organization:

1. **7 cervical vertebrae** — C1–C7;
2. **12 thoracic vertebrae** — T1–T12;
3. **5 lumbar vertebrae** — L1–L5;
4. **5 sacral segments**, usually fused;
5. **approximately 4 coccygeal segments**, with variation.

Common textbook total:
**33 vertebral segments**.

Project rule:

This is the standard pattern, **not a guarantee that every person has identical segmentation**.

Transitional vertebrae and other variants exist and are handled as normal/anatomical variation unless clinical context says otherwise.

---

## 4. Core structural functions of the vertebral column

The vertebral column:

1. supports head and trunk;
2. transmits load toward the pelvis;
3. protects the spinal cord and neural structures;
4. provides muscle/ligament attachment;
5. permits distributed movement across multiple motion segments;
6. anchors the thoracic cage.

Project model:

**spine = segmented load-bearing/mobile system**, not one rigid rod and not merely a stack of independent blocks.

---

## 5. Typical vertebra

A typical vertebra contains:

1. vertebral body;
2. vertebral arch;
3. pedicles;
4. laminae;
5. spinous process;
6. two transverse processes;
7. superior and inferior articular processes;
8. vertebral foramen.

The body and arch together enclose the vertebral foramen.

Across stacked vertebrae, the foramina form the vertebral canal.

Intervertebral foramina form between adjacent vertebrae and provide exit pathways for spinal nerves/roots.

---

## 6. Vertebral body and posterior elements

### Vertebral body

Major anterior load-bearing osseous component.

General regional trend:
vertebral bodies enlarge toward the lumbar region as load-bearing demands increase.

### Posterior elements

Pedicles, laminae and processes:

- complete the neural arch;
- provide muscle/ligament attachment;
- form facet articulations;
- contribute lever arms for segmental motion.

---

## 7. Zygapophyseal / facet joints

Superior/inferior articular processes form paired **zygapophyseal (facet) joints** between adjacent vertebrae.

These are synovial joints.

Structural roles:

1. guide segmental movement;
2. constrain translation/rotation;
3. share load with the disc/vertebral bodies;
4. contribute to region-specific motion pattern.

Facet orientation differs by region.

Project rule:

**facet orientation influences movement tendencies; it does not create a universal personal ROM prediction.**

---

## 8. Intervertebral discs

Current NCBI reference anatomy confirms **23 adult intervertebral discs**:

- 6 cervical;
- 12 thoracic;
- 5 lumbar.

No disc:
- occiput–C1;
- C1–C2;
- within the fused sacrum.

Most caudal mobile disc:
**L5–S1**.

---

## 9. Disc structural components

Core:

1. **nucleus pulposus**;
2. **annulus fibrosus**;
3. **cartilaginous endplates**.

### Nucleus pulposus

Hydrated proteoglycan-rich central region.

### Annulus fibrosus

Concentric lamellar collagenous/fibrocartilaginous structure surrounding the nucleus.

Alternating collagen fiber orientations allow multidirectional tensile support.

### Cartilaginous endplates

Interfaces between disc and adjacent vertebral bodies.

Important:
vertebral endplate architecture is not the same thing as ordinary appendicular articular cartilage.

---

## 10. Disc function

Intervertebral discs contribute to:

1. mobility between vertebral bodies;
2. axial load distribution;
3. compression resistance;
4. maintenance of intervertebral spacing;
5. continuity of the spinal column during movement.

Project rule:

**"shock absorber" is incomplete.**

The disc is a deformable load-bearing joint component integrated with facet joints, ligaments and vertebral bodies.

---

## 11. Functional spinal motion segment

Useful structural unit:

**two adjacent vertebrae + intervertebral disc + bilateral facet joints + associated ligamentous/capsular structures**.

This concept matters because load and movement are shared among multiple structures.

Project consequence:

Do not analyze the spine as if:
- disc alone carries the whole load;
- facets are irrelevant;
- each vertebra moves independently of adjacent tissue constraints.

---

## 12. Major spinal ligaments

### Longitudinal

- anterior longitudinal ligament;
- posterior longitudinal ligament.

### Posterior/segmental

- ligamenta flava;
- interspinous ligaments;
- supraspinous ligament;
- intertransverse ligaments;
- ligamentum nuchae in cervical region.

These structures contribute passive constraint/stability.

Project rule:

Do not reduce each ligament to a single "stops flexion" or "stops extension" role; geometry and segmental context matter.

Detailed tension mechanics → MEC.

---

## 13. Regional curvatures

Normal adult sagittal profile includes:

- cervical lordotic curvature;
- thoracic kyphotic curvature;
- lumbar lordotic curvature;
- sacral kyphotic curvature.

Critical project rule:

**lordosis and kyphosis are anatomical curvature terms, not diagnoses by themselves.**

"Neutral spine" is therefore **not anatomically straight**.

---

## 14. Normal alignment is variable

This pass strengthens the legacy finding.

A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of >35,900 asymptomatic adults found meaningful variation in thoracic, lumbar, pelvic and global sagittal parameters with age, sex and population.

A 2026 global meta-analysis likewise emphasizes substantial heterogeneity in asymptomatic spinopelvic alignment.

A 2026 Journal of Anatomy study specifically reports physiological regional lumbar/lumbosacral variation in healthy adults.

Project rule:

**There is no single universal anatomical sagittal angle that defines "correct neutral posture" for every healthy person.**

This does not mean alignment never matters.

It means:
- anatomical variation is normal;
- posture/alignment thresholds must be question-specific;
- technique recommendations cannot be inferred from one atlas image.

Clinical deformity thresholds → MED.
Exercise-specific mechanics → MEC/TEC.

## 15. Cervical region

7 cervical vertebrae.

### C1 — atlas

Structurally atypical:
- ring-like;
- lacks a typical vertebral body.

### C2 — axis

Has the dens/odontoid process, forming a pivot relationship with atlas.

Therefore:
upper cervical anatomy differs substantially from the typical subaxial vertebral model.

Detailed cervical mechanics is not a current strength-programming dependency.

---

## 16. Thoracic region

12 thoracic vertebrae.

Distinctive features:
- rib articulations;
- thoracic cage integration;
- region-specific facet/process morphology.

Thoracic movement is influenced by:
- discs;
- facet joints;
- ribs;
- costovertebral/costotransverse articulations;
- sternocostal/costal cartilage architecture.

Project rule:

Do not interpret thoracic spine mobility in isolation from the thoracic cage.

---

## 17. Lumbar region

Typically 5 lumbar vertebrae.

Features:
- relatively large bodies;
- robust posterior elements;
- major role in axial load transfer;
- region-specific facet orientation.

General structural tendency:
lumbar architecture permits substantial flexion/extension and comparatively less axial rotation than cervical/thoracic regions.

Project prohibition:

**"lumbar spine cannot rotate" is anatomically false.**

Relative available rotation is lower; it is not zero.

Exact ROM → MEC/TST.

---

## 18. Sacrum and coccyx

### Sacrum

Usually 5 fused sacral segments.

Functions:
- forms posterior pelvic ring;
- transfers load between spine and pelvis;
- articulates with ilia at sacroiliac joints;
- forms lumbosacral junction with L5.

The sacrum is the major structural bridge between axial spine and pelvic ring.

### Coccyx

Terminal variable fused/partly fused segments.

Attachment site for:
- pelvic-floor structures;
- ligaments;
- local connective tissues.

Low direct programming relevance but part of axial anatomy.

---

## 19. Lumbosacral / spinopelvic boundary

L5–S1 is the transition between:
- mobile lumbar spine;
- sacrum/pelvis.

Current alignment research reinforces that lumbar and pelvic alignment parameters covary and vary between healthy individuals.

Project rule:

**lumbar position and pelvic position are related but not synonymous.**

Do not describe pelvic tilt as if it were identical to lumbar flexion/extension.

Detailed lumbopelvic mechanics → MEC/ANA regional work.

---

## 20. Thoracic cage

Major components:

1. 12 thoracic vertebrae;
2. ribs;
3. costal cartilages;
4. sternum.

Roles:
- protects thoracic organs;
- creates respiratory framework;
- provides attachment for trunk/shoulder muscles;
- supplies the 3D surface on which the scapula moves;
- contributes to trunk stiffness and mobility.

---

## 21. Ribs

Typical rib anatomy includes:
- head;
- neck;
- tubercle;
- body/shaft;
- angle;
- costal groove.

Ribs are curved/torsioned 3D bones, not simple hoops.

---

## 22. Rib classification — standard pattern and variation

Classical pattern:

### True ribs
1–7:
direct costal-cartilage connection to sternum.

### False ribs
8–10:
classically indirect anterior connection through the costal margin.

### Floating ribs
11–12:
no anterior sternal attachment.

### Important current nuance

The 2026 NCBI rib anatomy reference explicitly notes that the 10th rib may lack the classical anterior connection.

A 2026 CT-based anatomical study of 278 patients reported substantial variability in:
- rib number;
- number of true ribs;
- costal cartilage attachment;
- side-to-side symmetry.

In that sample:
- 11 rib pairs occurred in 3.5%;
- 13 pairs in 1%;
- 72% showed some right–left rib-cage asymmetry.

These numbers are sample-specific, not universal prevalence estimates.

Project rule:

**rib-cage atlas symmetry is a reference model, not an assumption about every person.**

---

## 23. Posterior rib articulations

Most typical ribs articulate posteriorly through:

1. costovertebral joint(s);
2. costotransverse joint.

Atypical ribs differ.

These are small but functional synovial articulations contributing to coordinated thoracic/rib movement.

Detailed breathing mechanics → PHY/MEC.

---

## 24. Costal cartilage

Hyaline cartilage extends ribs anteriorly.

Functions structurally:
- provides flexible rib–sternum/costal-margin connection;
- contributes thoracic compliance;
- preserves continuity while allowing movement.

Costal cartilage also shows meaningful anatomical variation.

---

## 25. Sternum

Main parts:

1. manubrium;
2. body;
3. xiphoid process.

Important landmarks:
- jugular notch;
- clavicular notches;
- sternal angle;
- costal notches.

The clavicular notches form part of the sternoclavicular articulation.

---

## 26. Axial–upper-limb structural connection

The clavicle connects the appendicular upper limb to axial skeleton through the **sternoclavicular joint**.

The scapula does not form a conventional bony joint with the thoracic cage; the scapulothoracic articulation is a functional gliding relationship.

Therefore upper-body force/motion is structurally linked to the axial skeleton through:

1. sternoclavicular joint;
2. acromioclavicular relationship;
3. scapulothoracic interface;
4. muscular/connective-tissue attachments.

Detailed shoulder architecture → ANA-04.

---

## 27. Thoracic cage and scapular geometry

The scapula rests and moves over the posterolateral thoracic wall.

Therefore thoracic cage shape/orientation constrains the geometric surface on which scapular motion occurs.

But:

**this does not imply one universally correct thoracic posture for shoulder function.**

Current scapulothoracic literature describes a complex 3D relationship rather than a single static ideal.

Detailed interaction → ANA-04/MEC.

---

## 28. Thoracic cage and breathing

Rib, sternum and thoracic vertebral motion contribute to changes in thoracic volume.

Traditional teaching terms include:
- pump-handle;
- bucket-handle.

These are useful descriptive simplifications.

Actual rib motion is:
- 3D;
- level-dependent;
- coupled to surrounding structures.

Detailed respiratory mechanics → PHY/MEC.

---

## 29. Anatomical variation

Normal variation can include:

- vertebral morphology;
- transitional vertebrae;
- facet orientation;
- sagittal alignment;
- rib number;
- costal cartilage attachment;
- rib-cage asymmetry;
- sacral/coccygeal segmentation.

Project rule:

**variation ≠ pathology.**

Do not infer diagnosis from:
- posture photo;
- asymmetric rib contour;
- curve magnitude alone;
- an atlas mismatch.

Clinical significance requires MED/TST context.

## 30. Anatomy vs biomechanics vs pathology

### Anatomy
- structures;
- regional organization;
- articulations;
- normal variation.

### Biomechanics
- load sharing;
- moments;
- stress/strain;
- stiffness;
- segmental movement;
- exercise-specific spinal/rib mechanics.

### Medicine
- disc degeneration/herniation;
- scoliosis;
- spondylolysis;
- fracture;
- neurological compromise;
- pain diagnosis.

No pathology is inferred from anatomy alone.

## 32. Established findings

### HIGH

1. Vertebral column is a segmented load-bearing/mobile system.
2. Standard regional organization is 7 cervical / 12 thoracic / 5 lumbar vertebrae plus fused sacral and variable coccygeal segments.
3. Typical vertebra contains body, arch, processes and vertebral foramen.
4. Facet joints are paired synovial joints guiding/constraining segmental motion.
5. Adult spine normally has 23 intervertebral discs, none at C0–C1 or C1–C2.
6. Disc comprises nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus and cartilaginous endplates.
7. Spinal load/motion is distributed across disc, facets, ligaments and muscular structures.
8. Normal sagittal spine includes regional lordotic/kyphotic curves; straightness is not neutral anatomy.
9. Thoracic cage comprises thoracic vertebrae, ribs, costal cartilages and sternum.
10. Rib/costal-cartilage anatomy shows meaningful normal variation.
11. Sacrum provides the principal structural bridge between vertebral column and pelvic ring.
12. Scapulothoracic motion occurs over the thoracic wall rather than through a true scapula–rib bony joint.

### MODERATE / INDIVIDUAL-VARIATION DEPENDENT

1. Exact regional motion capacity cannot be predicted from textbook facet orientation alone.
2. Exact sagittal alignment and rib-cage geometry vary substantially among asymptomatic adults.
3. Static alignment alone cannot determine optimal exercise technique.
