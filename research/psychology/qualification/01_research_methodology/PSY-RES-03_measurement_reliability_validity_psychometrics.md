# PSY-RES-03 — Measurement, reliability, validity, and psychometrics

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Establish the minimum psychometric framework required before psychological scores, ratings, tasks, questionnaires, classifications, or repeated measurements are used as evidence about a construct or an individual.

This block is not a complete psychometrics course. Advanced item-response theory, test construction, factor modeling, and formal differential-item-functioning methods can be deepened later in the dedicated psychometrics domain.

## Core rule

**A measurement result is evidence only through a justified interpretation.**

Do not reason as:

`test -> score -> psychological fact`.

Use:

`construct -> operationalization -> measurement procedure -> score -> precision/error -> validity evidence -> population/context -> interpretation -> conclusion`.

## 1. Test, measure, score, and construct are different objects

- **Construct:** the theoretical attribute/process of interest.
- **Measure/test/task:** the procedure used to obtain observations.
- **Score:** the numerical or categorical result generated under a scoring rule.
- **Interpretation:** the meaning assigned to that score for a specified purpose.

A score is not the construct itself.

A behavioral task, questionnaire, interview rating, or physiological measure may sample only one part of a broader construct and may also be influenced by nuisance factors.

## 2. Validity applies to interpretations and uses

The AERA/APA/NCME Standards treat validity as a unitary concept: the degree to which accumulated evidence and theory support the intended interpretation of test scores for a proposed use.

Therefore avoid:

`this test is valid`.

Prefer:

`there is sufficient / limited / insufficient validity evidence for interpreting these scores in this way, in this population, for this purpose`.

Different intended interpretations can require different evidence.

## 3. Major sources of validity evidence

Use the Standards' evidence framework.

### 3.1 Evidence based on test content

Ask whether the items/tasks adequately represent the intended domain.

Check:

- relevance;
- coverage;
- omissions;
- wording and task demands;
- whether irrelevant skills dominate performance.

### 3.2 Evidence based on response processes

Ask whether respondents, raters, or scorers actually use the psychological/cognitive processes assumed by the interpretation.

Examples of threats:

- misunderstanding items;
- strategic responding;
- guessing;
- memory strategies not intended by the task;
- rater heuristics;
- differential interpretation of wording.

### 3.3 Evidence based on internal structure

Ask whether relations among items/subscales support the proposed scoring structure.

Possible evidence includes:

- factor structure;
- dimensionality;
- IRT/Rasch fit;
- local dependence;
- expected relations among subscales.

A total score should not be interpreted as one dimension merely because items correlate.

### 3.4 Evidence based on relations to other variables

Check predicted convergent, discriminant, criterion, known-groups, prospective, or nomological relations.

The key is **a priori theoretical prediction**, not merely finding some significant correlation after looking at the data.

### 3.5 Evidence concerning consequences and fairness

Use consequences carefully.

A harmful or unequal consequence does not automatically prove psychometric invalidity, and a beneficial consequence does not prove validity.

But score use should be examined for:

- systematic bias;
- fairness;
- accessibility;
- inappropriate differential consequences;
- unsupported decisions.

## 4. Reliability / precision

Reliability concerns consistency or precision of scores under relevant replications.

Important forms include:

### 4.1 Test-retest reliability

Relevant when the construct is expected to remain sufficiently stable across the retest interval.

Low retest association can reflect:

- measurement error;
- real change;
- state dependence;
- memory/practice effects;
- changed testing conditions.

Therefore low test-retest reliability is not always an instrument defect; the expected temporal behavior of the construct matters.

### 4.2 Interrater reliability

Relevant when scoring or observation depends on raters.

Evaluate:

- rater training;
- scoring rules;
- whether raters are independent;
- whether reliability generalizes to operational raters rather than only expert developers.

### 4.3 Internal consistency

Internal consistency concerns interrelatedness among items within a scale.

It is **not** the same as unidimensionality.

A high Cronbach alpha does not prove that:

- the scale measures one construct;
- the construct is validly measured;
- scores are stable over time;
- the scale is unbiased;
- individual score interpretation is precise.

Unidimensionality / appropriate structural validity should be established before interpreting a single internal-consistency coefficient as evidence about one scale.

Alpha or omega should be reported for each meaningful unidimensional scale/subscale rather than used as a universal quality stamp.

### 4.4 Reliability depends on population and conditions

Reliability coefficients can differ when:

- score variance differs across samples;
- test administration changes;
- raters differ;
- language or familiarity differs;
- population characteristics alter error sources.

Do not import a reliability coefficient from one population as if it were a fixed property of the test.

## 5. Measurement error and standard error of measurement

Reliability coefficients describe relative precision across people but can hide how uncertain an individual's score is.

For individual interpretation, inspect where available:

- standard error of measurement (SEM);
- conditional SEM at the relevant score level;
- confidence/error bands;
- smallest detectable change / difference where appropriate.

A point score should not be treated as infinitely precise.

Canonical rule:

**If the uncertainty interval materially overlaps competing interpretations, the categorical conclusion should be weakened.**

## 6. Change and difference scores need their own evidence

A reliable baseline score does not guarantee that a **change score** or **difference score** is reliable.

When interpreting:

- pre-post change;
- profile discrepancies;
- differences between subtests;
- longitudinal growth;

evaluate:

- standard error of the difference/change;
- practice/retest effects;
- regression to the mean;
- form equivalence;
- interval length;
- expected real change;
- relevant reliability for the difference metric.

Do not declare a meaningful psychological change merely because the raw number changed.

## 7. Structural validity

Before using a composite or subscale score, ask:

1. What dimensional structure is claimed?
2. Was that structure tested in a relevant population?
3. Are items sufficiently coherent within each proposed dimension?
4. Are factors/subscales empirically distinguishable?
5. Was the model specified a priori or found after exploratory searching?
6. Does the scoring rule match the supported structure?

A scale can have high internal consistency while still having an invalid or ambiguous structure.

## 8. Measurement invariance and differential item functioning

Comparisons across groups, languages, contexts, or time require evidence that the measurement system behaves comparably enough for the intended comparison.

Potential noninvariance means the same observed score may not represent the same latent standing in different groups or occasions.

Relevant approaches include:

- multi-group confirmatory factor analysis;
- longitudinal invariance analysis;
- differential item functioning (DIF);
- IRT/Rasch approaches.

Important distinction:

**Observed mean differences do not themselves prove bias.**

DIF/invariance analysis asks whether item/measurement behavior differs after accounting for the underlying construct.

If meaningful invariance fails, group or longitudinal score comparisons may be uninterpretable or require restricted interpretation.

## 9. Norms and reference groups

Norm-referenced interpretation depends on the comparison group.

Before interpreting a percentile, standard score, or "high/low" classification ask:

1. Who was in the norm sample?
2. How large and representative was it?
3. When were norms collected?
4. Which language/version/mode was used?
5. Are age, education, culture, setting, and other relevant variables sufficiently represented?
6. Was administration equivalent to the norming procedure?

A percentile is not an absolute psychological property.

It describes relative standing against a reference distribution.

## 10. Standardization of administration

Changes in instructions, timing, environment, device, language, examiner behavior, prompting, scoring, or allowed assistance can alter the meaning of a score.

If administration deviates from standardization:

- record the deviation;
- consider whether available norms remain applicable;
- weaken interpretations whose validity depends on standardized administration.

Do not silently compare nonstandard administrations to standard norms.

## 11. Cut scores and classifications

A threshold such as "positive/negative", "clinical/nonclinical", "impaired/not impaired", or "pass/fail" requires separate evidence supporting that threshold for the intended use.

Do not infer:

`score above cutoff -> diagnosis established`.

Check:

- how the cut score was derived;
- reference standard;
- sensitivity/specificity or classification consequences when relevant;
- false-positive / false-negative costs;
- population prevalence and base rates where predictive values matter;
- uncertainty near the threshold.

Close-to-cutoff results should be treated especially cautiously when measurement error is substantial.

## 12. Sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and base rates

For classification measures:

- **sensitivity** = ability to identify positives among true positives;
- **specificity** = ability to identify negatives among true negatives.

Positive and negative predictive values depend on prevalence/base rate.

Therefore a screening result with good sensitivity/specificity can still have a low positive predictive value in a low-prevalence population.

Classification performance does not automatically establish:

- construct validity;
- causal mechanism;
- individual diagnosis without appropriate assessment context.

## 13. Responsiveness

Responsiveness concerns ability to detect meaningful change over time in the intended construct.

Do not confuse:

- high test-retest stability in a stable construct;
- sensitivity to real change in a changing construct.

An outcome measure can be reliable yet poorly responsive, or responsive but insufficiently precise for individual change decisions.

## 14. Multimethod and multisource assessment

When stakes are meaningful, a single test should rarely be treated as the entire assessment.

Potential sources include:

- standardized tests;
- self-report;
- informant report;
- structured/semi-structured interview;
- behavioral observation;
- performance tasks;
- records;
- physiological or digital data.

Convergence can increase confidence.

Disagreement is also information and should be explained rather than averaged away automatically.

Possible causes of disagreement include:

- context specificity;
- reporter perspective;
- construct heterogeneity;
- method variance;
- state changes;
- response bias;
- measurement error.

## 15. Individual Interpretation Gate

Before using any score as evidence about an individual, answer all of the following.

### A. Construct

1. What exact construct is being inferred?
2. Which part of that construct does the measure actually sample?

### B. Purpose

3. Is the intended interpretation the same as the use for which evidence was established?
4. Is this screening, description, classification, diagnosis support, prediction, treatment monitoring, or research?

### C. Population

5. Is there reliability/validity evidence for a sufficiently relevant population?
6. Are language, culture, age, education, clinical status, and testing context relevant to comparability?

### D. Precision

7. What reliability/precision evidence applies?
8. What is the SEM or other error estimate near this score?
9. Does uncertainty cross a relevant threshold or alternative interpretation?

### E. Structure

10. Does the supported factor/measurement structure justify the score being interpreted?
11. If comparing groups/time, is measurement invariance adequate?

### F. Norms and thresholds

12. Is the norm/reference group appropriate and current enough?
13. Is any cutoff independently justified?

### G. Administration

14. Was administration/scoring standardized?
15. Could effort, fatigue, practice, device, language, examiner behavior, or situational factors materially affect the result?

### H. Corroboration

16. Does other high-quality evidence converge?
17. If sources diverge, is there a plausible explanation?

### I. Conclusion

18. What is the strongest conclusion supported?
19. What remains uncertain?
20. What conclusion would be an overreach?

If several critical questions cannot be answered, treat the score as **limited evidence**, not as a stable psychological fact.

## 16. High-value failure modes

### Failure A — reliability = validity

Incorrect:

`alpha = 0.90 -> scale valid`.

Correct:

High internal consistency may support precision within a suitable structure; validity requires broader evidence for the intended interpretation.

### Failure B — alpha = one construct

Incorrect:

`high alpha -> unidimensional scale`.

Correct:

Unidimensionality/structural validity must be evaluated separately.

### Failure C — test score = latent trait

Incorrect:

`score 120 -> true ability is exactly 120`.

Correct:

Observed scores contain measurement error and depend on the scale/norming model.

### Failure D — one norm fits everyone

Incorrect:

`percentile from published manual -> universally meaningful`.

Correct:

Norm interpretation depends on relevance and comparability of the reference group and administration conditions.

### Failure E — cutoff = diagnosis

Incorrect:

`screening score crosses threshold -> disorder established`.

Correct:

A cutoff may support further evaluation; diagnostic inference requires appropriate clinical evidence and context.

### Failure F — raw change = real change

Incorrect:

`score changed by 5 -> person changed`.

Correct:

Change must exceed plausible measurement/retest variation and be interpretable on that scale.

### Failure G — group difference = measurement bias

Incorrect:

`groups have different means -> test biased`.

Correct:

Bias/noninvariance requires evidence that measurement behavior differs conditional on the construct, not merely that populations differ.

### Failure H — no group mean difference = fairness

Incorrect:

`same average -> test fair`.

Correct:

Item/score functioning and validity can still differ despite similar means.

## 17. Closure audit

### Criterion 1 — score, measure, construct, and interpretation are separated

**PASS.**

### Criterion 2 — reliability is separated from validity

**PASS.**

### Criterion 3 — internal consistency is not treated as unidimensionality

**PASS.**

### Criterion 4 — individual score uncertainty is represented

**PASS.**

SEM/error bands and conditional precision are explicitly required where available.

### Criterion 5 — validity evidence is interpretation-specific

**PASS.**

Content, response-process, internal-structure, relations-to-other-variables, and use/fairness evidence are routed to the intended inference.

### Criterion 6 — structural validity and invariance are included

**PASS.**

Factor structure and measurement comparability across groups/time are explicit gates.

### Criterion 7 — norms and cut scores are constrained

**PASS.**

Reference-group relevance, age of norms, standardization, and threshold evidence are required.

### Criterion 8 — repeated/change-score inference is constrained

**PASS.**

Difference/change reliability, measurement error, practice effects, and regression-to-mean concerns are included.

### Criterion 9 — multisource evidence is integrated correctly

**PASS.**

Convergence strengthens inference; disagreement is treated as informative rather than automatically discarded.

### Criterion 10 — individual-use decision rule exists

**PASS.**

A 20-question Individual Interpretation Gate prevents a score from becoming an unsupported personal fact.

## Canonical working rule

**Never interpret a psychological score before checking what it measures, how precisely it measures it, for whom that interpretation is supported, under what conditions, and what uncertainty remains.**

## Scope note

COSMIN is designed primarily for health outcome measurement instruments, especially PROMs. Its taxonomy and distinctions between content validity, structural validity, internal consistency, reliability, measurement error, cross-cultural validity, and responsiveness are used here where conceptually transferable; COSMIN-specific numerical thresholds are not treated as universal rules for all psychological tests.
