## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a sufficiently accurate motor-control model for later exercise technique, skill acquisition, monitoring, exercise selection and coaching without:

- treating feedforward and feedback control as mutually exclusive systems;
- treating sensory feedback as too slow to matter during ongoing movement;
- treating internal models as a literally proven neural representation rather than a useful computational framework;
- assuming the CNS must eliminate biomechanical redundancy;
- equating coordination with one fixed pattern or with simple simultaneity of muscle activation;
- equating extracted muscle synergies with proven hard-wired neural modules;
- defining stability as merely low movement variability;
- defining all variability as error/noise;
- assuming one movement trajectory must be reproduced exactly for a task to be well controlled;
- confusing maximal force production with force control/steadiness;
- collapsing postural control into the later NEU-10 balance topic;
- treating joint stabilization as the job of a fixed set of “stabilizer muscles”;
- treating co-contraction as universally good or universally bad;
- treating anticipatory postural adjustments as universal, invariant pre-programmed patterns;
- assuming fatigue creates one universal coordination signature;
- assuming a novel/heavier load automatically causes technical failure rather than a task-specific reorganization.

### Question type

Mechanistic + applied human sensorimotor-control evidence relevant to voluntary movement and resistance exercise.

### Population/context

Healthy adults are the main transfer target. Reaching, force-field adaptation, postural tasks, gait and laboratory perturbation paradigms are used when they clarify general control principles. Direct squat/resistance-training evidence is weighted more heavily for downstream transfer to strength exercise.

### Critical outcomes

- predictive/feedforward behavior;
- online sensory correction;
- goal/task-variable stabilization;
- muscle/joint coordination;
- movement variability structure;
- force steadiness;
- mechanical impedance/joint stiffness;
- anticipatory postural adjustment;
- adaptation to fatigue and changed external load/dynamics.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 16 exact Section 9 items must be covered at content level. The pass must preserve the distinction between descriptive models and established neural mechanisms, separate task-relevant variability from generic “noise”, distinguish postural control from the broader balance topic, and maintain task-/measurement-specific interpretation of coordination under fatigue and changed load.

## 3. Core synthesis

Motor control is best treated here as **task-directed regulation of movement and force through interacting predictive, sensory-feedback, neural and mechanical processes**.

The evidence does not support a simple pipeline:

`motor plan → fixed command → movement`.

A more defensible project model is:

`task goal + current state + prior learning/prediction → initial control policy → movement → sensory consequences → rapid/continuous corrections + updated prediction → task outcome`.

The relative contribution of prediction, feedback, impedance/stiffness and learned coordination changes with:

- task speed;
- sensory delay/availability;
- uncertainty;
- external load;
- perturbation;
- skill level;
- fatigue;
- training history.

Canonical rule:

**motor control is closed-loop and task dependent; feedforward prediction and feedback correction are complementary, not competing, control modes.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 4. Feedforward control

Feedforward control refers to control action generated **before the relevant sensory error from the current movement can be used to correct that movement**.

Examples include:

- anticipatory muscle activation;
- anticipatory postural adjustments;
- predictive compensation for a learned force field;
- movement initiation based on expected limb/environment dynamics.

Feedforward control can be informed by:

- prior experience;
- current estimated body state;
- expected task dynamics;
- contextual cues;
- learned associations.

It is not equivalent to:

- a permanently fixed motor program;
- movement without sensory information;
- a command that cannot later be corrected.

The 2026 tracking study (PMID 41980556) found that greater trajectory predictability altered the balance between real-time visual reliance and internal/predictive control even when overt tracking accuracy changed little.

The 2026 interception study (PMID 42729088) likewise supports anticipatory control when late corrections become ineffective.

Canonical rule:

**feedforward means prediction-based prospective control, not sensory isolation or a completely pre-scripted movement.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 5. Feedback control

Feedback control uses information about the actual state/outcome of movement to modify ongoing or subsequent control.

Relevant feedback sources include:

- proprioceptive;
- cutaneous;
- visual;
- vestibular;
- force/load-related sensory input.

Feedback should not be modeled as one slow conscious correction channel.

Long-latency responses and other sensorimotor feedback pathways are:

- rapid;
- task sensitive;
- goal dependent;
- capable of integrating information across joints/effectors.

The optimal-feedback-control literature shows that corrective responses can be scaled to task goals and urgency rather than acting as invariant reflexes.

Canonical rule:

**sensory feedback is continuously integrated at multiple latencies; fast feedback can be goal directed and is not merely a hard-wired reflex layer.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 6. Feedforward and feedback are coupled

The practical distinction is useful, but the biological system does not cleanly alternate between two isolated controllers.

Prediction affects feedback by changing:

- expected sensory consequences;
- feedback gain;
- state estimation;
- which deviations matter to the task.

Feedback affects future feedforward control through:

- error-based updating;
- recalibration;
- adaptation to changed dynamics.

In force-field studies, humans both:

- generate predictive compensation as learning develops;
- change impedance/co-contraction;
- continue to use feedback.

Canonical rule:

**the normal state is coupled predictive + feedback control, with weighting changing by task and uncertainty.**

## 7. Internal models — working framework, not literal established object

Internal-model language remains highly useful in computational motor control.

Typical distinction:

- **forward model** — predicts sensory/mechanical consequences of commands/state;
- **inverse model** — maps desired outcome/state toward a command/control solution.

Evidence compatible with internal-model-like computation includes:

- predictive compensation;
- force-field adaptation;
- after-effects when the perturbation is removed;
- adaptation despite limited explicit awareness;
- reduced reliance on online sensory input when environmental structure becomes predictable.

However, this does **not** establish that the nervous system contains one discrete, literal, inspectable “model” matching the mathematical representation.

The 2025 critique (PMID 40866575) highlights unresolved representational and physiological questions. The 2025 cerebellar review (PMID 40523942) likewise discusses predictive control mechanisms that can resemble internal-model computations without requiring a classical explicit representation.

Project formulation:

> **Internal models are retained as a productive computational framework for prediction/adaptation, not as a closed ontological claim about the exact neural implementation.**

Confidence: **HIGH for usefulness of the framework; MODERATE for exact neural implementation.**

## 8. Degrees of freedom problem

Human movement has many potentially redundant degrees of freedom:

- joints;
- muscles;
- motor units;
- segment configurations;
- timing combinations.

A task usually constrains only a subset of all possible elemental variables.

Therefore the system does not necessarily need to find one unique movement solution.

Contemporary coordination frameworks often treat redundancy as **motor abundance**:

- many combinations can satisfy the same task goal;
- some variability can be allowed in dimensions that do not meaningfully disturb the goal;
- corrections can be concentrated on goal-relevant deviations.

Optimal-feedback-control and UCM frameworks converge on the practical idea that task success does not require freezing all available degrees of freedom.

Canonical rule:

**redundancy is not merely a problem to eliminate; it can provide flexibility and multiple valid solutions.**

Confidence: **HIGH at behavioral level**.

## 9. Coordination

Coordination is not simply:

- simultaneous activation;
- “good timing” in the abstract;
- a visually identical movement;
- maximally low variability.

For project use, coordination means:

> **task-relevant organization of multiple elemental variables so that important performance variables remain acceptably controlled.**

The coordinated elements may include:

- joints;
- segments;
- muscles;
- forces;
- posture;
- timing.

Coordination is therefore relational and task specific.

A coordination pattern that is efficient for:

- one load;
- one stance;
- one tempo;
- one anthropometry;
- one skill level

need not be optimal for another.

Direct squat evidence in 2026 (PMID 42516016) found robust low-dimensional synergy structure with smaller changes in timing/weighting across training status and loads, supporting both stability of broad coordination structure and flexible reorganization.

Confidence: **HIGH for task specificity; MODERATE-HIGH for direct resistance-exercise transfer.**

## 10. Movement synergies

“Synergy” is used for several related but non-identical constructs.

### Performance-stabilizing synergy

In UCM-type analysis, covariance among elemental variables can stabilize a task-relevant performance variable.

### Muscle synergy

EMG decomposition methods such as non-negative matrix factorization can identify lower-dimensional patterns that account for substantial variance across multiple muscle signals.

These are useful descriptive tools.

But:

`extracted EMG components ≠ proven fixed neural modules`.

Reasons include:

- decomposition-method dependence;
- task dependence;
- biomechanical constraints;
- signal-processing choices;
- non-unique factor solutions;
- possible neural and non-neural contributors.

The systematic and theoretical synergy literature explicitly recognizes ongoing debate over origin and interpretation.

Direct squat studies also show:

- recurring common structure;
- individual-specific combinations;
- training-related timing/weighting changes.

Canonical rule:

**synergy analysis describes structured coordination; it does not by itself prove a hard-wired CNS module.**

Confidence: **HIGH for descriptive value; LOW-MODERATE for literal modular-neural interpretation.**

## 11. Stability

Stability needs a defined controlled variable.

Possible meanings include:

- stable endpoint/trajectory;
- stable force output;
- postural stability;
- joint/limb mechanical stability;
- resilience to perturbation;
- rapid return toward a task goal.

Therefore:

`low variability ≠ automatically high stability`.

A movement can show:

- noticeable variability in redundant dimensions;
- yet stable task outcome.

Conversely, a movement can be highly repeatable in quiet conditions yet respond poorly to perturbation.

The 2026 systematic review of running stability (PMID 42732068) confirms that different linear, nonlinear and coordination-based metrics capture different constructs and that no universal stability metric exists.

Canonical rule:

**stability is resistance/recovery of a defined task-relevant variable, not generic movement sameness.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 12. Movement variability

Movement variability is intrinsic to repeated human movement.

It can appear in:

- joint angles;
- segment trajectories;
- force;
- timing;
- muscle activation;
- intermuscular coordination.

Variability can reflect:

- sensory/motor noise;
- exploration;
- redundant solution space;
- adaptation;
- fatigue-related reorganization;
- changing task constraints.

Direct squat evidence demonstrates both:

- inter-individual variability in successful squat strategies;
- intra-individual variability between successful repetitions;
- load-related changes in variability measures.

Therefore the project rejects:

> “the best technique is the one with the least variability.”

The correct interpretation depends on:

- what variable varies;
- whether task outcome is maintained;
- whether variability is structured;
- whether it changes with load/fatigue/skill;
- whether it predicts useful adaptation or loss of control.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 13. Functional variability vs noise

The distinction cannot be made from variability magnitude alone.

A practical framework:

### Potentially functional variability

Variation that:

- occurs mainly in redundant/task-irrelevant dimensions;
- preserves the salient task outcome;
- supports exploration or adaptation;
- allows compensation among joints/muscles;
- helps tolerate perturbation or changing constraints.

### Potentially nonfunctional variability / noise

Variation that:

- directly destabilizes the task goal;
- increases outcome error;
- reflects loss of force/trajectory control;
- becomes less structured and less adaptable.

But no universal threshold separates the two.

The 2025 systematic review (PMID 39547333) found that relationships between initial variability and later learning/adaptation depend on:

- task type;
- error-based vs reward-based learning;
- whether variability magnitude or temporal structure is measured.

The 2026 UCM methodological paper (PMID 42814146) further shows that similar spatial covariance can coexist with very different temporal structure.

Canonical rule:

**functional vs nonfunctional variability must be judged against the task goal and structure of variation, not SD alone.**

Confidence: **HIGH for principle; MODERATE for operational thresholds.**

## 14. Trajectory control

Motor control does not require the nervous system to enforce one exact precomputed trajectory in every task.

Under optimal-feedback-control logic:

- task-relevant deviations are corrected strongly;
- deviations that do not threaten the goal may be tolerated;
- the exact trajectory can emerge from interaction among task cost, feedback, prediction and body/environment dynamics.

However, trajectory itself can become a task-relevant variable when the task explicitly requires:

- avoiding an obstacle;
- following a path;
- reproducing a sport-specific movement;
- controlling bar/implement position within limits.

Force-field and walking studies show that trajectories adapt when environmental dynamics change and can display after-effects when those dynamics are removed.

Canonical rule:

**trajectory is controlled to the degree that trajectory matters to the task; successful control does not imply exact path replication in every degree of freedom.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 15. Force control

Force control is distinct from maximal force capacity.

Relevant constructs include:

- force steadiness;
- force tracking;
- force-matching error;
- rate/precision of force adjustment;
- coordination across multiple force-producing effectors.

The Enoka/Farina review (PMID 33595382) shows that force steadiness reflects the interaction of motor-unit discharge, common synaptic input and task demands.

Important boundary:

`stronger ≠ automatically steadier at every target force`.

Force-control performance depends on:

- target force level;
- muscle/task;
- sensory feedback;
- age/training status;
- fatigue;
- measurement method.

This remains consistent with NEU-05.

Canonical rule:

**maximal force production and force-control precision are related but distinct motor-control outcomes.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 16. Postural control

Postural control is the regulation of body orientation and center-of-mass/body-segment state relative to:

- gravity;
- base of support;
- intended voluntary movement;
- external perturbation.

It combines:

- sensory integration;
- anticipatory control;
- reactive feedback;
- changes in muscle activation and mechanical impedance.

This PASS owns the **general control principle**.

NEU-10 will separately own:

- static/dynamic balance;
- sensory weighting for balance;
- postural sway;
- reactive/anticipatory balance as a fitness construct;
- transfer of balance training.

Canonical rule:

**postural control is part of motor control, but the broader balance/neuromotor-fitness evidence remains NEU-10.**

## 17. Joint stabilization

Joint stabilization is not adequately modeled as:

> “activate the stabilizer muscle.”

Mechanical joint/limb stability can be influenced by:

- passive tissue properties;
- muscle activation;
- agonist-antagonist co-contraction;
- reflex/feedback responses;
- anticipatory pre-activation;
- task-specific mechanical impedance.

The stabilizer-muscle systematic review (PMID 25922556) supports early feedforward/feedback activation and contribution to stiffness, but does not justify one universal list of muscles whose only role is “stability”.

Recent perturbation evidence shows that co-contraction can improve rapid corrective performance, but it also has costs:

- metabolic cost;
- reduced movement freedom;
- altered voluntary movement speed;
- potentially unnecessary stiffness when uncertainty is low.

The 2026 comparison of co-contraction indices (PMID 42213698) also shows that different EMG indices capture different properties; “co-contraction” is not a single interchangeable number.

Canonical rule:

**joint stabilization is an adaptive neuromechanical function produced by multiple tissues and control processes; co-contraction is one adjustable strategy, not a universal goal.**

Confidence: **HIGH for principle; MODERATE for optimal amount in complex resistance tasks.**

---

## 18. Anticipatory postural adjustments

Anticipatory postural adjustments (APAs) are preparatory changes that occur before an expected destabilizing event associated with:

- a voluntary movement;
- predictable external perturbation;
- force production that creates predictable reaction forces/moments.

Current evidence includes:

- anticipatory COM/muscle changes before predictable floor perturbation (PMID 41942633);
- APAs compensating for forces and moments during upper-limb isometric force production (PMID 40974778).

APAs are:

- task dependent;
- context dependent;
- adaptable;
- not necessarily identical across individuals or movements.

They are compatible with predictive/internal-model frameworks but do not uniquely prove one specific computational mechanism.

Canonical rule:

**APAs are evidence of prospective postural preparation, not a fixed universal pre-program.**

Confidence: **HIGH for phenomenon; MODERATE for exact computational implementation.**

## 19. Coordination under fatigue

This item is already mechanistically bounded by NEU-08.

Fatigue can alter:

- muscle activation amplitude;
- timing;
- intermuscular coherence;
- joint/segment strategy;
- movement variability;
- force steadiness.

But there is no single universal “fatigued coordination pattern”.

The 2026 study in strength-trained vs untrained adults (PMID 41297828) found fatigue-related changes in force and coordination with training-status-dependent patterns.

The recent motor-variability fatigue study (PMID 41334719) also supports the idea that fatigue can change the structure of squat movement variability beyond a simple reduction in output.

Interpretive rule:

`coordination changed under fatigue ≠ coordination necessarily became globally worse`.

Some changes may be compensatory.

Canonical rule:

**fatigue perturbs the motor-control solution; whether the resulting reorganization is compensatory, neutral or performance-limiting depends on the task and controlled variable.**

Confidence: **HIGH for change/task dependency; MODERATE for functional interpretation of any one metric.**

## 20. Coordination under novel load

A novel load can mean:

- changed external resistance;
- changed direction of force;
- changed mechanical environment;
- added perturbation;
- changed inertial/dynamic properties.

The immediate response may combine:

- trajectory error;
- increased co-contraction/impedance;
- altered muscle weighting/timing;
- increased feedback reliance.

With repeated exposure, humans can show:

- error reduction;
- predictive compensation;
- after-effects;
- altered impedance;
- updated coordination.

Force-field adaptation studies provide strong mechanistic evidence for this process.

For resistance exercise, direct evidence is more limited but relevant:

- 2026 squat data across ~40–90% 1RM show broad synergy structure can remain robust while weighting/timing differs across load/training status (PMID 42516016);
- 2024 data show squat variability structure changes with relative load (PMID 39298439).

Canonical rule:

**changing load can reorganize the motor solution without requiring a wholly new coordination pattern; novelty and magnitude of the changed dynamics determine how much prediction, feedback and impedance must be updated.**

Confidence: **HIGH for general adaptation; MODERATE-HIGH for load-dependent squat reorganization; MODERATE for transfer to every free-weight exercise.**

## 21. Direct resistance-training interpretation

The direct evidence supports several practical boundaries.

### 21.1 Squat coordination is low-dimensional but not identical across people

PMID 39848201 found both:

- a broadly recurring squat synergy;
- additional person-specific synergy patterns.

Therefore:

**successful squat execution can have shared structure without one person-independent muscle solution.**

### 21.2 Training modifies coordination gradually

PMID 42516016 found that after 6 weeks of squat training:

- strength increased;
- broad synergy structure remained robust;
- muscle weights/timing showed smaller changes;
- experienced lifters differed more clearly in synergy timing/weighting.

Therefore neural skill adaptation can involve **reweighting/re-timing of an existing broad solution**, not necessarily creation of a new pattern.

### 21.3 Load changes variability

PMID 39298439 found systematic changes in squat acceleration-variability metrics across loads from 10–90% 1RM.

This supports:

**load is itself a motor-control constraint.**

It does not prove:

- one ideal variability level;
- that more complexity is always better;
- that a threshold predicts injury;
- that nonlinear variability metrics should directly prescribe training load.

### 21.4 Trained and untrained squat strategies can differ

PMID 41569842 reported different trunk-leg muscle-synergy patterns between trained and untrained participants.

This is consistent with skill/training-specific coordination.

It does not establish one universal “expert pattern” because:

- sample size is small;
- squat style/anthropometry constrain the solution;
- EMG synergy extraction is descriptive.

## 22. Measurement / interpretation boundaries

### 22.1 EMG amplitude

Does not directly measure:

- total neural drive;
- force;
- coordination quality.

### 22.2 Muscle synergy extraction

Does not directly prove:

- fixed CNS primitives;
- causation;
- optimality.

### 22.3 UCM / covariance analysis

Can identify task-related structure of variability.

It does not by itself identify:

- the neural generator;
- temporal control dynamics;
- whether a strategy is globally optimal.

### 22.4 Variability metrics

SD, CV, entropy, DFA, Lyapunov-style measures and UCM-based measures are not interchangeable.

They answer different questions about:

- magnitude;
- temporal organization;
- local divergence;
- task-variable stabilization.

### 22.5 Stability metrics

“Stability” must specify:

- what variable;
- what perturbation;
- what time scale;
- what return criterion.

### 22.6 Co-contraction indices

Different indices emphasize:

- amplitude;
- timing overlap;
- waveform similarity.

Do not compare them as if they were one construct.

### 22.7 Kinematic similarity

Two similar trajectories can arise from different muscular/control strategies.

Two different joint-level trajectories can preserve the same task outcome.

## 23. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| feedforward vs no feedback | predictive control can coexist with online feedback |
| feedback vs conscious correction | rapid feedback is often automatic and goal dependent |
| internal model framework vs proven neural object | useful computation does not fix neural ontology |
| redundancy vs dysfunction | multiple valid solutions can stabilize the same goal |
| coordination vs identical movement | coordinated solutions may vary |
| muscle synergy factor vs CNS module | decomposition is descriptive/model dependent |
| stability vs low variability | stable outcomes can coexist with redundant variability |
| variability vs error | some variability is task-irrelevant or adaptive |
| functional variability vs “more is better” | usefulness depends on task and structure |
| exact trajectory vs successful control | trajectory matters only to the degree constrained by task |
| maximal force vs force control | strength capacity and steadiness/precision differ |
| postural control vs balance topic | NEU-09 owns mechanism; NEU-10 owns balance fitness evidence |
| co-contraction vs joint stability | co-contraction is one contributor to impedance |
| high co-contraction vs good control | excessive stiffness has costs |
| APA vs fixed motor program | APAs adapt with context |
| fatigue-related change vs universal degradation | reorganization may compensate |
| heavier/novel load vs technical failure | altered solution may remain valid |

## 24. Practical downstream rules

1. **Treat feedforward and feedback as coupled control processes.**
2. **Do not infer absence of feedback control just because a movement is fast.**
3. **Use internal-model language as a computational explanation, not a literal anatomical claim.**
4. **Do not force one unique joint/muscle solution when multiple solutions preserve the task goal.**
5. **Define the controlled task variable before judging coordination quality.**
6. **Do not treat extracted muscle synergies as hard-wired modules without independent evidence.**
7. **Define stability by perturbation tolerance/recovery of a relevant variable, not by repetition sameness.**
8. **Do not penalize movement variability automatically; first determine whether it affects the task goal.**
9. **Do not prescribe “more variability” as universally beneficial either.**
10. **Separate maximal-force capacity from force-control precision.**
11. **Treat co-contraction/stiffness as adjustable strategies with performance-cost trade-offs.**
12. **Expect predictive postural preparation when voluntary force/movement creates predictable disturbances.**
13. **Under fatigue, interpret coordination changes relative to performance and task constraints.**
14. **Under a new/heavier load, allow task-specific reorganization before labeling a movement error.**
15. **Use direct resistance-exercise evidence where available; do not transfer every reaching/force-field result directly into barbell technique.**
16. **Reserve balance-training efficacy and transfer questions for NEU-10.**
