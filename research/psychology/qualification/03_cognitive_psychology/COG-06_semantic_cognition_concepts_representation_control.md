# COG-06 — Semantic cognition and concepts: representation, categorization, abstraction, and semantic control

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Establish a current working model of semantic cognition for psychological inference.

The block separates:

- semantic knowledge from language;
- semantic representation from semantic control;
- concepts from words/labels;
- concepts from categories;
- category membership from similarity;
- concrete from abstract concepts without treating abstractness as one homogeneous property;
- representation from retrieval/access;
- stored knowledge from context-sensitive deployment;
- conceptual knowledge from episodic memory;
- semantic task performance from a pure measure of semantic knowledge.

## Core rule

**Semantic cognition is the ability to acquire, represent, retrieve, combine, and flexibly use conceptual knowledge to guide thought and behavior.**

A practical architecture is:

`perceptual / motor / linguistic / social experience`
-> `learned semantic representations`
↔ `semantic control / task context`
-> `context-appropriate activation, categorization, inference, language, action`

This is not a claim that semantics lives in one isolated store.

## 1. Semantic cognition is broader than semantic memory as a static store

Older accounts often treated semantic memory as a repository of stable facts and concepts.

Current reviews favor a more dynamic view in which semantic knowledge emerges from learned mappings among:

- sensory representations;
- motor/action representations;
- linguistic representations;
- social/interoceptive/emotional information;
- higher-order multimodal structure.

Rogers' 2026 review describes a historical shift away from isolated domain-specific semantic modules toward interacting learned mappings across perception, action, and language.

Canonical rule:

**semantic cognition = representation + context-sensitive use, not merely storage.**

## 2. Semantic cognition is not language

Language is one major route into and out of semantic knowledge.

But semantic knowledge also supports:

- object recognition;
- action selection;
- nonverbal categorization;
- tool use;
- social knowledge;
- visual/auditory meaning;
- conceptual inference.

A person can show semantic processing in nonverbal tasks.

Therefore:

`semantic = lexical/verbal`

is false.

Likewise:

`word retrieval failure = loss of concept`

is not automatically valid.

## 3. Semantic cognition is not episodic memory

Semantic knowledge is relatively decontextualized knowledge about:

- concepts;
- facts;
- meanings;
- categories;
- regularities.

Episodic memory is tied more strongly to particular events and contexts.

The systems interact:

- repeated episodes can contribute to semantic learning;
- semantic knowledge scaffolds episodic encoding/reconstruction;
- a semantic fact may remain after acquisition context is forgotten.

Canonical boundary:

**knowing != remembering when/where the knowledge was acquired.**

## 4. Concept

The multidisciplinary semantic glossary emphasizes that "concept" is used differently across fields.

For this qualification system, use a deliberately modest operational definition:

> A concept is a structured representation or representational state that supports generalization across instances and enables context-appropriate inference, categorization, prediction, language, or action.

This does not commit to:

- one neural code;
- one amodal symbol format;
- one prototype;
- one exemplar set;
- one linguistic representation.

## 5. Word is not concept

A word/lexeme is a linguistic form.

A concept is the knowledge/representation that the form can activate or express.

One concept can have:

- multiple words across languages;
- synonyms;
- nonverbal access routes.

One word can also be ambiguous/polysemous and activate different meanings by context.

Therefore:

`same word -> same concept`

and

`different word -> different concept`

are both unsafe assumptions.

## 6. Category is not identical to concept

A category groups instances according to some equivalence relation.

A concept provides knowledge that can support categorization, inference, prediction, and other uses.

Category and concept overlap strongly but are not identical.

A person can possess conceptual knowledge that is not exhausted by category membership.

Example:

Knowing what a "hammer" is includes more than deciding whether an object belongs to the category HAMMER.

## 7. Categorization is task- and context-dependent

Categorization can depend on:

- similarity;
- explicit rules;
- learned boundaries;
- goals;
- context;
- prior knowledge;
- language;
- action relevance.

Current categorization research still debates single-system versus multiple-system accounts.

A 2024 Nature Reviews Psychology review concludes that both explicit/rule-like and similarity/procedural mechanisms have empirical support, while the correct architecture remains contested.

Canonical rule:

**one categorization paradigm does not define the architecture of concepts.**

## 8. Prototype, exemplar, rule, and theory-based accounts are explanatory models

### Prototype-like account

Category judgments depend on similarity to a summary/central tendency.

### Exemplar-like account

Judgments depend on similarity to stored instances.

### Rule-based account

Membership follows explicit or learned decision boundaries/rules.

### Theory/knowledge-based account

Background causal/relational knowledge shapes category structure.

These mechanisms need not be mutually exclusive across all tasks/domains.

Do not infer:

`prototype effect -> concepts are literally stored prototypes`

without stronger evidence.

## 9. Categorization may arise throughout processing

A 2026 Nature Reviews Neuroscience perspective argues that categorization is not merely a final post-perceptual step but can shape processing throughout the sensory-to-conceptual hierarchy.

This is an influential current perspective, not settled consensus.

Use the safer conclusion:

**categorization can interact with perception early and recurrently; do not assume a strict perception-first/category-later pipeline.**

## 10. Representation versus control

Controlled Semantic Cognition (CSC) distinguishes two interacting functions:

### Semantic representation

Relatively stable learned conceptual structure.

### Semantic control

Processes that regulate which aspects of knowledge are activated and used for the current goal/context.

Examples of control demands:

- weak/non-dominant association;
- resolving ambiguity;
- suppressing irrelevant meanings;
- switching semantic dimensions;
- using an unusual property of a familiar object;
- adapting knowledge to a novel context.

Canonical rule:

**knowing something and selecting the right aspect of that knowledge are separable problems.**

## 11. Semantic control is not identical to domain-general executive control

Semantic control overlaps with broader cognitive-control demands.

But neuropsychological/neuroimaging work supports partially distinct semantic-control networks that are especially engaged when conceptual knowledge must be flexibly shaped for task context.

A 2026 cross-modal fMRI study found semantic-control-network responses modulated by task knowledge across auditory and visual semantic tasks, while domain-general multiple-demand activity tracked other task demands.

Therefore:

`semantic control = generic executive function`

is too coarse.

At the same time, representation/control separation is not perfectly clean in every task or patient dataset.

## 12. Representation/control dissociation is useful but not absolute

Classic evidence from semantic dementia versus semantic aphasia motivated a representation-versus-control distinction.

Later studies have shown that some predicted dissociations are less clean than originally assumed.

Therefore CSC is used here as a **working framework**, not as a diagnostic law.

A semantic deficit must be localized by pattern:

- cross-item consistency;
- context sensitivity;
- cueing;
- distractor effects;
- modality;
- task demands;
- knowledge specificity.

## 13. Hub-and-spoke framework

The influential hub-and-spoke model proposes:

- modality-specific "spokes" carrying sensory/motor/linguistic information;
- a heteromodal "hub", classically associated with bilateral anterior temporal regions, supporting coherent/generalizable conceptual representations.

This framework explains how concepts can integrate features across modalities.

But current semantic science contains competing/hybrid accounts.

Canonical rule:

**hub-and-spoke is a strong model family, not a settled literal map of every concept.**

## 14. Distributed and hub representations can coexist

Current models increasingly combine:

- distributed modality-related representations;
- integrative heteromodal regions;
- learned connectivity patterns;
- control mechanisms.

The 2026 Annual Review describes semantics as emerging from learned mappings across sensory, motor, and language systems, rather than requiring a choice between "fully embodied" and "fully amodal" extremes.

## 15. Grounded cognition is not one doctrine

Grounded/embodied accounts propose that conceptual processing depends substantially on sensory, motor, interoceptive, emotional, and action systems.

But "grounded cognition" includes multiple theories with different claims.

Important distinctions:

- reactivation/contribution of modality systems;
- necessity of those systems;
- representational format;
- role of heteromodal hubs;
- role of language.

Evidence that motor cortex activates during action-word processing does not by itself prove that the concept is stored only in motor cortex.

## 16. Concrete concepts

Concrete concepts often have relatively strong links to:

- sensory features;
- object form;
- action;
- spatial structure.

They are often processed faster/more accurately than abstract concepts in many tasks.

But the "concreteness effect" varies with:

- word frequency;
- familiarity;
- imageability;
- category specificity;
- task;
- context.

A 2026 study shows categorical specificity itself contributes to processing differences.

Therefore:

`concrete > abstract performance -> one semantic system favors concreteness`

is too simple.

## 17. Abstract concepts are heterogeneous

"Abstract" is not one homogeneous category.

Abstract concepts can rely differently on:

- language;
- emotion;
- interoception;
- social interaction;
- mental-state knowledge;
- relational structure;
- cultural conventions.

Recent 2025–2026 reviews argue for multiple-representation accounts of abstract concepts.

Examples:

- justice;
- belief;
- freedom;
- probability;
- friendship

may depend on different semantic feature mixtures.

Canonical rule:

**abstractness is multidimensional.**

## 18. Language can be especially important for abstract concepts

Because some abstract concepts have fewer stable sensory referents, language can provide important structure through:

- definitions;
- relational patterns;
- discourse;
- social learning;
- linguistic co-occurrence.

This does not imply:

`abstract concept = word definition`.

Abstract concepts can also be grounded in emotional, social, motor, interoceptive, or mentalizing experience.

## 19. Social interaction can shape semantic representation

A 2025 Nature Reviews Psychology perspective proposes that social interaction is especially important in acquisition/use of many abstract concepts.

This is a current theoretical proposal with supporting evidence, not a universal law.

Use the generic conclusion:

**concept learning can be socially scaffolded, and social context may be constitutive for some conceptual domains.**

## 20. Abstraction

The 2025 multidisciplinary semantic glossary distinguishes abstraction from abstractness.

A useful operational definition:

**abstraction is the process of deriving representations that preserve relevant structure while reducing dependence on specific surface details.**

Abstraction can support:

- generalization;
- categorization;
- transfer;
- analogy;
- rule learning.

Do not treat abstraction as equivalent to:

- vagueness;
- verbal thinking;
- high intelligence;
- decontextualization in every sense.

## 21. Specificity and abstraction are not simple opposites

Concepts can be represented at different levels:

- specific: "my dog";
- basic: "dog";
- superordinate: "animal".

But abstraction can also operate over relations or properties that do not fit a simple taxonomic ladder.

Therefore category level and abstractness are related but distinct dimensions.

## 22. Conceptual flexibility

Current flexible-representation approaches propose that conceptual representations can change with:

- task;
- context;
- current goals;
- linguistic framing;
- recent experience.

This does not require that there is no stable knowledge structure.

A useful compromise:

**semantic representations have learned structure, while the currently activated/expressed representation is context-sensitive.**

## 23. Ad hoc categories

People can rapidly form task-specific categories such as:

- things to take from a burning house;
- objects useful for climbing;
- foods appropriate for a particular event.

These categories show that categorization can be goal-dependent and not limited to stored taxonomic classes.

Ad hoc categorization is especially relevant to semantic control.

## 24. Taxonomic and thematic relations

### Taxonomic relation

Shared category/class membership.

Example:

dog–cat.

### Thematic relation

Co-occurrence or participation in the same event/context.

Example:

dog–leash.

Both are legitimate semantic relations.

Different tasks, development, expertise, and context can emphasize them differently.

Do not treat one relation type as "more semantic" by definition.

## 25. Associative strength is not semantic identity

Two words/concepts can be strongly associated without sharing meaning.

Example:

doctor–hospital.

Association norms can predict semantic-task behavior but do not define conceptual similarity.

Canonical boundary:

**association != synonymy != conceptual similarity != category membership.**

## 26. Semantic distance is model-dependent

Semantic distance can be computed from:

- feature norms;
- association networks;
- distributional language models;
- behavioral similarity judgments;
- neural representations.

Different metrics capture different aspects of semantic structure.

Therefore:

`embedding distance = human conceptual distance`

is not automatically valid.

Any semantic-space measure needs behavioral/psychological validation for the intended claim.

## 27. Distributional semantics and language models

Text-based models learn semantic regularities from linguistic co-occurrence.

They can predict substantial human semantic behavior.

But text statistics omit or indirectly encode many:

- sensory;
- motor;
- social;
- causal;
- embodied

properties.

Recent semantic reviews emphasize the need to compare computational models explicitly with human behavioral baselines.

Do not treat success of an LLM/vector model as proof that human semantics is purely linguistic.

## 28. Concepts support inference beyond observed features

A concept allows expectations about unobserved properties.

If an object is categorized as a bird, knowledge may support predictions about:

- anatomy;
- behavior;
- habitat;
- causal properties.

This makes semantic cognition central to generalization.

But an inference derived from a category is not necessarily directly stored as one fact.

## 29. Category membership can be graded

Many natural categories show:

- typicality effects;
- fuzzy boundaries;
- context dependence.

A robin may be judged a more typical bird than a penguin.

This does not mean every category is graded.

Some categories can be rule-structured or institutionally defined.

Canonical rule:

**do not universalize prototype structure to all concepts/categories.**

## 30. Typicality effects do not prove prototype storage

Typicality effects can arise from:

- feature distributions;
- exemplars;
- learned statistical structure;
- language;
- task demands.

Behavioral typicality is a phenomenon; representational architecture requires additional evidence.

## 31. Expertise changes semantic structure

Experts can develop:

- finer-grained categories;
- richer feature knowledge;
- different similarity relations;
- faster retrieval;
- different task strategies.

Therefore semantic performance depends partly on knowledge history.

A domain-specific semantic advantage does not automatically indicate a general cognitive ability.

## 32. Semantic knowledge versus semantic access

A person may possess knowledge but fail to access the task-relevant aspect efficiently.

Potential causes include:

- weak cueing;
- ambiguity;
- interference;
- control demands;
- lexical access;
- task comprehension.

Therefore:

`failed semantic response -> knowledge absent`

is unsafe.

This parallels the availability/accessibility distinction in long-term memory.

## 33. Semantic task families are not interchangeable

Common tasks include:

- picture naming;
- word–picture matching;
- synonym judgment;
- semantic association;
- category verification;
- property verification;
- feature generation;
- semantic fluency;
- sorting;
- analogy;
- lexical decision with semantic effects.

Each mixes semantic representation with different amounts of:

- language;
- perception;
- retrieval;
- control;
- working memory;
- motor output.

No one task is a pure measure of "semantic knowledge."

## 34. Semantic fluency is highly task-impure

Category fluency ("name animals") depends on:

- semantic knowledge;
- lexical retrieval;
- search strategy;
- switching;
- clustering;
- speed;
- inhibition;
- working memory.

Therefore low semantic fluency does not localize a semantic-memory deficit by itself.

## 35. Naming is not a pure semantic task

Picture naming requires:

`visual recognition -> conceptual access -> lexical selection -> phonological encoding -> speech output`.

Failure can occur at any stage.

Canonical rule:

**anomia != semantic knowledge loss by default.**

## 36. Semantic errors need classification

Different errors imply different candidate loci.

Examples:

- coordinate/category error;
- superordinate response;
- thematic association;
- visual similarity error;
- unrelated response;
- omission;
- circumlocution.

But error type is not a diagnostic mechanism by itself.

Patterns across tasks and contexts matter more than one error.

## 37. Semantic control can vary across adulthood independently of accumulated knowledge

A 2026 lifespan study of 537 adults found:

- verbal semantic knowledge increased across adulthood;
- nonverbal knowledge plateaued around midlife;
- controlled semantic processing declined with age, particularly inhibition of irrelevant semantic knowledge.

This illustrates a critical distinction:

**having more knowledge != being better at flexibly controlling that knowledge.**

Do not generalize this group-level aging result to an individual without person-specific evidence.

## 38. Semantic control and context

Context can make different features relevant.

For "paper":

- office context -> writing;
- fire context -> flammability;
- packaging context -> wrapping.

Semantic control helps amplify context-relevant aspects and suppress dominant but irrelevant ones.

Thus concept use is not merely retrieval of a fixed feature list.

## 39. Semantic cognition and reasoning

Semantic knowledge provides content for reasoning.

Reasoning can:

- combine concepts;
- infer relations;
- generate new conclusions.

But semantic retrieval is not the same as reasoning.

A correct conceptual association can be retrieved without explicit multi-step inference.

Detailed reasoning is routed to COG-07.

## 40. Semantic cognition and language

Language can:

- cue conceptual knowledge;
- structure category learning;
- provide labels;
- support abstraction;
- transmit culturally accumulated concepts.

But the core language network and semantic/conceptual systems are functionally distinguishable.

Detailed language architecture is routed to COG-08.

## 41. Semantic Cognition Inference Gate

Before interpreting a semantic result, ask:

### A. Target

1. Is the claim about representation, access/retrieval, categorization, abstraction, or semantic control?
2. Is the target verbal, nonverbal, concrete, abstract, social, relational, or mixed?

### B. Representation

3. What conceptual knowledge is required?
4. Is the proposed representation modality-specific, distributed, hub-like, linguistic, or hybrid?
5. Does the evidence actually distinguish these architectures?

### C. Language boundary

6. Could lexical access explain the result?
7. Could phonological/syntactic processing explain it?
8. Is semantic knowledge being inferred from a language-heavy task only?

### D. Episodic boundary

9. Could episodic memory support the response?
10. Is decontextualized knowledge actually demonstrated?
11. Could repeated episodes or current familiarity explain performance?

### E. Categorization

12. Is categorization based on rule, similarity, exemplar, prototype, causal knowledge, or task context?
13. Does the paradigm distinguish these?
14. Is typicality being overinterpreted as prototype storage?

### F. Abstraction

15. What does "abstract" mean in this study?
16. Are concreteness, specificity, imageability, emotionality, socialness, and language dependence separated?
17. Is abstraction being confused with verbal complexity?

### G. Control

18. Is the required knowledge weak/non-dominant?
19. Are distractors or ambiguity present?
20. Is context-sensitive retrieval required?
21. Could domain-general cognitive control explain part of the difficulty?
22. Is representation loss being confused with control/access failure?

### H. Task impurity

23. What perceptual demands exist?
24. What language demands exist?
25. What working-memory/control demands exist?
26. What response/motor demands exist?
27. Is naming/fluency being treated as a pure semantic measure?

### I. Knowledge history

28. Could expertise/familiarity explain the performance?
29. Could cultural/language experience change the semantic structure?
30. Is a group norm being overgeneralized?

### J. Computational measures

31. What definition of semantic distance/similarity is used?
32. Is an embedding/association metric validated against human behavior?
33. Is model fit being mistaken for representational identity?

### K. Conclusion

34. What exact semantic component is supported?
35. What rival explanation remains?
36. What second task/manipulation would best distinguish representation from control/access?
37. What would constitute overclaiming?

## 42. High-value failure modes

### Failure A — word = concept

Incorrect.

Correct:

Words are linguistic forms; concepts can be accessed nonverbally and one word can express multiple meanings.

### Failure B — semantic memory = language

Incorrect.

Correct:

Semantic cognition supports verbal and nonverbal knowledge-guided behavior.

### Failure C — failed naming = concept lost

Incorrect.

Correct:

Naming includes perceptual, conceptual, lexical, phonological, and motor stages.

### Failure D — category = concept

Incorrect.

Correct:

Categorization is one use of conceptual knowledge, not its whole content.

### Failure E — prototype effect = prototype stored

Incorrect.

Correct:

Typicality effects can emerge from several representational/learning architectures.

### Failure F — abstract concepts are one type

Incorrect.

Correct:

Abstract concepts vary in linguistic, emotional, social, interoceptive, relational, and other content.

### Failure G — semantic representation = semantic control

Incorrect.

Correct:

Knowledge structure and context-appropriate selection/use are separable inferential targets.

### Failure H — semantic control = generic executive function

Incorrect as a full account.

Correct:

Semantic control overlaps with general control but can show domain-sensitive network/process structure.

### Failure I — hub-and-spoke = settled final architecture

Incorrect.

Correct:

It is an influential model supported by converging evidence, not the only live framework.

### Failure J — embodied activation proves embodied storage

Incorrect.

Correct:

Modality-specific activation can reflect contribution, simulation, access, or context without proving exclusive storage.

### Failure K — association = semantic similarity

Incorrect.

Correct:

Association, similarity, synonymy, and category membership are different relations.

### Failure L — embedding distance = human meaning distance

Incorrect.

Correct:

Computational semantic metrics require psychological validation.

### Failure M — semantic fluency = semantic knowledge amount

Incorrect.

Correct:

Fluency also depends on lexical retrieval, search, switching, inhibition, speed, and working memory.

### Failure N — more knowledge = better semantic cognition

Incorrect.

Correct:

Accumulated knowledge and flexible semantic control can dissociate.

## Canonical working rule

**Define the concept/semantic relation, separate representation from access/control, identify language/episodic/task demands, test alternative categorization architectures, and infer only the semantic structure that survives those alternatives.**

Never:

**semantic-task response -> direct readout of stored conceptual knowledge.**

## Current-standard note

As of 2026-11-19:

- the newest Annual Review synthesis describes semantic knowledge as emerging from learned mappings across sensory, motor, and linguistic representations rather than isolated semantic modules;
- Controlled Semantic Cognition remains an influential representation-plus-control framework, while current computational and neuroimaging work continues to refine the boundary between semantic-specific and domain-general control;
- current concept research emphasizes context-sensitive/flexible representations and rejects a simple one-format answer to concrete versus abstract knowledge;
- categorization architecture remains actively debated, including single-system, multiple-system, predictive, prototype/exemplar, and knowledge-based accounts.
