# PSY-RES-01 — Question framing, construct definition, and operationalization

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Build a reusable protocol for turning an informal psychological question into a scientifically tractable one without confusing labels, observations, constructs, measures, and explanations.

This block does **not** attempt to teach all study designs or psychometrics. Those are downstream topics.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Observation is not a construct

An observation is what was actually detected or reported: a behavior, response, statement, task performance, physiological value, recurring pattern, or contextual event.

A psychological construct is a theoretical concept used to organize and explain patterns of observations.

Therefore:

`observed pattern -> candidate construct`

is legitimate as a hypothesis.

`observed pattern = construct`

is not.

### 2. A label is not an explanation

Naming a pattern does not establish its mechanism.

If a construct is defined only by the same behavior that it is later invoked to explain, the explanation becomes circular.

Example of a circular form:

`repeated behavior -> label X -> behavior occurs because of X`

A useful construct must add independently testable structure: predicted relations, boundary conditions, differentiating indicators, temporal patterns, mechanisms, or consequences.

Cronbach and Meehl's construct-validity framework treats a construct as embedded in a nomological network whose claims must connect to observable predictions. A construct is therefore not validated by terminology alone.

### 3. Construct definition and operationalization are different

A **conceptual definition** specifies what the construct is intended to mean and where its boundaries lie.

An **operationalization** specifies how the construct will be represented or measured in a particular study.

A measure is not automatically equivalent to the construct itself.

Flake, Pek, and Hehman describe construct validation as beginning with construct identification and definition, followed by a theory of its structure, measure selection, and evidence that the measure appropriately represents the construct.

### 4. Measurement is interpretation-dependent

Validity concerns whether evidence and theory support a particular interpretation or use of measurement results; it is not adequately captured by saying that a test is simply "valid" in all contexts.

Population, context, administration, scoring, and intended use can change the defensibility of an interpretation.

### 5. Constructs can overlap

Different labels may refer to strongly overlapping constructs, and similar labels may conceal meaningfully different constructs.

Recent metascience work documents substantial construct and measure proliferation in psychology. Therefore, apparent novelty or a familiar name is not sufficient evidence that a construct is distinct.

## Operational protocol: Psychological Question Card

Before searching the literature or assigning a psychological interpretation, create the following card.

### A. Raw phenomenon

Describe the phenomenon in minimally interpretive terms.

Include:

- what was observed or reported;
- frequency or recurrence if known;
- time course;
- context;
- triggers or antecedents if known;
- consequences if known;
- what is **not** known.

Avoid diagnostic or theoretical labels at this stage.

### B. Candidate construct

For each candidate construct specify:

1. name;
2. conceptual definition;
3. included features;
4. excluded features;
5. whether it is primarily a state, trait, process, behavior, capacity, symptom cluster, relation, or outcome;
6. expected temporal stability;
7. expected contextual dependence.

### C. Alternatives

List plausible competing constructs or non-construct explanations.

At minimum ask:

- Could the same observation arise from another psychological mechanism?
- Could it be situational rather than trait-like?
- Could measurement, memory, reporting, demand, or context explain it?
- Could several mechanisms produce the same surface behavior?

Do not collapse alternatives merely because one explanation is intuitively attractive.

### D. Observable indicators

Specify what evidence should be expected if the construct interpretation is useful.

Possible units include:

- systematic behavioral observation;
- task performance;
- self-report;
- informant report;
- interview;
- physiological measurement;
- neural measurement;
- longitudinal pattern;
- response to an experimental manipulation.

NIMH RDoC is useful as an example of keeping constructs distinct from units of analysis: constructs may be studied using multiple measurement levels, including behavior and self-report, and construct domains may overlap.

### E. Discriminating evidence

State what would distinguish the target construct from its nearest alternatives.

Useful questions:

- What observation should differ between candidate A and candidate B?
- What result would weaken A?
- What context should change the phenomenon if A is correct?
- What should remain stable if A is trait-like?
- Which predicted external relations should converge?
- Which theoretically different constructs should remain distinguishable?

### F. Claim type

Classify the intended claim before choosing evidence.

| Claim | Core question | Evidence family to prioritize |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | What occurs, how often, and in what form? | descriptive observation, survey, qualitative or mixed evidence as appropriate |
| Measurement | Does this indicator/score represent the intended construct? | psychometric and construct-validation evidence |
| Association | Are variables related? | correlational/observational designs with appropriate controls |
| Prediction | Does information improve prospective prediction? | prospective validation and prediction studies |
| Causal | Does changing X change Y? | experimental, quasi-experimental, or explicitly causal designs |
| Mechanistic | Through what process could X produce Y? | converging mechanistic, temporal, perturbational, and mediation evidence |
| Intervention | Does an intervention improve a target outcome? | controlled intervention evidence |
| Diagnostic/classification | Can a method classify a clinically defined condition or state? | diagnostic-accuracy and clinical-validation evidence |
| Experiential/meaning | How is a phenomenon experienced or understood? | qualitative or mixed-methods evidence |

This routing is deliberately high-level. Study-design hierarchy and causal identification are handled in PSY-RES-02.

### G. Operationalization

For every proposed measure state:

1. why this measure maps to the construct;
2. what part of the construct it covers;
3. what it omits;
4. administration conditions;
5. scoring rule;
6. known validity evidence;
7. known reliability evidence;
8. population/context limits;
9. whether the measure was modified.

Flake and Fried emphasize that unreported or weakly justified measurement choices can undermine conclusions even when the rest of a study is statistically sophisticated.

### H. Confidence and unresolved uncertainty

End the card with:

- strongest supported interpretation;
- viable alternatives;
- evidence that would discriminate among them;
- current confidence;
- what should **not** yet be concluded.

## Anti-circularity test

Before accepting an explanation, ask:

1. Was the construct inferred from the same observation it is now being used to explain?
2. Does the construct generate any independent prediction?
3. Could the construct be wrong while the original observation remains true?
4. Is there evidence beyond semantic fit?

If the answer pattern is **yes / no / no / no**, the explanation is circular or close to circular.

## Construct-overlap test

Before treating two labels as distinct constructs:

1. compare conceptual definitions;
2. compare item/indicator content;
3. compare expected antecedents and consequences;
4. compare nomological networks;
5. examine convergent and discriminant evidence;
6. check whether apparent differences are measurement-method artifacts.

Do not assume:

- same name -> same construct;
- different name -> different construct.

## Multi-method rule

Multiple measurement methods can strengthen inference when they probe the construct through meaningfully different channels.

However, agreement across methods is not automatically proof of construct validity, and disagreement is not automatically disproof. Method variance, context, timing, construct heterogeneity, and imperfect operationalization can all matter.

## What this protocol prevents

1. treating terminology as mechanism;
2. turning one observation into a stable trait without evidence;
3. selecting a measure before defining the construct;
4. using a score as if it were the construct itself;
5. accepting one attractive explanation without alternatives;
6. using correlational evidence for causal claims;
7. mistaking construct overlap for conceptual precision;
8. silently moving between state, trait, process, behavior, and diagnosis.

## Closure audit

### Criterion 1 — distinguish construct from label

**PASS.** The protocol requires an explicit conceptual definition and independent predictions rather than semantic naming.

### Criterion 2 — separate observation from interpretation

**PASS.** Raw phenomenon is recorded before candidate constructs.

### Criterion 3 — identify plausible alternative constructs

**PASS.** Alternatives are mandatory and must remain live until discriminating evidence is available.

### Criterion 4 — define measurable/observable indicators

**PASS.** Each construct must map to explicit indicators and units of analysis.

### Criterion 5 — prevent circular explanations

**PASS.** A dedicated anti-circularity test rejects explanations that add no independent prediction.

### Criterion 6 — route questions to the correct evidence type

**PASS.** Claim-type routing distinguishes descriptive, measurement, association, prediction, causal, mechanistic, intervention, diagnostic, and experiential questions.

### Criterion 7 — preserve uncertainty when constructs overlap

**PASS.** The overlap test and final uncertainty section prohibit premature collapse or separation of constructs.

## Canonical working rule

**Describe first. Define second. Compare alternatives third. Operationalize fourth. Match evidence to the claim fifth. Conclude only at the level the evidence supports.**
