Direct publisher HTML, selected abstract, methods and discussion inspected, not every line/table. ## 4. A — Adult transition, intimacy and physiological inference

Task: two retired spouses disagree about relocation. A woman reports unchanged value of music and independence, stops rehearsing after the move, and reports poor sleep and discomfort. A relative claims menopause permanently changed personality and desire; spouse treats declining a sexual invitation as loss of love. Both want to preserve the marriage and voluntary intimacy. Later: accessible rehearsal space opens, music resumes, discomfort persists, and she still declines sex.

Response (C1–C8):
1. Distinguish self-report of values/symptoms, behavior, circumstances, relative's causal claim, spouse's interpretation; no measured hormonal status or stable trait change.
2. Goal importance, opportunity, adult developmental transition, sleep, pain, global versus relationship-specific desire, love, attachment, consent and relationship satisfaction have separate evidence requirements. Sex/gender group findings do not diagnose an individual.
3. Competing possibilities include reduced access, altered goal importance, symptom burden, relational tension, changed sexual preference, or combined causes. Return to rehearsals when access improves weakens a strong generalized loss-of-interest model; continuing refusal does not establish its cause.
4. Coincidence of move, symptoms and behavioral change is confounded; different rates of change do not establish a hormonal mechanism or reciprocal causal path.
5. Prospective target: voluntary resumption of ordinary rehearsals in next four weeks if access remains usable and participant wishes to take part. Record attendance only from voluntary reports/legitimate records; no access = ineligible for access-conditional prediction; unknown = unresolved. Continued participation despite discomfort is compatible with context-specific effects. Sexual consent is not a performance target or test.
6. New access and resumed rehearsal reopen the general personality/goal-change explanation and dependent predictions about leisure engagement; preserve past absence, symptoms and actual refusal. Do not infer resumed sexual interest from music activity.
7. Compare current living arrangement with shared voluntary trials of local activities, a reversible alternative relocation plan if feasible, and participant-directed healthcare consultation for persistent discomfort. Consider access, cost, autonomy, both partners' preferences and reversibility; no assumption that intimacy must take a sexual form. Avoid diagnosing or prescribing treatment.
8. Respect refusal now, irrespective of hypothesized cause; no pressure, covert measurements or testing desire. No health disclosure without consent.
Self-review: all eight criteria addressed, no critical failure identified. No independent validation.

## 5. B — Parenthood, goal stability and dyadic conflict

Task: first-time parents consider alternating care schedules. Mother highly values career and family but declines a conference. Partner infers lost ambition and attributes arguments to her attachment style. Both report fatigue while infant sleep is irregular. Colleague asserts women naturally become less ambitious after childbirth. Later: corrected schedule shows childcare unavailable for conference; partner's work deadline coincides with several arguments.

Response:
1. Separate stated goals, declined event, verified childcare availability, reported fatigue, deadlines, arguments and third-party interpretations.
2. Distinguish agency/communion, maternal identity, preference, constrained opportunity, state/trait, attachment dimensions, role demand, actor/partner/shared effects and adult-development timing. Do not conflate parenthood with sex or assume all mothers have the same trajectories.
3. Compare stable career goal with unavailable childcare, changing priorities, temporary fatigue, mismatch of schedules, shared environmental burden and reciprocal conflict. Childcare correction weakens inference from the declined event to low ambition; partner's deadline strengthens a competing shared/contextual account without ruling out interaction effects.
4. A candidate time-indexed graph has care/sleep and deadlines affecting both partners' states; possible cross-partner feedback is a separate hypothesis. Observations and concurrent inputs do not identify causal coefficients or fixed attachment type.
5. Prospectively compare conflict frequency over the next two jointly feasible ordinary scheduling cycles using voluntary reports and comparable exposure to disagreements; record missing reports and unavailable schedule alternatives separately. If sleep, deadlines and scheduling all change, contrast does not identify a single cause. A voluntarily accepted later event with childcare may distinguish opportunity from broad abandonment of career, not prove stable importance.
6. Correct opportunity count and interpretation of conference decline; reopen attachment attribution, conflict model and schedule comparison; preserve earlier behavior and reports. Do not rewrite predictions or force a new opportunity to test them.
7. Compare feasible alternating schedule, current arrangement, or voluntary redistribution if both agree, including childcare feasibility, work obligations, rest, family and career objectives and burdens for both. No predetermined gender assignment, no invented utility weights.
8. Consent, caregiving safety, independent goals and fair discussion constrain choices. No clinical attachment diagnosis, blame, coercion or surveillance.
Self-review: all eight criteria addressed, no critical failure identified.

## 6. C — Trust, veracity, influence and autonomy

Task: two adults share a community arts budget with a spending limit; one says automatic renewal caused excess. The other calls nervous tone proof of deception and demands every personal account password. Both want accurate shared-budget records; personal finances independent. Later: receipt verifies renewal, earlier notice reached purchaser but reading unknown; other person threatens to withhold shared funds unless given personal passwords.

Response:
1. Verify agreement, receipt, notice delivery and actual threat separately from claims, tone and inferred knowledge. Receipt supports automatic charge, not whether purchaser expected it.
2. Factual falsity, knowledge/belief, intent to mislead, private nondisclosure, budget reliability, apology/repair, affect expression, influence and coercive conduct are distinct. Domain-specific trust does not grant access to all finances.
3. Compare oversight, misunderstanding, deliberate omission and factual miscommunication. Verified renewal weakens claim of knowingly fabricated mechanism but not every omission hypothesis. Delivery is not proof of reading. Independent transaction records could resolve budget facts; no behavioral cue alone identifies belief.
4. Nervous tone can reflect dispute, stress, fear of accusation or other causes; group meta-analysis not calibrated to this case. Illustrative arithmetic only: stipulated prevalence .10, sensitivity .70 and specificity .80 imply positive predictive value .28 (executed JavaScript), showing why prevalence matters. These rates are invented, not assigned to tone or people.
5. Prospective target: for next two authorized shared-budget renewals if any occur, compare timely accurate entries to legitimate receipts; no renewal ineligible, missing ledger unresolved. Better bookkeeping supports domain-specific reliability but not proof of truthfulness in other domains.
6. Revise charge mechanism and dependent factual accusation, retain documented limit excess and uncertainty about awareness. New threat changes autonomy/safety appraisal independently of whether the original error was intentional. Prior tone-based hypothesis loses evidential force; do not replace with confident counteraccusation.
7. Compare shared-only accounting reconciliation, consensual spending controls and renewal reminders, separate responsibility or stepping away from shared arrangement if feasible. Repair can address verifiable budget breach; refusal to disclose unrelated passwords is legitimate. If threat creates financial risk, prioritize protection and independent support, not maximal reconciliation.
8. Threatening access to shared resources to obtain private credentials is coercive pressure in the stipulated act; no need to establish perpetrator's inner motive or diagnose a pattern to recognize it. Do not disclose passwords, monitor private accounts or devise pressure tactics. Protect voluntary choice and safety.
Self-review: all eight criteria addressed; no critical failure identified.
