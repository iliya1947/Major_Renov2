## 1. Question and integration gap

How do motivational priorities, goal-specific appraisal, affect, regulation goals, strategies and observable behavior interact across time? How can alternative explanations of an observed change be compared without equating emotional relief with goal attainment or presuming a unique motive?
Scope: adult nonclinical psychology, conceptual integration and descriptive/conditional inference. No private examples, source observations or personal models. This is not an experimentally identified causal system.

## 3. Unified conceptual model

Preserve separate levels:
- Stable tendencies and history; current context, opportunities and constraints (IND-01).
- Goal content, hierarchy, importance, endorsement, expectancy, competing goals and current priority (IND-02.1).
- Event appraisal, emotion components, intensity, time course and action tendency (IND-02.2).
- Regulation purpose: desired experience/expression and any broader end; strategy choice, attempt, implementation and outcome.
- Observable action, actual and perceived goal progress, immediate affective outcome, other consequences and later appraisal/valuation.

A conceptual feedback sequence: context and goals influence appraisal and experience; appraisal/experience may influence allocation, regulation and action; action and events produce outcomes; perceived outcomes may update appraisal, expectancy, value, priority and later action. Arrows are hypotheses at a specified time scale, not all established causal links. Multiple loops and competing goals can coexist. Immediate mood or tension reduction is an outcome channel, not evidence of achievement of a separate higher-level goal. A higher-level goal may be pursued despite unpleasant emotion; pleasant emotion may occur without commitment. A decision and consent are distinct from any inferred feeling.

## 4. Operational protocol

1. Define the actual question and the goal hierarchy. Separate target end state, intermediate results, indicators and side effects. Do not substitute relief, immediate cooperation or a local success for the target outcome.
2. Separate observed behavior and its opportunities from reported feelings, motives, intentions and analyst hypotheses. Use time windows and provenance; missing observations are not proof of absence.
3. Compare explanations of motivation: value change, relative priority shift, expectancy, constraints, different means, mixed motives, state, implementation failure and disengagement. A lack of action does not prove either absence or presence of a hidden desire.
4. Compare explanations of affect: goal appraisal, mixed affect, alternating experience, spontaneous change, expression norms, regulation attempts and observer error. Do not assume regulation or concealment without evidence.
5. If proposing a regulatory process, specify its possible purpose, target, timing, evidence and alternative account. Distinguish use from efficacy, and outcome for emotion from outcome for broader goals.
6. Trace the sequence with feedback and alternative causal directions. Compare similar contexts across time; preserve within-person versus between-person estimates, event exposure, reactivity, recovery and recurrence.
7. Rank competing models by discriminating evidence rather than plausibility alone. Specify rival conditional predictions, relevant naturally occurring observations, uncertainty and revision triggers. No fabricated probabilities, covert tests, engineered pressure or manipulation.
8. Output: supported description, best-supported conditional inference, remaining alternatives, limits and what new evidence would change it. Respect explicit choices and boundaries independently of the inferred emotional or motivational account.

## 5. External-data challenge and practice

Retrospective tests against already-visible findings, not blind or independent evaluation.
A. E2: distraction has three positive goal correlates in the same model; therefore identifying a unique purpose from that strategy is unsupported. The null interaction blocks a deterministic intensity-based selection rule. Report association, not causal policy.
B. E3: daily and between-person comparisons favor different strategies on selected outcomes. Keep level and outcome separate; do not rank a strategy universally. Negative affect alongside effort is compatible with several accounts, including difficult goal pursuit, not proof of success or failure.
C. E4: person-specific model heterogeneity warrants individual evidence, not an assumption that every person requires a complex unique model. Predictive validation, measurement and alternative simpler models remain necessary.

Synthetic transfer check (original neutral task, not derived from private information):
A workshop participant reports strong interest in completing a sculpture, stops working for two sessions, feels relieved after a deadline extension and later resumes work. Initial evidence supports stated interest, behavioral interruption and reported relief; not a uniquely identified motive. Competing explanations: constraint or fatigue, altered priority, changed expectancy, reduced desire, combinations. Resumption strengthens evidence of current pursuit under changed conditions, but does not establish unchanged inner value or why the interruption occurred. Relief is not completion. A rival prediction of continued nonparticipation under accessible comparable conditions would be weakened by sustained resumption; a constraint account needs evidence of the actual constraint. No exact probability or claim of causation. This check demonstrates application of distinctions, not generalizable competence.
