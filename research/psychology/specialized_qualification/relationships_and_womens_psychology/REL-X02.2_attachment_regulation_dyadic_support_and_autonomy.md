## 3. Regulatory construct map
Attachment-figure functions are candidate relational roles: proximity seeking, safe haven (comfort or assistance during threat), secure base (support for autonomous exploration), and response to separation. A person can provide some functions without being a mutually desired romantic partner; neither ordinary contact nor receipt of help establishes all functions. Separate attachment orientation, felt security, threat appraisal, actual threat, support need, support request, provider behavior, recipient perception, recovery, autonomous action and lasting change.

Hyperactivation and deactivation are theoretical strategies concerning regulation under perceived attachment threat; reassurance seeking or withdrawal is not by itself diagnostic of either. Actual unavailability, excessive demands, ordinary preferences, fatigue, need for privacy, disagreement or realistic safety concerns can produce similar behavior. Autonomy means volition, not simply absence of reliance or physical distance. Responsiveness does not imply agreement, compliance or permanent availability.

Illustrative dyadic model (not fitted or causally identified): each person's prior state, appraisal and current circumstances contribute to subsequent state and action; each partner's behavior and shared circumstances may also contribute. A partner coefficient estimated conditional on actor characteristics is still an association unless the causal design and assumptions support more. Contemporaneous coordination can arise with zero direct partner effect, e.g., a shared external event changes both states. Lagged effects can arise from unmeasured shared exposures or measurement timing. A temporary reduction in distress is distinct from change in baseline orientation, durable preference, commitment or mutual agreement.

## 4. Operational inference and revision
1. Define the regulatory endpoint, relationship target, actor, context and timescale. Do not use attachment as an omnibus explanation for contact, attraction, refusal or commitment.
2. Distinguish actual stressor, subjective appraisal, state, observed behavior and function. Use voluntary reports and naturally occurring observations; no score assignment or motive inference from one cue.
3. Compare relationship-specific appraisal, ordinary preference/boundary, general stress, opportunity limits, actual support mismatch and attachment-related regulatory hypotheses. Allow coexistence. Evaluate actual conditions before attributing behavior to an internal orientation.
4. Separate desired, offered, received and perceived support; distinguish comfort from autonomous exploration. Evaluate whether the recipient freely wants the support and can decline it. Respect both actors' autonomy and safety.
5. Assess whether the proposed effect concerns simultaneous coordination, prospective association, transient recovery or enduring change. Consider prior baseline, both actors' behavior, shared context, reverse direction, measurement and sampling. Do not interpret APIM, mediation or correlation as a causal estimate without additional support.
6. Predefine ordinary discriminating observations and update only the supported claim. If rival explanations predict the same outcome, no diagnostic update. Do not manufacture insecurity, pressure, dependence or tests. A stated boundary or relationship choice remains valid regardless of hypothesized regulation.
7. Treat evidence from established couples, laboratory tasks and security priming as context-limited. No extrapolation to an unwilling partner, clinical intervention or guaranteed personal change. No uncalibrated individual probabilities.

## 5. Internal synthetic checks
Rubric fixed before answering: construct separation, competing explanation, distinguishing evidence, inference boundary. Critical failures: unsupported diagnosis/motive, causal inflation, ignoring counterevidence, coercion, privacy breach. Neutral authored cases, not independent or held-out.

A. Two partners' security scores rise together after a shared housing problem resolves. Response: coordination is not causal partner influence; common context and mutual support can coexist. Compare timing, individual reports and naturally varying circumstances; no directional effect established.
B. A person declines help with a hobby but seeks comfort during illness. Response: secure-base assistance and safe-haven needs differ; preference, competence and task context compete. Ask only through ordinary voluntary communication about desired support. No avoidance classification.
C. A person withdraws during a disagreement in a noisy setting. Response: withdrawal is behavior, not a regulatory function by definition; sensory load, anger, boundary or attachment threat are alternatives. Compare ordinary contexts and voluntary account; no inferred hidden desire.
D. A partner feels less distressed after a supportive conversation. Response: recovery and durable orientation differ; resolution, elapsed time and perceived understanding may all matter. Observe repeated baseline over time; no guaranteed change in attachment or romance.
E. A meta-analysis correction reduces an estimate and bias-adjustment methods disagree. Response: revised estimates supersede old figures, but adjusted estimates depend on assumptions; publication selection and heterogeneity are alternatives. Inspect correction and sensitivity analyses, report uncertainty. No certainty about a true effect or individual response.
F. A person prefers independent travel and freely requests logistical help. Response: autonomy and help seeking are distinct, potentially compatible; preference, convenience and actual needs compete. Distinguish voluntary choice and support received. No dependency or avoidant label.
