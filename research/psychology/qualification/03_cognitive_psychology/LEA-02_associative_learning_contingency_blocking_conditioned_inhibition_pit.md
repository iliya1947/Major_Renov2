# LEA-02 — Associative learning: contingency, blocking, conditioned inhibition, and Pavlovian–instrumental transfer

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Deepen the associative-learning architecture established in LEA-01.

This block separates:

- temporal pairing from statistical contingency;
- contingency from causal belief;
- cue–outcome learning from action–outcome learning;
- excitatory learning from conditioned inhibition;
- conditioned inhibition from latent inhibition;
- cue competition from simple low learning;
- blocking from overshadowing;
- prediction-error accounts from attentional, inferential, and representational alternatives;
- Pavlovian conditioned responding from instrumental action;
- general PIT from outcome-specific PIT;
- cue-triggered influence from automaticity;
- explicit expectancy from direct mechanistic proof;
- awareness dependence in human fear conditioning from claims about all associative learning.

## Core rule

**Associative-learning claims require a specified relation, a valid contingency comparison, and a discriminating test of the representation/process controlling behavior. Pairing, reduced responding, cue competition, or transfer effects do not uniquely identify one associative mechanism.**

A generic relation map is:

`context + cue(s) + timing`
-> `predicted outcome distribution`
-> `comparison with background / competing predictors`
-> `update of cue–outcome relation, attention, expectancy, or causal model`
-> `conditioned response and/or influence on action`.

For instrumental interaction:

`Pavlovian S–O knowledge`
+
`instrumental A/R–O knowledge`
-> `cue-dependent modulation of action`.

## 1. Association must specify its relata

"Association" is underspecified unless the related representations are named.

Examples:

- S–O: stimulus predicts outcome;
- S–S: stimulus predicts another stimulus;
- A/R–O: action predicts outcome;
- S–R: stimulus triggers response;
- context–O: context predicts outcome.

Canonical rule:

**observed conditioning does not reveal relation type by itself.**

## 2. Contiguity

Contiguity concerns temporal/spatial closeness between events.

Closer CS–US timing often facilitates learning.

But close pairing alone does not establish predictive information.

## 3. Contingency

Contingency concerns whether one event changes the probability/rate of another.

A simple binary approximation is:

`ΔP = P(O|C) - P(O|¬C)`.

Thus a cue may be repeatedly paired with an outcome but provide little information if the outcome occurs equally often without the cue.

Canonical rule:

**number of pairings != contingency.**

## 4. Background rate is part of the learning problem

The predictive value of a cue depends on what happens in its absence.

High background outcome rates can weaken cue informativeness without changing the number of cue–outcome pairings.

This is why learning models that represent only paired trials can miss important contingency effects.

## 5. Timing carries information beyond pair counts

Harris's 2025 review emphasizes:

- CS–US temporal proximity;
- trial spacing;
- contingency.

Current temporal-causal models likewise show that interval distributions can explain conditioning phenomena normally assigned only to trial-wise prediction error.

Canonical rule:

**associative evidence is distributed in time, not only across discrete paired trials.**

## 6. Continuous outcomes require richer representations

Many real outcomes vary continuously rather than being present/absent.

Chow et al. (2026) showed across four human contingency-learning experiments that a distributed prediction-error model preserving outcome magnitude/distribution fit behavior better than a simple point-estimate delta model for nearly all participants.

This supports a broader lesson:

**the representation of the outcome matters to the learning rule.**

## 7. Statistical contingency is not identical to causal belief

Human participants can be asked:

- "Does X predict Y?"
- "Does X cause Y?"

These are not the same judgment.

Causal belief can additionally depend on:

- prior causal knowledge;
- mechanism plausibility;
- alternative causes;
- intervention assumptions;
- instructions;
- language.

Therefore:

**cue–outcome prediction != causal attribution by definition.**

## 8. Explicit descriptions can change associative-learning phenomena

Kelly and Ludvig (2025) showed that verbally describing one cue's causal relation could block later trial-by-trial learning about a new cue.

This demonstrates that a canonical associative-learning effect can be altered/created through explicit symbolic information.

Canonical rule:

**experience-based and description-based learning can interact inside the same blocking paradigm.**

## 9. Excitatory conditioning

A cue is excitatory when it predicts an increase in the expected outcome relative to the relevant baseline/context.

Excitation can be indexed by:

- conditioned responding;
- expectancy;
- transfer;
- summation with other cues.

The exact interpretation depends on task and response system.

## 10. Conditioned inhibition

A conditioned inhibitor predicts that an otherwise expected outcome will be absent/reduced.

Classic design:

- A+ : A predicts outcome;
- AX− : compound A+X occurs without outcome.

X can acquire inhibitory properties.

Canonical rule:

**conditioned inhibition is learned negative predictive information, not executive response inhibition.**

## 11. Conditioned inhibition != response inhibition

Conditioned inhibition concerns learned relations among cues/outcomes.

Response inhibition concerns suppression/cancellation of an action.

The two use "inhibition" at different levels of analysis.

Sosa's conceptual review explicitly distinguishes:

- inhibitory learning;
- conditioned inhibition;
- response inhibition;
- inhibitory control.

## 12. Summation test

A conditioned inhibitor should reduce responding when combined with an independently trained excitatory cue.

Example:

- B+ trained separately;
- test B versus B+X.

If X reduces responding to B, this supports transfer of inhibitory properties.

Canonical rule:

**summation tests generalization of inhibitory control to a novel excitor.**

## 13. Retardation test

A conditioned inhibitor should often be slower than a control cue to acquire excitatory properties when later paired with the outcome.

But retardation alone is ambiguous.

Simple nonreinforced pre-exposure can also slow later acquisition.

## 14. Conditioned inhibition != latent inhibition

### Latent inhibition

Prior nonreinforced exposure to a cue slows later learning about that cue.

### Conditioned inhibition

Cue acquires information that an expected outcome will not occur.

A cue can show retardation without possessing transferable conditioned-inhibitory properties.

## 15. Retardation alone cannot establish conditioned inhibition

Lovibond et al. found that conditioned-inhibition training produced stronger summation effects than matched latent-inhibition training, while retardation did not clearly distinguish them.

Therefore:

**retardation = conditioned inhibition**

is invalid.

A stronger case combines:

- appropriate inhibitory training;
- summation;
- retardation where interpretable;
- controls for pre-exposure/attention/configural effects.

## 16. Latent inhibition can itself acquire stronger inhibitory-like properties under special procedures

Human work in 2025 showed that compound pre-exposure with successive novel cues produced:

- enhanced retardation;
- successful summation-like inhibitory transfer.

This demonstrates that labels based solely on training history can be insufficient.

The functional properties of the learned cue must be tested.

## 17. Cue competition

When multiple cues predict the same outcome, learning/behavioral control may be unevenly distributed among cues.

Cue competition includes phenomena such as:

- blocking;
- overshadowing.

But competition is not guaranteed.

Effects vary with:

- salience;
- timing;
- attention;
- task representation;
- prior learning.

## 18. Blocking

Classic blocking:

1. A+ is learned.
2. AB+ is trained with the same expected outcome.
3. Learning about B is reduced relative to suitable controls.

Prediction-error account:

- A already predicts outcome;
- little outcome surprise remains;
- B acquires less associative strength.

Blocking is historically central evidence for error-correction learning.

## 19. Blocking is not a unique signature of prediction error

Blocking can also be explained/contributed to by:

- reduced attention to B;
- inferential reasoning that B adds no causal information;
- learned predictiveness;
- configural representation;
- explicit beliefs/instructions.

The 2025 explicit-description experiment is especially strong evidence against treating blocking as a uniquely low-level trial-wise phenomenon.

Canonical rule:

**blocking supports cue competition/predictive redundancy; it does not uniquely identify one learning algorithm.**

## 20. Blocking is not perfectly robust across every preparation

Recent literature continues to report failures and boundary conditions for blocking.

Therefore it should not be used as if:

`blocking always occurs whenever A already predicts O`.

Temporal structure, stimulus arrangement, instructions, and representation matter.

## 21. Overshadowing

In overshadowing, two novel cues are trained together:

`AB+`

but one cue later controls more behavior than the other, often because of greater:

- salience;
- attention;
- prior relevance.

Unlike blocking, neither cue necessarily had prior outcome training.

Canonical boundary:

**blocking != overshadowing.**

## 22. Salience can drive overshadowing

A preregistered 2024 human study found that emotionally valenced cues overshadowed neutral cues.

The effect scaled with differences in absolute emotional valence.

This supports attention/salience as one contributor to cue competition.

## 23. Cue competition is conditional, not universal

Research on rapid-streaming procedures shows:

- overshadowing in many conditions;
- no competition in others;
- interactions with timing/contiguity.

Therefore:

**one weakly learned cue in a compound does not prove a universal competitive-capacity mechanism.**

## 24. Attention can be both cause and consequence of learning

Learning can alter attention toward:

- predictive cues;
- surprising cues;
- salient cues.

Attention in turn changes subsequent learning.

This creates feedback:

`learning history -> attention -> new learning`.

Canonical rule:

**cue associability is dynamic.**

## 25. Learned predictiveness

Cues that previously predicted outcomes well may receive more attention/weight in later learning.

This can mimic or modify pure prediction-error effects.

Thus prior cue history matters even when current objective contingencies are matched.

## 26. Configural versus elemental representation

A compound AB can be represented as:

- separate elements A and B;
- a configural representation AB;
- a mixture of elemental/configural codes.

Blocking, overshadowing, summation, and inhibition predictions depend on representation.

Canonical rule:

**compound cue != guaranteed sum of independent elemental representations.**

## 27. Occasion setting is not simple conditioned inhibition

A cue may modulate whether another cue–outcome relation is active rather than directly predict outcome absence.

Negative occasion setting can therefore resemble conditioned inhibition behaviorally but differs representationally.

The dissociation between summation and retardation in human work is compatible with such hierarchical accounts.

## 28. Protection from extinction is theory-sensitive

If an inhibitor accompanies an excitatory cue during nonreinforcement, some elemental PE models predict reduced extinction of the excitatory cue because the outcome omission is already "explained."

Human causal-learning work has not always found the expected differential protection effect.

Canonical rule:

**failure or presence of protection-from-extinction effects should be treated as model evidence, not as a definition of inhibition.**

## 29. Pavlovian versus instrumental relations

### Pavlovian

Cue predicts outcome independent of the instrumental response.

### Instrumental

Action changes probability of outcome.

A response can be instrumentally goal-directed even while Pavlovian cues bias:

- vigor;
- direction;
- selection.

## 30. Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer (PIT)

PIT tests whether a Pavlovian cue changes instrumental responding.

Typical phases:

1. learn S–O relations;
2. separately learn A/R–O relations;
3. test action while Pavlovian cues are presented, usually without outcome delivery.

This isolates transfer more cleanly than training cue/action together.

## 31. General PIT

A reward-predictive cue can broadly increase instrumental response vigor.

General PIT does not require the cue and response to share the same specific outcome representation.

It may reflect general motivational value/arousal.

## 32. Outcome-specific PIT

A cue predicting Outcome 1 selectively increases the action associated with Outcome 1 more than an action associated with another outcome.

This supports access to outcome-specific information.

Canonical rule:

**general PIT != outcome-specific PIT.**

## 33. PIT is a robust family but methods are heterogeneous

The 2024 preregistered meta-analysis identified substantial variability in:

- task design;
- training;
- cue/outcome properties;
- transfer procedures;
- participant factors.

Therefore:

**PIT effect size is partly paradigm-dependent.**

## 34. Outcome-specific PIT is not pure automatic cue control in humans

Huseynzade et al. (2026) found outcome-specific PIT was:

- larger in participants reporting strategic cue use;
- reduced by directed-forgetting instructions;
- further attenuated under working-memory load;
- but not completely abolished.

This strongly supports mixed control involving explicit reward expectancies/beliefs plus residual cue-driven influence.

Canonical rule:

**PIT can contain both cognitively mediated and relatively persistent associative components.**

## 35. Explicit expectancy is evidence, not automatic proof of mechanism

If self-reported expectancy predicts PIT, this supports an inferential contribution.

But self-report can be:

- retrospective;
- incomplete;
- demand-sensitive.

Therefore expectancy should be manipulated/triangulated when possible.

## 36. Working-memory load is not a pure "automaticity test"

A load manipulation can disrupt:

- strategic rule use;
- maintenance of cue–outcome relations;
- attention;
- general performance.

Reduced PIT under load suggests cognitive-resource dependence but does not by itself locate the exact process.

## 37. Instructions can reverse or reduce cue use

Human participants can change responding when explicitly told:

- cues are irrelevant;
- contingencies reversed;
- previous learning should be ignored.

This constrains any theory claiming behavior is completely encapsulated from explicit knowledge.

## 38. Residual cue influence after instruction still matters

Conversely, incomplete elimination of cue effects under instructions/load constrains a pure conscious-propositional account.

Canonical rule:

**human associative behavior is often neither purely automatic nor purely propositional.**

## 39. Awareness in human fear conditioning

Contingency awareness means explicit recognition of CS–US relation.

A large 2026 study (N=895) found conditioned responding only among contingency-aware participants across its tested human fear-conditioning procedures.

A separate preregistered EEG study likewise found learning-related neural effects only in aware participants.

These findings strengthen the case that awareness is important in many human fear-conditioning paradigms.

## 40. Awareness findings should not be generalized to all associative learning

The strongest warranted conclusion is task-specific:

- many standard human fear-conditioning effects appear tightly linked to contingency awareness.

This does **not** establish:

- all associative learning requires conscious awareness;
- nonhuman conditioning depends on explicit awareness;
- every residual associative influence is conscious.

Canonical rule:

**awareness requirement is an empirical boundary, not a universal definition of association.**

## 41. Measuring awareness can itself alter learning

Online expectancy ratings, concurrent tasks, instructions, and awareness probes can change:

- attention;
- contingency learning;
- explicit knowledge.

The 2026 N=895 study showed awareness rates varied widely with experimental parameters.

Therefore:

**awareness measurement is not always observationally neutral.**

## 42. Conditioned response and explicit expectancy can dissociate partly

Different response systems can show different sensitivity to:

- contingency;
- instructions;
- extinction;
- awareness.

A conditioned response should not be treated as a direct readout of one hidden association without converging measures.

## 43. Associative and inferential levels can coexist

A useful human architecture is:

`experienced statistical structure`
-> `learned predictive representations`
↔ `explicit expectancies / causal interpretation`
-> `behavior`.

The interaction can be bidirectional.

This avoids the false dichotomy:

`association OR cognition`.

## 44. Associative model comparison requires representational assumptions

Two learning algorithms can produce similar behavior if they assume different:

- cue representations;
- outcome distributions;
- timing states;
- attention.

Likewise the same algorithm can make different predictions under different representations.

Canonical rule:

**algorithm and representation must be evaluated jointly.**

## 45. Behavioral prediction is not equivalent to mechanism identification

A model can accurately predict:

- learning curves;
- blocking;
- PIT;
- contingency judgments

without uniquely identifying the underlying psychological implementation.

Model comparison should ask:

- out-of-sample prediction;
- discriminating manipulations;
- parameter recovery;
- alternative representations;
- process convergence.

## 46. Associative Learning Inference Gate

Before making an associative-learning claim, ask:

### A. Relation

1. What exactly is associated: S–O, S–S, A/R–O, S–R, context–O?
2. Is the relation Pavlovian, instrumental, or mixed?
3. Is compound/configural representation possible?

### B. Contingency and timing

4. What is P(O|cue/action)?
5. What is P(O|no cue/action)?
6. What is the background outcome rate?
7. How close are cue/action and outcome?
8. What are trial/inter-event intervals?
9. Could timing rather than pair count explain learning?

### C. Prediction versus causation

10. Is the task asking prediction or causal judgment?
11. What prior causal knowledge/instructions exist?
12. Could explicit causal inference explain the response?

### D. Excitation/inhibition

13. Is a cue excitatory relative to a valid baseline?
14. What training makes a cue a candidate inhibitor?
15. Does it pass a summation test?
16. Does it pass a retardation test?
17. Could retardation reflect latent inhibition/pre-exposure instead?
18. Could occasion setting/configural control explain the pattern?

### E. Blocking

19. Was A learned before AB training?
20. Was B compared with a valid control?
21. Is the outcome truly already predicted?
22. Could reduced attention to B explain blocking?
23. Could causal inference/description explain it?
24. Is blocking robust under the exact timing/procedure?

### F. Overshadowing

25. Were A and B novel when compound training began?
26. Are salience/attention differences measured or manipulated?
27. Is weak B learning being called blocking incorrectly?
28. Could compound/configural encoding explain the effect?

### G. PIT

29. Were Pavlovian and instrumental relations learned separately?
30. Is transfer tested without new reinforcement?
31. Is PIT general or outcome-specific?
32. Are outcome expectancies measured?
33. Could explicit strategy explain transfer?
34. Does instruction/load manipulate the effect?
35. Does residual transfer remain?
36. Are task-design heterogeneity and reliability considered?

### H. Awareness

37. Is contingency awareness measured?
38. When/how is it measured?
39. Could the awareness measure alter learning?
40. Is a fear-conditioning awareness result being generalized beyond its paradigm?

### I. Models

41. What representation does the model assume?
42. Does it encode timing/background rates/outcome distributions?
43. Is prediction error one candidate among alternatives?
44. Are attention/inference/configural accounts compared?
45. Does model fit uniquely discriminate mechanisms?

### J. Individual inference

46. Is the effect replicated across sessions/tasks?
47. Could explicit knowledge, attention, memory, state, or strategy explain it?
48. Is a group conditioning phenomenon being converted into a personal trait?
49. What rival mechanism remains?
50. What manipulation best discriminates it?
51. What is the strongest warranted conclusion?
52. What wording would overclaim "automatic", "unconscious", "causal", or "inhibitory"?

## 47. High-value failure modes

### Failure A — paired = associated

Incorrect.

Correct:

Pairing must be evaluated against contingency/background/timing.

### Failure B — contingency = causality

Incorrect.

Correct:

Prediction and causal attribution are distinct human judgments.

### Failure C — conditioned inhibition = executive inhibition

Incorrect.

Correct:

Conditioned inhibition is learned negative predictive information.

### Failure D — retardation proves conditioned inhibition

Incorrect.

Correct:

Latent inhibition can also retard later excitatory learning.

### Failure E — low responding to X = X is an inhibitor

Incorrect.

Correct:

Inhibition requires relational tests such as summation with proper controls.

### Failure F — blocking = prediction error proven

Incorrect.

Correct:

PE, attention, causal inference, configural representation, and explicit descriptions can all contribute.

### Failure G — overshadowing = blocking

Incorrect.

Correct:

Blocking uses prior learning about one cue; overshadowing typically begins with novel compound cues.

### Failure H — cue competition always occurs

Incorrect.

Correct:

Competition varies with timing, salience, attention, representation, and procedure.

### Failure I — PIT = automatic cue control

Incorrect.

Correct:

Human PIT can depend strongly on explicit expectancies/WM while retaining residual cue influence.

### Failure J — PIT = explicit reasoning only

Incorrect.

Correct:

Residual effects after directed forgetting/load constrain a purely propositional account.

### Failure K — working-memory load proves unconscious automaticity

Incorrect.

Correct:

Load affects multiple cognitive processes and only demonstrates resource dependence.

### Failure L — explicit expectancy = causal mechanism proven

Incorrect.

Correct:

Expectancy is one observable/mediator candidate requiring manipulation and convergence.

### Failure M — aware conditioning result = all associative learning is conscious

Incorrect.

Correct:

Awareness findings are paradigm- and response-specific.

### Failure N — conditioned response = hidden association strength directly

Incorrect.

Correct:

Performance expression depends on response system, context, motivation, and measurement.

### Failure O — model fit = algorithm/representation identified

Incorrect.

Correct:

Algorithms and representations can trade off and remain observationally equivalent.

## Canonical working rule

**Specify the associative relation, establish contingency relative to background and timing, distinguish excitation from inhibition with valid transfer tests, separate blocking from overshadowing and PE from attention/inference alternatives, then test whether Pavlovian cues influence action through general motivation, outcome-specific representation, explicit expectancy, residual cue control, or a mixture.**

Never:

**pairing -> association; retardation -> inhibition; blocking -> PE; PIT -> automaticity.**

## Current-standard note

As of 2026-11-19:

- current Pavlovian-learning reviews emphasize temporal proximity, trial spacing, contingency/background rate, and uncertainty rather than simple pair counts;
- blocking remains a central cue-competition phenomenon, but current human evidence shows it can be produced/altered through explicit causal descriptions and is not a unique signature of one low-level PE algorithm;
- conditioned inhibition requires functional evidence beyond slow later learning because latent inhibition can also produce retardation;
- current human PIT literature is methodologically heterogeneous, and new 2026 experiments provide strong evidence that outcome-specific PIT often includes explicit expectancy/working-memory contributions while retaining a smaller residual cue-driven component;
- recent large/preregistered human fear-conditioning studies strengthen evidence that contingency awareness is important for many standard human fear-conditioning effects, but this result should not be generalized into a universal consciousness requirement for associative learning;
- contemporary associative theory increasingly treats learning algorithm, representation, timing, attention, and explicit inference as interacting rather than mutually exclusive explanatory levels.
