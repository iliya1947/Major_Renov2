# DEV-03 — Lifespan developmental transitions and aging: adolescence, adulthood, midlife, later life, role transitions, cognition, social development, healthy aging, and measurement

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public developmental-science evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

DEV-01 established that chronological age is not a mechanism and that developmental timing, maturation, cohort, period, practice, attrition, and measurement must be separated.

DEV-02 established that development is transactional, multilevel, probabilistic, and subject to equifinality/multifinality.

DEV-03 extends those rules across the full lifespan:

**How should adolescence, the transition to adulthood, midlife, retirement, and later-life aging be interpreted without turning age bands into natural kinds, normative transitions into universal scripts, or population-average cognitive/social trajectories into individual forecasts?**

High-risk errors to prevent:
- "adolescence is one fixed biological stage";
- "adolescent behavior is explained by an unfinished brain";
- "identity should be complete by adulthood";
- "18/21/25 marks psychological adulthood";
- "emerging adulthood is a universal stage";
- "leaving home/employment/marriage/parenthood defines adulthood universally";
- "later social adulthood means delayed psychological maturity";
- "midlife = crisis";
- "middle age has one universal age range";
- "more roles always increase stress";
- "retirement is harmful";
- "retirement is beneficial";
- "role exit = loss of identity";
- "older age = cognitive decline";
- "cognitive decline = dementia";
- "memory complaint = neurodegeneration";
- "healthy aging = absence of disease";
- "smaller social network = loneliness";
- "older adults are uniformly better at emotion regulation";
- "fluid abilities decline, therefore overall competence declines";
- "crystallized knowledge is preserved in everyone";
- "cross-sectional age curve = longitudinal aging";
- "longitudinal aging curve is unbiased by practice/attrition";
- "terminal decline = normal chronological aging";
- "average older-adult trajectory = personal prognosis";
- "age-related difference = irreversible";
- "super-aging is a distinct biological type";
- "one successful-aging score defines healthy aging".

## Core rule

**Lifespan development is multidirectional and heterogeneous. Age-linked transitions reflect interacting biological maturation, accumulated learning, social roles, institutions, historical context, health, and person–environment transactions. Developmental periods are useful descriptive regions, not universal mechanisms. Healthy aging concerns functional ability within an environment, not simply absence of disease, and age-related population trends must be separated from pathology, cohort/period effects, selective survival, practice, and individual trajectory variation.**

Canonical reasoning:

\`define life phase/transition and exact process -> identify biological, social, institutional, and historical clocks -> separate normative age trend from role/event/cohort/period effects -> measure within-person trajectory and functional consequences -> distinguish healthy variation from impairment/pathology -> model practice, attrition, survival and measurement equivalence -> preserve multidirectionality and individual heterogeneity -> state only the narrowest lifespan-development claim\`.

## 1. Lifespan development is not a staircase

Development does not consist of universally ordered steps toward one endpoint.

Across adulthood, different functions can:
- improve;
- stabilize;
- decline;
- reorganize;
- compensate.

One domain can improve while another declines.

Thus:
**older = globally worse**
and
**older = globally wiser**
are both invalid simplifications.

## 2. Gains and losses coexist

Lifespan approaches emphasize simultaneous:
- gains;
- maintenance;
- losses;
- compensation.

Examples:
- accumulated knowledge can increase while processing speed declines;
- emotional priorities can change while health burden rises;
- role expertise can increase while some novel-learning tasks become harder.

Development is multidirectional.

## 3. Developmental periods are descriptive abstractions

Common labels:
- childhood;
- adolescence;
- emerging/young adulthood;
- midlife;
- later adulthood.

They summarize recurring combinations of:
- biology;
- roles;
- institutions;
- expectations.

They are not homogeneous natural kinds.

## 4. Boundaries of life stages vary

Stage boundaries differ across:
- disciplines;
- cultures;
- historical periods;
- institutions.

A legal age threshold is not automatically:
- biological threshold;
- cognitive threshold;
- social-role threshold.

## 5. Age norms can become self-fulfilling institutions

Expectations about what people "should" do at certain ages can influence:
- opportunity;
- hiring;
- education;
- family roles;
- self-perception.

Observed age patterns can therefore partly reflect institutional age norms.

## 6. Adolescence is a transition-rich period

Adolescence includes changes in:
- puberty;
- cognition;
- autonomy;
- identity;
- peer ecology;
- schooling;
- family relationships.

These processes do not start or end simultaneously.

## 7. Adolescence is not one synchronized biological stage

DEV-01 already separated:
- chronological age;
- pubertal status;
- timing;
- tempo.

Adolescent developmental inference should therefore specify which transition is relevant.

## 8. Adolescence can create continuity and discontinuity

A 2024 lifespan perspective emphasizes that adolescence:
- carries forward childhood characteristics and contexts;
- also introduces transitions that can alter trajectories.

It can function as a turning point.

It is not simply a continuation or a universal reset.

## 9. Turning point ≠ guaranteed transformation

A school transition, pubertal transition, or new peer ecology can alter a trajectory.

But:
- effects vary;
- prior constraints remain;
- later contexts matter.

"Turning point" is a probability-changing opportunity, not fate.

## 10. Adolescent identity is dynamic

A 2026 review characterizes identity development as involving:
- commitments;
- exploration;
- reconsideration;
- personal and social identity processes;
- variation across domains and time.

Identity development is cyclic/dynamic rather than one final adolescent decision.

## 11. Identity consolidation is not identity completion

Adulthood can include:
- career identity revision;
- relationship-role change;
- cultural/social identity change;
- value revision.

Therefore:
**adult = fixed identity**
is invalid.

## 12. Identity content is domain-specific

A person can be:
- committed educationally;
- uncertain occupationally;
- stable relationally;
- changing politically/socially.

One identity-status label should not be generalized across all domains.

## 13. Adolescent risk-taking should not be reduced to "immature brain"

Risk-related behavior can depend on:
- peers;
- reward context;
- opportunity;
- norms;
- emotion;
- experience;
- control demands.

Neural-development descriptions do not replace behavioral/contextual explanation.

Detailed neural mechanisms route to NEU.

## 14. Transition to adulthood is multidimensional

Markers can include:
- biological maturity;
- legal status;
- leaving education;
- entering employment;
- financial independence;
- leaving parental home;
- partnership;
- parenthood;
- subjective adult identity.

No single marker defines psychological adulthood universally.

## 15. Biological and social transitions are diverging historically

A 2025 review concludes that:
- biological maturation has tended to occur earlier;
- several socioeconomic adult-role transitions occur later;
- legal markers often remain fixed.

This creates longer intervals between biological maturity and some social-adult roles.

## 16. Later social transition ≠ delayed psychological development

Delayed:
- parenthood;
- stable employment;
- independent household

can reflect:
- education;
- housing markets;
- labor conditions;
- culture.

Do not interpret structural timing as personal immaturity.

## 17. "Emerging adulthood" is context-sensitive

The concept can be useful for describing periods of:
- exploration;
- instability;
- partial independence

in some societies/populations.

It should not be treated as a biologically mandatory stage from 18–29.

## 18. Emerging-adulthood markers differ by culture and cohort

The meaning of:
- leaving home;
- marriage;
- employment;
- financial independence

depends on cultural/institutional context.

Thus the same age can imply different developmental roles across societies.

## 19. Transition to adulthood can be nonlinear

People can:
- leave and return home;
- enter and leave education;
- change jobs;
- enter and exit relationships.

Adult-role transitions are often reversible/nonlinear.

Stage models should allow reversals.

## 20. 2026 generational transition evidence

Recent longitudinal population evidence shows that pathways through emerging-adult transitions differ across generations.

This supports:
**transition structure is historically contingent**.

A cohort-specific role sequence should not be used as a universal developmental standard.

## 21. Subjective adulthood is distinct from objective role status

A person can feel adult without:
- marriage;
- parenthood;
- home ownership.

Another can occupy those roles without strong subjective adult identity.

Subjective identity and demographic marker are different constructs.

## 22. Early adulthood contains multiple concurrent clocks

Relevant clocks can include:
- time since education completion;
- time in career;
- relationship duration;
- financial independence;
- biological age.

Chronological age can be a poor proxy for role experience.

## 23. Career development is nonlinear

A 2026 review emphasizes career transitions as:
- dynamic;
- identity-relevant;
- increasingly nonlinear.

Professional identity can be revised repeatedly.

A career change should not be interpreted as developmental failure.

## 24. Role transition ≠ role replacement

A new role can coexist with existing roles:
- worker;
- caregiver;
- partner;
- parent;
- student.

Development in adulthood often involves role accumulation/reconfiguration.

## 25. Role accumulation is not uniformly beneficial or harmful

Multiple roles can provide:
- resources;
- meaning;
- social connection.

They can also create:
- time pressure;
- conflict;
- overload.

Effect depends on:
- role quality;
- voluntariness;
- resources;
- conflict.

## 26. Midlife is not one fixed age band

Operational definitions vary.

Midlife is often characterized by combinations of:
- established work roles;
- caregiving;
- health transitions;
- intergenerational responsibilities.

Age boundaries are pragmatic rather than biologically exact.

## 27. Midlife is not inherently a crisis

"Midlife crisis" should not be assumed from age alone.

Midlife can contain:
- gains;
- stability;
- stressors;
- transitions.

Individual and cohort variation are substantial.

## 28. Midlife can be a role-density period

A 2026 cross-national review highlights that middle-aged adults often balance:
- work;
- children;
- aging parents;
- community obligations.

This role density can create both:
- competence/resources;
- stress/time pressure.

## 29. Midlife development is historically changing

The 2026 cross-national review found recent U.S. midlife trends including:
- more loneliness/depressive symptoms;
- worse memory/physical-health indicators.

Comparable trends were not consistently observed in peer countries.

Therefore:
**midlife age effect ≠ historical midlife effect**.

## 30. Cross-national differences block universal midlife narratives

If age patterns differ across countries/cohorts, possible contributors include:
- healthcare;
- labor markets;
- family policy;
- inequality;
- culture;
- historical shocks.

Developmental age alone is insufficient.

## 31. Midlife cognition is multidirectional

Some accumulated abilities can remain strong or improve while:
- speed;
- novel reasoning

may show earlier decline.

A single "cognitive peak" is not a universal property of all cognitive domains.

## 32. Composite "peak age" depends on weighting

A 2025 paper combined cognitive/personality dimensions into a weighted composite and reported a midlife peak.

This is a model-dependent summary.

Changing:
- included dimensions;
- weights;
- criterion

can move the estimated peak.

Do not treat one composite peak as a biological optimum age.

## 33. Adulthood role events interact with personality and context

PER showed that:
- work;
- marriage;
- divorce;
- retirement-related transitions

can show small average personality changes.

DEV interprets these as:
- developmental contexts;
not universal age-stage mechanisms.

## 34. Caregiving in adulthood is heterogeneous

Caregiving can vary by:
- relationship;
- intensity;
- duration;
- voluntariness;
- available support.

"Caring for aging parents" is not one standardized developmental exposure.

## 35. Empty nest is not one universal outcome

Children leaving home can be associated with:
- relief;
- loss;
- relationship change;
- little effect.

Outcome depends on:
- role investment;
- relationship quality;
- cultural expectations;
- economic context.

Avoid one-direction stage narratives.

## 36. Retirement is a transition, not a developmental endpoint

Retirement alters:
- work demands;
- income;
- routine;
- identity;
- social network;
- autonomy.

Its psychological effects are conditional.

## 37. Retirement can be risk or relief

A 2025 longitudinal study:
- N=5,913;
- nine annual waves;
- ages 60–74

found average post-retirement improvements across several health/well-being domains.

But effects varied with:
- retirement age;
- pre-retirement job satisfaction;
- voluntariness.

## 38. Retirement voluntariness matters

Involuntary retirement can involve:
- lost control;
- financial stress;
- health constraints.

Voluntary retirement can involve:
- relief;
- goal pursuit.

Do not infer effect from role exit alone.

## 39. Pre-retirement work context matters

Leaving:
- highly satisfying work
can differ from leaving:
- stressful or low-control work.

Same retirement status can represent different developmental transitions.

## 40. Retirement is family-system relevant

A 2026 scoping review emphasizes that retirement can affect:
- spouse/partner dynamics;
- household routines;
- family role allocation.

Retirement should not be modeled only as an individual event.

## 41. Later adulthood is highly heterogeneous

At the same chronological age, people can differ greatly in:
- health;
- cognition;
- mobility;
- social engagement;
- functional independence.

WHO explicitly rejects the idea of a "typical older person."

## 42. Healthy aging is not absence of disease

WHO defines healthy ageing around:
**developing and maintaining functional ability that enables well-being in older age**.

A person can have chronic disease and still experience healthy aging if functional ability is preserved/supportable.

## 43. Functional ability is person × environment

WHO's framework distinguishes:
- intrinsic capacity;
- environment;
- their interaction.

Thus functional outcome cannot be inferred from individual capacity alone.

## 44. Intrinsic capacity is multidimensional

Intrinsic capacity includes mental and physical capacities.

Current frameworks include domains such as:
- cognition;
- locomotion;
- vitality;
- sensory capacities;
- psychological capacity.

Exact operationalization remains under development.

## 45. Psychological capacity in healthy aging

A 2026 international working group defined psychological capacity as psychological attributes, assets, and skills contributing to functional ability, including:
- appraisal;
- emotion regulation;
- adaptability;
- coping;
- social competence;
- agency.

This is a framework for measurement development, not a single validated score.

## 46. Healthy aging measurement remains fragmented

A systematic review of 55 healthy-aging studies found substantial heterogeneity across:
- intrinsic capacity;
- functional ability;
- environment.

No one current measure captures the construct perfectly.

## 47. Healthy aging ≠ successful-aging perfection

Criteria requiring:
- no disease;
- high cognition;
- high activity

can exclude many people who maintain meaningful functional ability despite illness/disability.

WHO's functional approach is less binary.

## 48. Cognitive aging is multidimensional

Cognitive domains include:
- processing speed;
- episodic memory;
- working memory;
- reasoning;
- language;
- crystallized knowledge;
- executive functions.

They do not share one identical age trajectory.

## 49. Cognitive aging is heterogeneous

A 2024 neurocognitive-aging review emphasizes:
- average decline in many domains;
- substantial interindividual heterogeneity.

A 2026 healthy-cognitive-aging review similarly argues chronological age alone is a poor predictor of cognitive health.

## 50. Fluid and crystallized abilities differ

Systematic review evidence generally finds:
- fluid/novel processing and explicit learning/memory more vulnerable to aging;
- crystallized knowledge and some implicit learning relatively more stable.

This is an average pattern, not an individual law.

## 51. Processing speed often changes earlier

Cross-sectional and longitudinal literatures commonly find earlier decline in:
- speeded processing;
- some fluid abilities.

But exact onset depends on:
- measure;
- design;
- cohort;
- practice.

"Decline begins at age X" should be used cautiously.

## 52. Memory does not decline uniformly

Different memory systems:
- episodic;
- semantic;
- procedural/implicit;
- working memory

show different trajectories.

A global "memory declines with age" statement is too coarse.

## 53. 2026 pooled longitudinal cognitive evidence

A pooled analysis:
- N=14,389;
- three cohorts across Asia, North America, and Europe

reported earlier detectable decline in several non-memory domains and later detectable decline in memory.

Individual trajectories remained highly heterogeneous.

This supports domain asynchrony, not a universal onset age.

## 54. Cross-sectional cognitive aging can overstate or distort change

Cross-sectional comparisons can include:
- cohort education differences;
- health selection;
- historical test familiarity.

They do not directly measure within-person decline.

## 55. Longitudinal cognitive aging can be optimistic because of practice

Repeated testing can produce:
- familiarity;
- strategy learning;
- reduced anxiety.

DEV-01 established that practice effects can be large enough to resemble years of age difference.

## 56. Practice effects can also carry information

A 2026 longitudinal study of cognitively healthy middle-aged/older adults found:
- generic retest gains;
- individual practice-effect differences associated with cognitive level and some longitudinal outcomes.

Therefore:
**practice effect = pure nuisance**
is also too simple.

## 57. Attrition biases cognitive-aging samples

An IPD meta-analysis in adults 50+ found:
- average wave-to-wave dropout ~26%;
- people who later dropped out had lower prior cognition;
- overall difference about d=.37.

Remaining samples can therefore look cognitively healthier than the source population.

## 58. Selective survival compounds attrition

Older participants available for testing are selected by:
- survival;
- health;
- mobility;
- participation capacity.

This can flatten apparent late-life decline or distort variability.

## 59. Rank-order cognitive stability is not absence of decline

A 2024 meta-analysis documents substantial rank-order stability in cognitive abilities across longitudinal studies.

People can retain relative ordering while mean performance declines.

PER's stability/change distinction applies to cognition too.

## 60. Cognitive decline is not dementia

NIA explicitly distinguishes:
- common age-related mild forgetfulness;
- mild cognitive impairment;
- dementia.

Dementia is not a normal inevitable part of aging.

## 61. Mild cognitive impairment is not inevitable dementia

MCI involves greater cognitive difficulty than expected for age while often preserving everyday independence.

Not everyone with MCI progresses to Alzheimer's disease/dementia.

Therefore:
**MCI = future dementia**
is invalid.

## 62. Memory complaint is not a diagnosis

Subjective memory concern can reflect:
- normal aging;
- mood;
- sleep;
- medication;
- medical conditions;
- cognitive impairment.

Clinical diagnosis requires appropriate assessment.

Clinical routing belongs to CLN/NEU.

## 63. Functional impairment matters clinically

A key boundary between normal aging, MCI, and dementia involves:
- severity;
- trajectory;
- interference with everyday functioning.

Age-adjusted lower performance alone is not dementia.

## 64. Cognitive aging ≠ neurodegeneration by definition

Normal age-related change can occur without:
- Alzheimer's disease;
- another neurodegenerative disorder.

Neural mechanism claims require clinical/biomarker evidence.

## 65. Cognitive maintenance exists

Some older adults preserve relatively strong cognitive performance.

This supports large individual heterogeneity.

It does not establish a binary "super-ager" natural type.

## 66. "Super-aging" definitions vary

Different studies define exceptional cognition using:
- age thresholds;
- memory cutoffs;
- younger-reference performance;
- multidomain criteria.

Thus prevalence/mechanisms depend on definition.

## 67. Reserve/maintenance/compensation are distinct ideas

Potential aging frameworks include:
- reserve;
- brain maintenance;
- compensation.

They should not be used interchangeably.

Detailed neural evidence routes to NEU.

## 68. Social aging is not simply social loss

Social networks can become:
- smaller;
- more selective;
- differently composed.

Network size and loneliness are different constructs.

## 69. Social network narrowing can be selective

A 2026 longitudinal midlife-to-late-adulthood study found:
- peripheral relationships declined more than close relationships over ~15 years.

This is consistent with selective network reorganization.

It does not prove all narrowing is adaptive.

## 70. Smaller network ≠ loneliness

Loneliness concerns perceived mismatch between desired and actual connection.

A small close network can be satisfying.
A large network can coexist with loneliness.

Do not use network count as a direct loneliness measure.

## 71. Historical trends in loneliness are not pure aging effects

A 2026 cross-temporal meta-analysis:
- 125 studies;
- N=107,304;
- 1997–2025

examined historical changes in older-adult loneliness.

This illustrates that later-life social outcomes have:
- age;
- cohort;
- period;
- societal components.

## 72. Socioemotional selectivity is a theory, not an age law

Socioemotional selectivity theory proposes that perceived time horizons can shift goals toward:
- emotionally meaningful relationships;
- immediate emotional value.

Chronological age often correlates with time perspective, but age is not identical to mechanism.

## 73. Older age does not guarantee better emotion regulation

A 2026 systematic review of emotion regulation in work/health contexts found:
- some age differences;
- null/reversed findings;
- no single consistent older-adult advantage.

Therefore:
**older = better regulator**
is too broad.

## 74. Situational context remains relevant in later-life regulation

Experience-sampling evidence across ages 18–87 shows:
- tactic use varies with situation;
- age moderates some context-specific choices.

Age effects remain person × situation effects.

## 75. Psychological aging is multidimensional

A 2025 review describes psychological aging as:
- multidimensional;
- multidirectional;
- highly variable.

No single aging index should be treated as a complete psychological-age measure.

## 76. Subjective age is not biological age

People differ in:
- how old they feel;
- attitudes toward aging;
- perceived future time.

Subjective aging can predict well-being/behavior.

It is not direct evidence of biological aging rate.

## 77. Age stereotypes can affect outcomes

Ageism and internalized age expectations can influence:
- opportunity;
- behavior;
- self-efficacy;
- social treatment.

Observed older-age differences can partly reflect social context.

## 78. Historical context can alter aging trajectories

Education, healthcare, technology, work, and policy change across cohorts.

An age trajectory estimated in one generation may not transport to another.

## 79. Culture shapes cognitive and social aging

A 2026 review argues cognitive-neuroscience-of-aging research remains culturally narrow.

Cognitive performance and aging trajectories can reflect:
- language;
- education;
- cultural practices;
- test familiarity.

Do not universalize Western norms.

## 80. Terminal decline is distinct from ordinary age trends

Some functions change more steeply as death approaches.

Distance-to-death models can sometimes explain late-life decline beyond chronological age.

This is not identical to "normal aging."

## 81. Psychological/social terminal decline

A 2025 longitudinal study found estimated terminal phases beginning years before death for:
- purpose;
- cognitive activity;
- social activity;
- depressive symptoms;
- loneliness.

The timing differed by outcome.

Thus:
**one terminal-decline onset**
does not exist.

## 82. Functional terminal decline is multidomain

A 2026 multicohort study:
- N=79,305;
- 10-year follow-up

found decedents showed steeper decline in:
- physical functioning;
- cognition;
- to a lesser degree psychological functioning.

Terminal trajectories were heterogeneous.

## 83. Distance to death can confound age effects

Older groups contain more people closer to death.

An apparent late-age decline can partly reflect:
- terminal processes;
- disease burden;
- selective survival.

Age and distance-to-death should be distinguished where relevant.

## 84. Healthy aging remains possible with impairment

WHO's framework focuses on:
- what people can be/do;
- supportive environments.

Assistive technology, social support, and accessible environments can preserve functional ability despite capacity loss.

## 85. Environment can compensate for intrinsic-capacity decline

A 2026 study found functional ability over six years related to:
- intrinsic-capacity domains;
- environmental features;
- their interactions.

This reinforces:
**capacity ≠ function**.

## 86. Psychological capacity is not a single trait

The 2026 MOSAIC consensus includes:
- appraisal;
- affect/regulation;
- adaptability;
- coping;
- social competence;
- agency.

These should not be compressed into one "psychological age" score without validation.

## 87. Successful/healthy aging scores are model-dependent

Different frameworks emphasize:
- disease;
- cognition;
- activity;
- autonomy;
- functional ability;
- wellbeing.

Classification rates can change with criteria.

Always state the definition.

## 88. Population trajectory ≠ individual prognosis

Even robust average age curves leave substantial:
- individual variation;
- cohort variation;
- health-context variation.

Individual prognosis requires:
- baseline level;
- slope uncertainty;
- health;
- environment;
- repeated measurement.

## 89. Age-normed score ≠ developmental mechanism

Age norms indicate relative performance within an age group.

They do not explain:
- why the score changed;
- whether decline is pathological;
- what mechanism is active.

Norming is measurement, not causal explanation.

## 90. Role transition ≠ age effect

Career change, retirement, caregiving, and partnership transitions correlate with age.

Their effects should be modeled by:
- event timing;
- voluntariness;
- role quality;
- resources.

Chronological age should not absorb the transition.

## 91. Healthy aging intervention evidence needs functional outcomes

An intervention can improve:
- one test;
- one symptom;
- one activity measure.

Healthy-aging claims require links to:
- functional ability;
- well-being;
- meaningful outcomes.

Test improvement alone is not healthy aging.

## 92. Cognitive intervention effects do not erase aging

A 2026 systematic review/Bayesian meta-analysis found cognitive interventions can improve some cognitive outcomes in healthy aging.

This supports plasticity.

It does not imply:
- prevention of dementia;
- reversal of biological aging;
- broad transfer.

## 94. Failure modes

A. Life stage = fixed biological kind: **invalid**.
B. Adolescence = unfinished brain explanation: **reductionist**.
C. Adult identity = completed identity: **invalid**.
D. Legal age = psychological adulthood: **invalid**.
E. Emerging adulthood = universal stage: **unsupported universalization**.

## 95. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- lifespan development is multidirectional and heterogeneous;
- developmental stages are descriptive regions rather than universal mechanisms;
- biological and social markers of adulthood are nonidentical and historically changing;
- emerging adulthood is context-dependent rather than a biologically fixed universal stage;
- midlife is not inherently a crisis;
- retirement effects depend on timing, voluntariness, work, health, and resources;
- cognitive domains show different aging trajectories;
- cognitive aging is heterogeneous and is not synonymous with dementia;
- dementia is not normal/inevitable aging;
- healthy aging centers on functional ability and person–environment interaction;
- practice, attrition, selective survival, cohort, and terminal decline can distort age trajectories;
- population age trends are not individual prognoses.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- adolescence can act as a turning point with continuity and discontinuity;
- identity development remains dynamic across adolescence/adulthood;
- midlife role density can create resources and strain;
- some retirement transitions improve well-being;
- fluid abilities tend to decline earlier than crystallized abilities;
- social networks can become more selective with age;
- terminal decline can begin years before death in several psychological/social domains;
- supportive environments can compensate for reductions in intrinsic capacity.

### Not established as universal
- one age of psychological adulthood;
- one emerging-adult stage across cultures;
- one midlife peak/crisis;
- one retirement effect;
- one age at which cognition begins to decline;
- one normal cognitive-aging trajectory;
- one social-network trajectory;
- one emotional-regulation advantage in older age;
- one terminal-decline onset;
- one successful/healthy-aging phenotype.
