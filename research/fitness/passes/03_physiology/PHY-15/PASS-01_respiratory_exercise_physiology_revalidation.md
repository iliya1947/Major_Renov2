## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a bounded respiratory-exercise model sufficient for later bioenergetics, conditioning, testing, technique/breathing and safety decisions without:

- minute ventilation equated with alveolar ventilation;
- tidal volume or breathing frequency treated in isolation;
- pulmonary gas exchange equated with whole-body oxygen delivery;
- oxygen uptake inferred from ventilation alone;
- CO2 removal reduced to breathing frequency;
- ventilatory thresholds treated as one literal biochemical switch;
- exercise hyperpnea reduced to one controller;
- respiratory muscle work treated as negligible;
- respiratory muscle fatigue assumed in every workout;
- routine endurance training assumed to enlarge/upgrade the lungs in proportion to VO2max;
- inspiratory-muscle-training effects generalized to ordinary exercise training;
- exercise desaturation or dyspnea automatically labeled normal physiology.

### Question type

Integrative respiratory physiology during resistance and aerobic exercise plus chronic training adaptation.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 11 exact Section 16 items must be covered at content level; VE/VT/frequency/alveolar ventilation must remain distinct; pulmonary gas exchange must be separated from systemic O2 transport; CO2-removal/VE-VCO2 logic must be explicit; respiratory muscle work/fatigue and healthy-system limitations must be bounded; acute vs chronic adaptation and clinical dyspnea boundaries must remain explicit.

## 3. Ventilation

Minute ventilation:

`VE = VT × fB`

where:

- `VT` = tidal volume;
- `fB` = breathing frequency.

During dynamic exercise, VE rises substantially with metabolic demand.

Important distinction:

`alveolar ventilation ≈ (VT - VD) × fB`

where `VD` is physiological dead-space volume.

Therefore two people/sessions can have the same minute ventilation but different effective alveolar ventilation and gas exchange.

Project rule:

**minute ventilation is total expired/inspired flow per minute; it is not identical to alveolar ventilation.**

---

## 4. Tidal volume

VT generally rises from rest through low-to-moderate exercise as deeper breaths increase effective alveolar ventilation.

At higher ventilatory demand, VT expansion becomes increasingly constrained by:

- lung/chest-wall mechanics;
- inspiratory capacity;
- expiratory flow limits;
- the increasing mechanical cost of taking larger breaths.

Further VE increase then depends increasingly on breathing frequency.

The exact VT trajectory varies by:

- body size;
- sex;
- age;
- exercise mode;
- posture;
- fitness;
- ventilatory mechanics.

Project rule:

**VT and breathing frequency jointly create VE; neither should be used as a stand-alone respiratory-load metric.**

---

## 5. Breathing frequency

Breathing frequency rises with exercise intensity and becomes an increasingly important component of VE at high work rates.

It is influenced by more than blood gases alone, including:

- central/feedforward exercise drive;
- chemoreflex inputs;
- locomotor/respiratory afferent feedback;
- mechanical constraints;
- behavioral/volitional breathing;
- exercise rhythm and mode.

The 2026 age-span study also shows that breathing pattern differs systematically with age; older adults shifted toward a more frequency-dominant exercise pattern.

Project rule:

**exercise breathing frequency is regulated and context dependent, not a direct one-to-one readout of metabolic rate.**

---

## 6. Gas exchange

Pulmonary gas exchange requires integration of:

1. alveolar ventilation;
2. pulmonary perfusion;
3. ventilation-perfusion matching;
4. alveolar-capillary diffusion;
5. sufficient transit/contact conditions;
6. blood oxygen/CO2 carrying properties.

In healthy exercise, ventilation and pulmonary blood flow rise together so arterial O2 and CO2 pressures are usually defended within a relatively narrow physiological range.

At very high endurance demand, this reserve can become less complete.

Project rule:

**gas exchange is a ventilation + perfusion + diffusion problem, not a ventilation-only problem.**

---

## 7. Oxygen transport

Pulmonary O2 uptake is only one segment of the oxygen-transport cascade.

A bounded project model is:

`inspired O2 → alveolar ventilation → alveolar-capillary diffusion/V-Q matching → arterial O2 content → cardiac output → tissue perfusion/diffusion → mitochondrial use`.

Arterial O2 content depends predominantly on hemoglobin-bound oxygen, with only a small dissolved component.

Therefore:

- normal ventilation does not guarantee high systemic O2 delivery;
- high VE does not prove high VO2;
- pulmonary gas exchange cannot be separated from hemoglobin and cardiac output when interpreting whole-body aerobic capacity.

Ownership boundary:

- pulmonary loading/diffusion/V-Q → PHY-15;
- cardiac output/blood flow → PHY-14;
- metabolism/utilization → BIO;
- measurement/CPET interpretation → TST.

Project rule:

**oxygen transport is a serial cascade; the lung is one conductance step rather than the whole system.**

---

## 8. CO2 removal

CO2 elimination depends on CO2 production and effective alveolar ventilation.

Exercise increases `VCO2`, requiring increased ventilation to defend arterial CO2.

Ventilatory efficiency is commonly represented by the `VE/VCO2` relationship, but interpretation depends on:

- physiological dead space;
- V/Q matching;
- arterial CO2 regulation/set point;
- exercise phase and analysis method.

Above the heavy/intense transition and especially near the respiratory compensation region, VE can rise disproportionately relative to VO2/VCO2 because acid-base disturbance and additional ventilatory drive increase.

Project rule:

**CO2 removal is governed by production + alveolar ventilation + gas-exchange efficiency; breathing frequency alone does not quantify it.**

---

## 9. Ventilatory response to exercise

Exercise hyperpnea is multi-controller.

Current evidence supports contributions from:

- central/feedforward exercise command;
- locomotor-muscle afferents;
- respiratory-muscle afferents;
- lung stretch/mechanical feedback;
- carotid/body chemoreflexes;
- CO2/H+ related feedback;
- temperature and other contextual inputs.

The 2026 exercise-hyperpnea work reinforces that the exact weighting of feedforward and CO2 feedback changes with exercise intensity and remains mechanistically unresolved.

Project rule:

**there is no single accepted “exercise ventilation sensor.”**

### Ventilatory thresholds

VT1/VT2/RCP-type landmarks are useful exercise-response constructs.

They should not be interpreted as one instantaneous biochemical switch.

Their values depend on:

- protocol;
- signal processing;
- criteria;
- gas-exchange noise;
- physiology of buffering/CO2 handling.

Detailed threshold testing → TST/GPP/PRO.

---

## 10. Respiratory muscle work

The diaphragm and accessory inspiratory/expiratory muscles perform real mechanical work during exercise.

As ventilation rises:

- inspiratory pressure generation increases;
- expiratory muscles become progressively active at high ventilation;
- the oxygen/blood-flow cost of breathing rises;
- respiratory muscles compete within the integrated circulation.

Respiratory muscle work is therefore not metabolically free.

Mechanical breathing changes also influence intrathoracic pressure and cardiovascular function, linking PHY-15 directly with PHY-14.

Project rule:

**the respiratory pump has a meaningful energetic and circulatory cost during demanding exercise.**

---

## 11. Respiratory muscle fatigue

Recent human studies using twitch-pressure methods show that both inspiratory and expiratory muscle fatigue can develop during severe-intensity cycling and can appear well before task failure.

However:

- magnitude depends on intensity and tolerable duration;
- it is not inevitable in every exercise session;
- respiratory fatigue is not equivalent to subjective breathlessness;
- fatigue measured after severe cycling should not be generalized to low-demand resistance work.

High respiratory muscle work can activate an inspiratory-muscle metaboreflex and alter blood-flow distribution, but exact performance impact varies by population/protocol.

Project rule:

**respiratory muscle fatigue is a real high-demand limitation, not a universal feature of all training.**

---

## 12. Acute respiratory response to aerobic exercise

Integrated model:

`metabolic demand ↑ → VO2/VCO2 ↑ → neural/chemical ventilatory drive ↑ → VT + fB ↑ → VE/alveolar ventilation ↑ → pulmonary gas exchange maintained`.

Across increasing intensity:

1. VT and fB both contribute early.
2. VT expansion becomes progressively constrained.
3. fB contributes increasingly to high VE.
4. VE tracks VCO2 over a broad range.
5. above ventilatory/respiratory-compensation regions, VE rises disproportionately.
6. respiratory muscle work rises substantially.

In most healthy untrained/moderately trained adults, pulmonary reserve is sufficient for ordinary exercise.

At very high endurance demands, limitation can emerge.

---

## 13. Acute respiratory response to resistance exercise

Resistance exercise is intermittent rather than steady-state, so breath-by-breath respiratory response depends strongly on:

- set duration;
- repetitions;
- muscle mass;
- metabolic accumulation;
- rest periods;
- breathing strategy;
- breath holding/Valsalva;
- BFR/occlusion protocol.

In the 2026 squat crossover study, breath holding reduced VE and VO2 during the active task and produced a postexercise ventilatory overshoot.

A 2026 BFR crossover trial found lower VE, VO2 and VCO2 during low-load BFR than during the compared high-load protocol, illustrating that respiratory demand is protocol-specific and cannot be inferred from “BFR” or %1RM alone.

Project rule:

**RT ventilation is intermittent and breathing-strategy dependent; steady-state aerobic rules cannot be copied directly.**

---

## 14. Chronic adaptation to ordinary exercise training

Chronic exercise training greatly increases whole-body aerobic capacity through cardiovascular, hematological, muscular and metabolic adaptations.

The lung/airway system is different:

- standard resting spirometric volumes/flows are not established as the primary trainable mechanism of VO2max improvement;
- athlete populations often show high lung-function/diffusing-capacity metrics, but cross-sectional data cannot separate training, selection, body size and sport exposure;
- the respiratory system can nonetheless support higher absolute ventilation because the trained person can sustain higher metabolic workloads;
- breathing pattern/ventilatory economy at a given absolute task can differ after training, partly because the same task represents a lower relative metabolic demand.

Project rule:

**better aerobic fitness does not imply proportional structural enlargement or spirometric improvement of the healthy lungs.**

---

## 15. Respiratory-muscle-specific training

Inspiratory/respiratory muscle training is a distinct intervention from ordinary endurance or resistance training.

Current 2026 evidence suggests:

- inspiratory muscle strength can improve;
- diaphragm thickness can increase with targeted IMT;
- selected performance outcomes may improve.

But the athlete meta-analysis rates much of this evidence low to very low certainty because of:

- small samples;
- heterogeneity;
- risk of bias;
- nonuniform performance tests;
- sensitivity to influential studies.

Spirometric improvements were not robust in sensitivity analysis.

Project rule:

**targeted IMT can train respiratory muscles, but its effects cannot be used as proof that ordinary exercise training meaningfully increases lung volumes or that IMT universally improves performance.**

---

## 16. Respiratory-system limitations in healthy exercise

For most healthy non-elite adults, the respiratory system usually has substantial reserve.

Limitations become more plausible when demand approaches or exceeds available mechanical/gas-exchange capacity.

Documented healthy-athlete limitations include:

- expiratory flow limitation;
- high work of breathing;
- respiratory muscle fatigue;
- exercise-induced arterial hypoxemia (EIAH);
- diffusion/V-Q limitations at very high cardiac outputs.

A 2025 study found expiratory flow limitation in 40% of a selected highly trained endurance-athlete cohort; this is not a population prevalence estimate.

EIAH is especially described in some highly trained endurance athletes and is multifactorial, potentially involving relative hypoventilation, V/Q mismatch and diffusion limitation.

Project rule:

**the healthy respiratory system is usually sufficient, but it is not universally “overbuilt,” especially in highly trained endurance exercise.**

---

## 17. Exercise-induced arterial hypoxemia boundary

EIAH can occur in otherwise healthy highly trained endurance athletes.

But exercise desaturation is not automatically physiological EIAH.

Unexplained or clinically relevant desaturation requires exclusion of cardiopulmonary pathology before attribution to athletic physiology.

Project rule:

**physiological EIAH is a diagnosis-of-context/exclusion concept, not a default explanation for low SpO2 during exercise.**

---

## 18. Dyspnea / clinical pathology boundary

Breathlessness during hard exercise can be normal because ventilatory drive and respiratory muscle work rise strongly.

PHY-15 does not diagnose:

- asthma/exercise-induced bronchoconstriction;
- COPD;
- interstitial lung disease;
- pulmonary vascular disease;
- dysfunctional breathing;
- inducible laryngeal obstruction;
- anemia/cardiac causes of dyspnea;
- unexplained exercise desaturation.

Clinical symptoms requiring interpretation belong to MED.

Project rule:

**expected exertional breathlessness and pathological dyspnea are not separated by intensity alone; symptoms/context and clinical evaluation matter.**

## 20. Established project-level model

### Acute dynamic exercise

`metabolic CO2/O2 demand ↑`
`→ neural + chemical ventilatory drive ↑`
`→ VT and fB adjust`
`→ VE and alveolar ventilation ↑`
`+ pulmonary perfusion/diffusion adapt`
`→ arterial gas homeostasis usually maintained`.

### Whole-body oxygen transport

`lung ventilation/diffusion`
`→ arterial O2 content`
`→ cardiac output / blood flow`
`→ tissue diffusion`
`→ mitochondrial utilization`.

### High-demand limitation

`very high ventilatory demand`
`→ mechanical flow constraints + respiratory muscle work/fatigue ± gas-exchange limitation`
`→ possible contribution to exercise limitation`.

### Chronic adaptation

Ordinary endurance training mainly raises system demand/capacity through cardiovascular, hematological and peripheral adaptation rather than proven proportional structural lung adaptation.

Targeted respiratory muscle training is a separate, more direct respiratory-muscle stimulus.

## 21. Training-relevant implications

1. **Do not interpret VE without VT, frequency and dead-space context.**
2. **Do not equate minute ventilation with alveolar ventilation.**
3. **Do not equate pulmonary gas exchange with whole-body O2 delivery.**
4. **VE/VCO2 is a ventilatory-efficiency construct, not a generic fitness score.**
5. **Ventilatory thresholds are useful landmarks, not one literal biochemical switch.**
6. **Respiratory muscle work can matter at high exercise demand.**
7. **Respiratory muscle fatigue is possible but not universal.**
8. **Resistance-exercise ventilation depends heavily on set structure and breathing strategy.**
9. **Ordinary endurance training should not be described as simply “training the lungs bigger.”**
10. **Targeted IMT/RMT is a distinct intervention with uncertain performance-transfer magnitude.**
11. **Healthy respiratory limitation becomes most relevant at very high endurance demand or in specific individuals.**
12. **Unexplained dyspnea/desaturation remains a medical question.**

## 23. Confidence / unresolved issues

### HIGH

- VE = VT × fB;
- minute ventilation differs from alveolar ventilation;
- gas exchange depends on ventilation/perfusion/diffusion integration;
- whole-body O2 transport includes pulmonary + hemoglobin/circulatory + tissue steps;
- VE rises with exercise VCO2/demand;
- respiratory muscles perform significant work at high ventilation;
- respiratory muscle fatigue can occur in severe/prolonged exercise;
- unexplained clinical dyspnea/desaturation is not normal-physiology territory.

### MODERATE-HIGH

- exact breathing-pattern shift from VT toward frequency at high demand is context dependent but robust as an integrated pattern;
- pulmonary mechanics can limit highly trained endurance athletes;
- EIAH is multifactorial;
- targeted IMT increases inspiratory strength and can increase diaphragm thickness.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- exact contribution of each controller to exercise hyperpnea;
- prevalence/magnitude of EFL or EIAH in a specific athlete population;
- magnitude of respiratory-muscle metaboreflex contribution to whole-body performance;
- chronic breathing-pattern/ventilatory-efficiency changes from ordinary endurance training;
- performance transfer from IMT/RMT;
- whether high athlete spirometry reflects training adaptation versus selection/body size.
