## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a sufficiently accurate model of unilateral/bilateral neural effects for later exercise selection, limited-equipment programming, testing and return-to-training without:

- treating bilateral deficit as a universal fixed trait;
- treating bilateral facilitation as universally desirable or expected;
- assuming bilateral force must equal the arithmetic sum of two unilateral tests;
- treating cross-education as hypertrophy of the untrained limb;
- localizing cross-education to one cortical or spinal mechanism;
- assuming dominant-to-nondominant transfer is reliably larger than the reverse;
- assuming unilateral training is globally superior to bilateral training;
- using cross-education as a substitute for direct training when both limbs can be trained normally.

### Question type

Human unilateral/bilateral force production and chronic contralateral transfer after unilateral resistance training.

### Population/context

Healthy adults are the main transfer target. Immobilization and clinical evidence is used only to define practical boundaries and is explicitly separated from ordinary healthy training.

### Critical outcomes

- bilateral index / bilateral deficit or facilitation;
- unilateral and bilateral strength;
- contralateral untrained-limb strength;
- contralateral muscle size;
- neural changes in the untrained homologous muscle/hemisphere;
- dominance direction;
- practical utility when load/equipment or one-limb training options are constrained.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 7 exact Section 7 items must be covered at content level; bilateral deficit/facilitation must remain task/test specific; cross-education must be distinguished from direct hypertrophy; neural mechanism claims must preserve uncertainty; dominance asymmetry must not be assumed; unilateral-vs-bilateral training implications must follow specificity rather than a universal superiority rule.

## 4. Bilateral deficit

Operationally, bilateral deficit (BLD) means that force/performance during simultaneous bilateral action is lower than expected from the sum of corresponding unilateral actions.

A simple bilateral index can be conceptualized as:

`bilateral performance / summed unilateral performance`.

Negative deviation from equivalence = deficit.

The 2026 J Strength Cond Res review (PMID 42171503) shows that BLD is common but not universal:

- more often reported in dynamic than isometric contractions;
- varies with angle, limb, population and movement;
- is affected by posture, stabilization, synergist contribution and shortening velocity;
- training specificity matters.

Mechanistically, interhemispheric/higher-order neural inhibition is a plausible contributor, but BLD is not purely neural because biomechanics and task setup can change the result.

Canonical rule:

**bilateral deficit is a task/test-specific expression, not a fixed nervous-system limitation or personality-like trait.**

Confidence: **HIGH for context dependence; MODERATE for exact mechanism.**

---

## 5. Bilateral facilitation

Bilateral facilitation is the opposite direction of the bilateral index:

> simultaneous bilateral performance exceeds the corresponding unilateral-sum expectation or the bilateral condition produces greater per-side output than the comparable unilateral setup.

It can occur in some tasks and trained populations.

Examples:

- 2016 unilateral-vs-bilateral training study (PMID 26348920): unilateral training produced a post-training deficit while bilateral training produced facilitation in that knee-extension model;
- 2024 hip dynamometry study (PMID 39197320): bilateral testing produced approximately 25% higher force than unilateral testing in that apparatus/task;
- 2023 bench-press study (PMID 37035666): force showed facilitation while power/velocity showed deficit, demonstrating that the sign can depend on the outcome variable even in the same task.

Therefore “facilitation” does not mean the nervous system has become globally more efficient.

Canonical rule:

**bilateral deficit and facilitation are signs of a task-specific bilateral index; the sign can change with training, setup and measured outcome.**

Confidence: **HIGH for existence/context dependence; LOW-MODERATE for universal mechanism.**

## 6. Training specificity of bilateral performance

The 2026 bilateral-deficit review reports that 10 of 11 training studies found specificity effects:

- bilateral training tended to reduce BLD;
- unilateral training tended to increase BLD.

This is consistent with newer meta-analytic evidence.

The 2026 multilevel meta-analysis (PMID 42458613; 24 RCTs) found:

- unilateral training better for unilateral strength;
- bilateral training better for bilateral strength;
- no significant between-group difference for hypertrophy.

The 2025 Sports Medicine meta-analysis (PMID 39794667) similarly found:

- bilateral training superior for bilateral dynamic strength;
- unilateral training superior for unilateral dynamic strength;
- no significant hypertrophy difference.

Canonical rule:

**the most defensible unilateral-vs-bilateral strength rule is specificity, not global superiority of one mode.**

Confidence: **HIGH.**

## 7. Cross-education: what is established

Cross-education is improvement in an untrained contralateral limb after unilateral training of the opposite limb.

Current evidence supports a real strength effect.

### Upper limb

2026 systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID 42323824):

- 31 studies;
- 1,214 participants;
- small significant contralateral gains in maximal voluntary isometric strength;
- small significant gains in contralateral 1RM;
- certainty low to very low.

### Lower limb

Systematic review/meta-analysis PMID 39235953:

- 29 studies;
- significant contralateral improvements across 1RM, MVC and several peak-torque outcomes;
- effect magnitude varied by contraction/testing conditions.

### Historical broader synthesis

2018 meta-analysis (PMID 28936703):

- pooled 785 participants;
- average contralateral strength gain about 11.9%;
- substantial methodological limitations/high risk of bias in the historical literature.

Canonical rule:

**unilateral resistance training can increase strength in the homologous contralateral untrained limb, but magnitude is variable and not guaranteed for an individual.**

Confidence: **HIGH for existence of strength transfer; MODERATE for magnitude.**

## 8. Cross-education is predominantly neural, but not one mechanism

The untrained limb does not receive the same direct mechanical overload as the trained limb, making neural transfer the primary explanation for ordinary cross-education of strength.

Candidate/observed adaptations include:

- altered cortical inhibition/excitability;
- bilateral motor-network engagement;
- altered descending drive;
- changes in shared synaptic input;
- motor-unit recruitment-threshold changes;
- task representation / motor learning.

The 2025 J Physiol study (PMID 40500979) found after four weeks unilateral training:

- increased force and force steadiness in the untrained limb;
- greater relative shared synaptic input;
- reduced variability of common input;
- lower motor-unit recruitment thresholds.

This provides direct motor-unit-level evidence of contralateral neural adaptation.

However, the 2018 neurophysiology meta-analysis (PMID 29446711) found:

- some changes in cortical silent period and SICI;
- no consistent change in untrained-limb EMG or MEP amplitude;
- no correlation between cross-education magnitude and pooled TMS changes.

The 2019 systematic review (PMID 31546134) likewise concluded that cross-education can occur without a consistent increase in ipsilateral motor-cortical excitability.

Canonical rule:

**cross-education is predominantly neural in ordinary healthy unilateral training, but no single cortical, spinal or motor-unit marker is the established master mechanism.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH.**

## 9. Contralateral neural adaptation: site boundary

Cross-education should not be represented as:

`train right limb → left motor cortex gets stronger → left limb gets stronger`.

The nervous system is bilateral and distributed.

Potential contributors include:

- ipsilateral and contralateral motor cortex;
- interhemispheric interactions;
- premotor/supplementary networks;
- corticospinal pathways;
- spinal/motoneuron output;
- bilateral task representation.

Older direct work (PMID 19756705) showed contralateral increases in MVC, normalized EMG and V-wave without H-reflex change in one plantar-flexor protocol, demonstrating that different neural measures can dissociate.

NEU-06 measurement rule therefore carries forward:

**MEP, SICI/cSP, H-reflex, V-wave, surface EMG and motor-unit metrics are not interchangeable mechanism readouts.**

No single site is required to explain all cross-education.

## 10. Dominant vs non-dominant limb

A common claim is:

> train the dominant limb because transfer to the nondominant limb is larger.

The 2024 meta-analysis (PMID 38568258) directly tested this claim.

Only three eligible studies / five effect sizes were available.

The pooled comparison provided **minimal support** for the asymmetry hypothesis, with wide uncertainty.

The authors concluded that available evidence does not support a reliable rule that dominant-to-nondominant transfer exceeds nondominant-to-dominant transfer.

A large 2024 unilateral elbow-flexion trial (PMID 39582380) also demonstrated cross-education from either trained side in its design.

Canonical rule:

**do not choose the trained limb solely from a presumed dominant→nondominant cross-education advantage.**

Confidence: **MODERATE that no practical universal asymmetry rule is justified; LOW certainty on whether small true asymmetries exist.**

---

## 11. Neural transfer ≠ equal contralateral hypertrophy

This is the most important construct boundary in NEU-07.

The 2026 upper-limb meta-analysis (PMID 42323824) found:

- contralateral strength improvement;
- **no significant contralateral muscle-thickness effect**.

The 2024 high-load elbow-flexion RCT (PMID 39582380) found:

- strength increased in the opposite untrained arm;
- muscle thickness increased only in directly trained arms.

Therefore ordinary cross-education should be interpreted primarily as transfer of strength/neural performance, not as equal muscle growth.

Canonical rule:

**contralateral strength gain does not imply contralateral hypertrophy.**

Confidence: **HIGH for the distinction.**

---

## 12. Immobilization nuance: preservation is not ordinary hypertrophy

A 2026 immobilization systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID 42141765; 8 studies, n=189) found that training the nonimmobilized arm:

- attenuated strength loss in the immobilized arm;
- produced a small average muscle-size preservation effect in some conditions.

This does not contradict the ordinary cross-education rule.

During immobilization, the relevant comparison is often:

`less atrophy/loss` versus `more atrophy/loss`,

not:

`growth of untrained muscle above baseline`.

Canonical rule:

**cross-education may help preserve muscle size during disuse in some contexts, but this is not evidence that unilateral training produces equal hypertrophy in a normally active untrained limb.**

Confidence: **MODERATE for immobilization preservation; HIGH for not equating preservation with ordinary hypertrophy.**

---

## 13. Clinical / rehabilitation boundary

The 2026 clinical-population meta-analysis (PMID 42410073) found:

- moderate improvement in maximal force production;
- small improvement in rapid force production;
- effects varied across clinical/immobilization models.

This supports cross-education as a potentially useful rehabilitation adjunct when direct loading of one limb is limited.

However, clinical use depends on:

- injury/pathology;
- surgical restrictions;
- pain;
- tissue-loading constraints;
- clinician guidance.

Those decisions remain MED/REC/PRO/IND, not NEU-07.

## 14. Practical applicability with limited equipment

For an otherwise healthy person with limited external load, unilateral training can be practically useful for two independent reasons.

### A. Direct loading of the trained limb

A bilateral external load is shared across two limbs.

Using one limb can make the same available equipment represent a higher relative challenge per active limb or allow progression when total external load is constrained.

This is a practical load-distribution consequence, not cross-education.

### B. Cross-education

If one side cannot be directly trained temporarily, training the available side can preserve/improve some contralateral strength.

This is cross-education.

These mechanisms must not be conflated.

Current unilateral-vs-bilateral meta-analyses support:

- similar hypertrophy on average when comparing unilateral vs bilateral programs;
- strength gains favor the mode in which strength is tested/trained.

Canonical practical rule:

**with limited equipment, unilateral variants are a legitimate way to increase relative per-limb challenge and train unilateral strength; cross-education is a secondary benefit, not the main overload strategy when both limbs are healthy and trainable.**

Status: **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC supported by training-specificity and hypertrophy evidence.**

## 15. What unilateral training does not automatically mean

Unilateral training does **not** automatically provide:

- greater whole-body hypertrophy;
- greater bilateral strength;
- greater athletic transfer;
- greater cross-education if the dominant limb is chosen;
- equal growth of the untrained limb;
- correction of every interlimb asymmetry;
- a superior neural stimulus in every exercise.

The 2026 comparative meta-analysis found unilateral advantages for unilateral strength/jump measures, while bilateral training had an advantage for bilateral strength and no clear hypertrophy superiority.

Project rule:

**choose unilateral vs bilateral modes from goal, load constraints, task specificity and tolerability—not from a blanket hierarchy.**

## 16. Implications for testing

Unilateral and bilateral strength values are not automatically interchangeable.

Examples:

- BLD or facilitation can change the relation between tests;
- bilateral testing can underestimate interlimb asymmetry in some setups (PMID 39197320);
- force, velocity and power can show different bilateral-index signs in the same exercise (PMID 37035666).

Therefore TST should specify:

- unilateral vs bilateral condition;
- stabilization;
- joint angle / ROM;
- load;
- outcome variable;
- dominant/non-dominant side;
- familiarization.

Canonical rule:

**a bilateral index is a property of a defined test, not a general score of nervous-system quality.**

## 18. Confidence / unresolved issues

### HIGH

- bilateral deficit is not universal and is strongly task/test dependent;
- unilateral vs bilateral strength adaptation follows specificity;
- cross-education of strength exists;
- contralateral strength transfer does not imply equal contralateral hypertrophy;
- dominance asymmetry is not sufficiently established for a practical rule.

### MODERATE-HIGH

- neural mechanisms dominate ordinary cross-education;
- contralateral motor-unit/shared-input adaptations can occur;
- training mode can shift the bilateral index;
- unilateral training is a valid strength-training mode rather than merely a rehabilitation substitute.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- exact magnitude of cross-education;
- exact mechanism and anatomical site;
- exact degree of muscle-size preservation during immobilization;
- whether eccentric protocols systematically maximize transfer;
- relation of bilateral deficit to athletic performance;
- optimal unilateral/bilateral mixture for a specific sport or exercise.

### LOW / NOT ESTABLISHED AS UNIVERSAL

- one master cross-education mechanism;
- dominant→nondominant transfer superiority;
- bilateral deficit as a stable individual trait;
- bilateral facilitation as a globally superior state;
- meaningful hypertrophy of a normally active untrained limb from contralateral training alone.
