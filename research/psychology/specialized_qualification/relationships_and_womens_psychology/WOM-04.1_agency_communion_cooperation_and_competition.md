## 2. Targeted evidence

Public searches 2026-11-25: agency communion gender meta-analysis; cooperation social dilemmas cross-national meta-analysis; competition entry and ambiguity; women's occupational roles, fear of backlash and within-women decision profiles. Selected primary and synthesis evidence including null/counterexamples. Not systematic, no private data or raw-data reanalysis.

E1. Hsu, Badura, Newman & Speach (2021), Gender, “Masculinity,” and “Femininity”: A Meta-Analytic Review of Gender Differences in Agency and Communion. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000343 ; https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/gender-masculinity-and-femininity-a-meta-analytic-review-of-gende/
Access: complete published abstract and metadata on author-institution record; no full article or supplement audit. Agency g=.40 (928 samples, N=254,731), communion g=-.56 (937 samples, N=254,465) with signs defined men minus women. These are separate analyses, not additive independent participant totals. Effects declined across historical time; communion gap smaller at older ages and larger with occupational segregation; agency gap larger in couple samples; instrument and orientation moderated effects. Scales were designed around gender-typed attributes; measurement and historical/context differences matter. Moderation is not a causal explanation. Agency and communion are not opposite ends of one continuum, nor measures of independent action or cooperation in every setting.

E2. Spadaro, Jin & Balliet (2023 issue, online 2022), Gender differences in cooperation across 20 societies: a meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2021.0438 ; https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/gender-differences-in-cooperation-across-20-societies-a-meta-anal/
Access: complete author-institution published abstract and indexed primary article excerpts; PMC direct opening blocked. Preregistered analysis of experimental social dilemmas (1961–2017), k=126, 20 industrialized societies; within-study contrast d=.011, 95% CI [-.038,.060]. Across-study comparisons (k=972) showed slightly more cooperation in predominantly female samples. These two levels differ; sample composition and ecological confounding prevent interpreting the latter as an individual effect. Predicted moderators (degree of conflict, equality and development) not supported. Near-zero pooled difference does not prove identical behavior in every context or equivalence under all thresholds. Experimental social-dilemma decisions with strangers are not friendship, caregiving or all cooperation. No full study-quality audit.
Contrasting prior Balliet et al. (2011), PMID21910518, indexed primary abstract: overall cooperation differences small, but reported differences by same/mixed-sex interaction composition. E2 and earlier synthesis likely share primary studies; not independent replications. Specific moderator discrepancies require full design and coding comparison. No universal context-to-cooperation rule.

E3. Markowsky & Beblo (2022), When do we observe a gender gap in competition entry? A meta-analysis of the experimental literature. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167268122001251 ; https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jeborg/v198y2022icp139-163.html
Access: indexed published abstract, selected methods excerpts; direct publisher opening returned 403. 110 studies, 409 effects, mean raw difference 13 percentage points more tournament entry by men. Experimental conditions and subject pools vary; tournament entry is a choice between reward structures, not pure competition preference, ability, ambition or general interpersonal assertiveness. Confidence, risk, rewards, norms and task selection are plausible contributors; detailed residual/moderator estimates not verified. Do not translate percentage points into relative percentage or stable individual motive.

E4. Risk, Ambiguity, and the gender gap in tournament entry (2026). https://doi.org/10.1007/s11166-026-09491-z
Access: publisher HTML abstract, selected introduction, design, sample, regression results and conclusion inspected; not full end-to-end. 348 undergraduates, mean age 21.57, 29 sessions in 2023 at METU. Experimental task calibrated to minimize ability differences, winning largely determined by noise. Known versus unknown noise distribution distinguished risk and ambiguity. Full-sample baseline gender contrast in entry under risk -.020 (p=.785), under ambiguity -.169 (p=.027); authors report female entry reduced by ambiguity and no significant complexity effects. Their treatment-based competitiveness measure also depends on uncertainty context. Do not infer interaction significance solely from one significant and one nonsignificant subgroup contrast; exact interaction model audit not completed. Authors discuss perceived competence/control as alternative to competitiveness per se. Luck-dominated laboratory task, student sample and measure assumptions limit transfer to life decisions. Experimental manipulation supports local contextual inference, not inherent female risk aversion, global preference or identified psychological mediator.

E5. Tan, Zuo, Wen, Xie & Song (2021), Fear of Backlash Moderates Female Senior Executives' Communion (but Not Agency) as Compared to Female Lecturers. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.520590
Access: publisher HTML hypothesis, Study 2 assignment/procedure, sample and results, Study 3 sample and Table 5, and selected discussion inspected. Study 1 N=212 prescriptive trait ratings. Study 2 N=207 women students randomly assigned imagined executive/lecturer role; self-rated workplace agency higher and communion lower in executive condition. This is an experimentally induced imagined role self-description, not observed work behavior or actual occupational selection. Study 3 N=202 women in actual occupations (96 executives/106 lecturers, Hubei China); role by fear-of-backlash interaction for communion adjusted beta=.15, SE=.07, p=.03; agency interaction beta=.02, SE=.07, p=.73. Higher fear of sanctions attenuated communion contrast; not a proven causal effect of fear. Simple slope patterns differed across studies. Self-reports, selected roles, cultural and item-content boundaries, cross-sectional Study 3 and occupational response selection matter. Within-women evidence: role and anticipated evaluation can be related to self-concept without proving fixed female types or concealed motives.

E6. Villanueva-Moya, Aguilera & Expósito (2026), Women's Decision-Making: A Latent Profile Analysis of Agency, Communion, and Fear of Negative Evaluation. https://doi.org/10.1177/00332941261442825 ; PMID41974430.
Access: indexed published abstract and metadata; direct PubMed opening blocked. N=345 Spanish women; reported three model-derived profiles combining agency, communion and fear of negative evaluation, including two profiles with similar communal and agentic levels but different fear. This is a useful within-women hypothesis about heterogeneity beyond two trait dimensions. Model fit, class size, validation, decision-specific effects and sampling not verified. Latent classes are model-dependent summaries, not established natural types or individual classifications. No causal inference that anticipated evaluation produces any particular decision.

E7. Negotiating gender roles: gender differences in assertive negotiating are mediated by women's fear of backlash and attenuated when negotiating on behalf of others (2010), PMID20085399.
Access: indexed primary abstract only. Reports context-specific differences in assertive bargaining when advocating for self versus others, linked to anticipated social evaluation. Supports distinguishing capability, choice, beneficiary and anticipated cost; mediation alone is not definitive causal mechanism. Exact designs and estimates not audited; reserve detailed negotiation and communication evidence for WOM-04.2.

Evidence limits: E4/E5 substantive selected HTML sections inspected; E1/E2 author-institution abstracts, E3/E6/E7 indexed abstract/excerpts. No complete paper read end-to-end, raw-data reanalysis, exhaustive correction/retraction check or independent assessment. No PDF analyzed in this pass. E6 is a supporting lead only for profiles; no class-specific decision effects claimed. Generalization from competitive/occupational tasks to personal relationships is not established.

## 3. Operational distinctions and practice

Separate (a) agentic/communal traits and values, (b) goals and autonomous endorsement, (c) actual role demands and resources, (d) expected social costs and perceived norms, (e) opportunity, competence, uncertainty and incentives, (f) observed choice, behavior and outcomes. Agency is not SDT autonomy; communion is not submission; assertiveness is not aggression; cooperation is not altruism; competition entry is not ability or stable competitive motive. Self- and other-oriented goals can coexist. Public compliance is not necessarily private agreement.
Evaluate relevant actor, recipient, audience, stakes, comparison and time. Distinguish the behavior and its function. Consider actual expectations and perceived expectations, not a presumed female motive. Compare repeated comparable contexts and voluntary reports, use rival conditional predictions and revision triggers; no invented personal probabilities or covert tests. Preserve explicit preferences and autonomy independently of group patterns.
Practice (original invented examples): A. A fictional employee volunteers to coordinate a group project while asserting her own working conditions: communion and agency can coexist; no unique motive. B. A woman enters a contest with transparent rules but declines a contest with uncertain reward: compare task preference, information, costs and perceived control before inferring low competitiveness. C. Two cooperative choices can reflect different incentives; no proof of altruism. D. Similar trait self-ratings can coexist with different concern about social evaluation; a latent profile does not establish a person's motives. External checks E1/E2 distinguish self-concept from behavior, E4 qualifies universal competition avoidance, E5/E6 demonstrate within-women variation. Results known before review; internal reasoning only, no blind assessment or validated forecast.
