# 2. Canonical scientific target

Interpretive Interactionism asks:

**How do personally consequential interactional experiences become meaningful turning points in biography, and how are those private experiences connected to wider social relationships, institutions, cultural meanings, public responses and policies?**

### Canonical project representation

problematic / consequential lived experience
+
biography
+
interaction
+
epiphanic / turning-point episodes
+
thick description
+
thick interpretation
+
critical examination of prior conceptions
+
reconstruction of meaning
+
contextualization in social/institutional/public worlds.

### Critical rule

II is not merely:

> “find themes in stories about important events.”

The design must move from individual experience toward an interpretive account of how that experience is produced, understood and shaped within broader social/institutional contexts.

---

# 3. Genealogy

Denzin's II combines several intellectual traditions.

The 2nd edition explicitly synthesizes influences from:

- symbolic interactionism;
- hermeneutics;
- phenomenology / lived experience;
- biography / life-history work;
- feminist thought;
- postmodern/poststructural thought;
- critical-biographical inquiry.

### Project rule

II should not be flattened into:

> “symbolic interactionism with interviews.”

Symbolic interactionism is a major foundation, but Denzin adds a specific methodological architecture for biographical and interactional interpretation.

---

# 4. Private troubles and public issues

A central Denzin target is the relationship between:

- **private/personal trouble**;
- **public/social/institutional response**.

Examples:

- alcoholism and treatment systems;
- domestic violence and healthcare/social systems;
- disability and sport institutions;
- injury and athletic culture;
- identity disruption and organizational expectations.

### Scientific value

II asks not only:

> What happened to this person?

but:

> How did institutions, cultural meanings, social relationships and public responses participate in what this experience became?

---

# 5. Biographical experience

II places **biographical experience** at the center of analysis.

The person is understood through:

- past life trajectory;
- interactional history;
- present situation;
- remembered turning points;
- social context;
- institutional encounters.

### Critical rule

A single decontextualized quote is weak evidence for an II claim.

Biographical and interactional context matters.

---

# 6. Epiphany

Denzin uses **epiphany** for consequential interactional moments that alter or reveal fundamental meanings in a person's life.

Epiphanies can:

- disrupt identity;
- expose hidden tension;
- change self-understanding;
- reorganize biography;
- become retrospectively meaningful turning points.

### Critical rule

Epiphany ≠ any memorable event.

The event/episode must have substantial biographical meaning or expose/produce a consequential transformation in meaning.

---

# 7. Four canonical epiphany types

Denzin distinguishes four broad forms.

## 7.1 Major epiphany

A dramatic event whose impact is immediate and life-altering.

Sport example:

> spinal cord injury during rugby that abruptly transforms bodily identity and future.

## 7.2 Cumulative epiphany

A gradual accumulation of experiences that eventually becomes recognized as a turning point.

Sport example:

> repeated exclusion, injury or burnout slowly producing realization that elite sport is no longer sustainable.

## 7.3 Minor / illuminative epiphany

A smaller event that reveals a deeper underlying problem, conflict or structure.

Sport example:

> one apparently minor interaction with a coach suddenly reveals a broader pattern of devaluation.

## 7.4 Relived epiphany

A major problematic experience whose meaning is repeatedly re-experienced/reconstructed later.

Sport example:

> an athlete repeatedly relives a catastrophic injury when returning to the same venue, telling the story or facing rehabilitation setbacks.

### Project rule

Store author terminology:

- “minor”;
- “illuminative”;
- or “minor/illuminative.”

The literature uses overlapping labels.

---

# 8. Epiphany classification is not the whole methodology

A study can use Denzin's four epiphany types as an **analytic framework** without implementing full II.

Example:

Sport Transitions as Epiphanies classified sport-transition stories using Denzin's epiphany typology.

That supports:

> Denzin-informed epiphany analysis.

It does not automatically prove that the entire study followed:

deconstruction
→ capture
→ bracketing
→ construction
→ contextualization.

### Project rule

Distinguish:

1. full Interpretive Interactionism;
2. II-informed epiphany analysis;
3. generic turning-point analysis.

---

# 9. Six-step interpretive process

Current SAGE material for Denzin's II represents the interpretive process as:

1. **framing the research question**;
2. **deconstruction**;
3. **capture**;
4. **bracketing**;
5. **construction**;
6. **contextualization**.

Some secondary/applied papers call the last five steps the “five phases,” treating question framing separately.

### Project rule

Store:

- six-part full process;
- author-specific five-phase shorthand if used.

Do not treat the nomenclature difference as a substantive methodological conflict.

---

# 10. Step 1 — framing the research question

The question should identify a personally/socially consequential phenomenon that can be examined through lived interaction and biography.

Strong II questions often concern:

- problematic experience;
- institutional response;
- turning points;
- identity transformation;
- public/private intersection.

### Example

> How do athletes experience a catastrophic injury, and how do sport culture, rehabilitation systems and masculinity norms shape the meaning of that injury?

---

# 11. Step 2 — deconstruction

Deconstruction involves critical examination of prior conceptions and representations of the phenomenon.

Targets can include:

- scientific literature;
- professional categories;
- institutional language;
- cultural assumptions;
- dominant narratives;
- taken-for-granted definitions.

### Scientific function

Expose:

- what prior accounts assume;
- whose perspective is privileged;
- what is omitted;
- how the phenomenon has already been socially/textually constructed.

### Critical rule

Deconstruction is not merely:

> “write the literature review.”

It is a critical analytic operation.

---

# 12. Deconstruction and prior theory

II does not require a theory-free entry.

Instead, prior concepts can themselves become objects of critique.

### Example

If sport medicine literature frames injured athletes mainly through:

- compliance;
- readiness;
- return-to-play outcome;

II can ask what such framing obscures about:

- masculinity;
- identity;
- institutional pressure;
- bodily alienation.

---

# 13. Step 3 — capture

Capture seeks multiple naturalistic instances/expressions of the phenomenon.

Possible sources include:

- open-ended interviews;
- life histories;
- personal experience stories;
- documents;
- observations;
- self-stories;
- archival material;
- participant observation.

### Scientific function

Capture should preserve:

- lived interaction;
- biography;
- context;
- concrete episodes.

### Critical rule

“Capture” does not mean literal objective recording of inner experience.

It means obtaining rich empirical representations of the phenomenon.

---

# 14. Multiple instances

Denzin's process emphasizes obtaining **multiple instances** of the phenomenon where possible.

This may mean:

- several people;
- several episodes;
- repeated events in one biography;
- multiple data sources.

### Critical rule

Multiple instances improve interpretive depth.

They do not create statistical representativeness.

---

# 15. Step 4 — bracketing

In Denzin's II, bracketing isolates the phenomenon's key structures/features from the captured material so they can be examined closely.

### Important boundary

This is not identical to:

- Husserlian epoché;
- “remove all researcher assumptions.”

It is an analytic reduction/isolating operation inside Denzin's interpretive sequence.

### Project rule

Do not import descriptive-phenomenology bracketing criteria automatically into II.

---

# 16. What bracketing seeks

The analyst asks:

- What is essential in this interactional episode?
- What meanings recur?
- What conditions make the epiphany consequential?
- Which elements of identity/self are altered?
- What social responses become relevant?

### Critical rule

Bracketing is temporary.

II ultimately returns the phenomenon to context during construction/contextualization.

---

# 17. Step 5 — construction

Construction reassembles the phenomenon into an interpretive account.

The analyst relates:

- key experiential structures;
- biography;
- interaction;
- meanings;
- identities;
- institutional conditions.

### Scientific function

Move from isolated analytic elements toward:

> an intelligible model/account of the interactional experience.

### Critical rule

Construction is interpretive.

It is not simple aggregation of codes.

---

# 18. Step 6 — contextualization

Contextualization relocates the interpreted phenomenon in the wider lived/social world.

This step connects:

- individual biography;
- social relationships;
- institutional practices;
- professional ethics;
- cultural narratives;
- public policy.

### Canonical movement

personal experience
→
interaction
→
biographical meaning
→
social/institutional/public context.

### Critical rule

A study that ends with purely intrapsychic themes without contextual return is weakly aligned with full II.

---

# 19. Contextualization is not background description

Weak:

> “Participants lived in a competitive sport environment.”

Stronger II contextualization:

> show how selection systems, coaching authority, rehabilitation practices, gender norms or institutional expectations shape the interactional meaning of injury/identity.

### Rule

Context should enter the explanatory interpretation, not remain a paragraph in Methods.

---

# 20. Thick description

II relies heavily on **thick description**.

Strong thick description includes:

- event;
- participants;
- biography;
- setting;
- interaction;
- meanings;
- emotional texture;
- relevant social context.

### Critical rule

Thick description ≠ long quotation.

Description is “thick” when the reader can understand what the action means in its social setting.

---

# 21. Thick interpretation

Mohr describes II as moving from:

> thick description

to:

> thick interpretation.

Thick interpretation connects lived material to:

- biography;
- cultural meaning;
- institutional structure;
- public policy;
- social relations.

### Rule

The aim is not only:

> reproduce participant voice.

It is to offer a contextually grounded interpretive reading.

---

# 22. Description and interpretation are not opposites

II does not treat description as value-free raw fact and interpretation as a later subjective add-on.

The research process already involves:

- selection;
- framing;
- language;
- interaction;
- representation.

### Project implication

Reflexivity must cover:

- interviewing;
- interpretation;
- writing;
- selection of epiphanies;
- contextual claims.

---

# 23. Researcher as knowing/interpretive subject

Denzin's second edition explicitly foregrounds the researcher as:

- newcomer;
- knowing subject;
- writer/interpreter.

### Critical rule

Researcher subjectivity is not treated as something that can be completely removed.

It must be:

- acknowledged;
- critically examined;
- represented transparently.

---

# 24. Intersubjectivity

II emerges from a broader interactionist tradition in which meaning is produced through relationships and interpretation.

Current interpretive-method literature continues to treat intersubjectivity as a core methodological problem.

### Project rule

Do not represent participant accounts as:

> isolated private mental objects.

Meaning is shaped through:

- prior interaction;
- interview encounter;
- social world;
- interpretive writing.

---

# 25. Voice and representation

II gives substantial interpretive authority to the researcher.

Potential risks:

- researcher speaks over participants;
- dramatic epiphanies are privileged because they are narratively attractive;
- social theory overwhelms biography;
- contradictions are smoothed into a coherent story.

### Quality requirement

Show:

- participant material;
- interpretive movement;
- alternative readings;
- researcher position.

---

# 26. Epiphany selection bias

Because II is attracted to consequential turning points, it can overrepresent:

- crisis;
- rupture;
- dramatic transformation.

It may underrepresent:

- routine continuity;
- slow nonconscious adaptation;
- uneventful stability.

### Project rule

If the scientific question concerns gradual ordinary process rather than consequential biographical moments, another methodology may fit better.

---

# 27. Retrospective meaning

Epiphanies are often recognized retrospectively.

A participant may only later identify an earlier event as:

> the moment everything changed.

### Critical rule

Retrospective epiphany meaning is legitimate II evidence.

It is not the same as proving that the event objectively caused all subsequent outcomes.

---

# 28. Epiphany ≠ causal mechanism

A turning point can appear causally decisive in a person's biography.

II can support:

> participant/interpreter understands X as transformative.

It cannot by itself establish:

> counterfactual causal effect of X.

### Rule

Epiphanic causality
≠
causal estimand.

---

# 29. Personal trouble ↔ institutional structure

A central II strength is connecting micro and macro levels.

Examples:

injury experience
↔
selection policy;

domestic violence
↔
healthcare response;

addiction
↔
treatment institution;

athletic identity loss
↔
sport masculinity culture.

### Critical rule

The structural link should be evidenced through:

- participant interaction;
- documents;
- policy;
- organizational practices;
- cultural material;

rather than inferred solely from one person's account.

---

# 30. Institutional/public-response claims

II can identify how people experience:

- healthcare;
- rehabilitation;
- social services;
- sport organizations;
- professional ethics;
- public policy.

### Claim boundary

It can support:

> interpreted experience of institutional response.

A claim that:

> policy X causes outcome Y

requires separate causal evidence.

---

# 31. Data-generation architecture

II can legitimately use multiple qualitative sources:

- open-ended interviews;
- repeated interviews;
- participant observation;
- documents;
- life histories;
- personal histories;
- self-stories;
- autobiographical material;
- creative/performative text.

### Critical rule

No one data source is constitutive.

The methodology is defined by the interpretive-biographical architecture.

---

# 32. Interviewing

II interviewing is generally:

- open;
- biographical;
- interactional;
- oriented toward concrete experience/epiphany.

Useful probes:

- What happened?
- What did it mean then?
- What does it mean now?
- Who was involved?
- What changed?
- How did organizations/other people respond?
- What happened to your understanding of yourself?

### Rule

A generic barriers/facilitators interview is not II automatically.

---

# 33. Observation

Observation can strengthen claims about:

- interaction;
- institutions;
- everyday practices;
- public responses.

### Critical rule

II is not observation-dependent.

But if the claim concerns how institutions actually interact with people, observation/documents can reduce overreliance on retrospective interview alone.

---

# 34. Documents / policy texts

Documents can contextualize participant experience.

Examples:

- return-to-play policy;
- treatment protocol;
- organizational code;
- media representation;
- institutional correspondence.

### Rule

Documents are not analyzed merely for content.

They help show how public/institutional responses connect to personal biography.

---

# 35. Writing and representation

Denzin's II allows/encourages experimentation with writing.

Possible representations:

- first-person accounts;
- polyphonic text;
- biographical scenes;
- interpretive narrative;
- performative writing.

### Critical rule

Experimental writing is a representation strategy, not proof of methodological quality.

Evidence and interpretive traceability remain necessary.

---

# 36. Six-step process is iterative in practice

Although often presented sequentially, applied II is not necessarily mechanically linear.

Researchers may move back and forth between:

- capture;
- bracketing;
- construction;
- contextualization;
- literature/deconstruction.

### Project rule

Appraise functional coverage rather than ritual order alone.

---

# 37. Quality architecture

Strong II should demonstrate:

1. clearly consequential personal/social phenomenon;
2. explicit Denzin lineage;
3. biographical context;
4. interactional detail;
5. epiphany/turning-point relevance where appropriate;
6. deconstruction of prior conceptions;
7. rich empirical capture;
8. bracketing/analytic isolation of key features;
9. construction of an interpretive account;
10. contextualization back into social/institutional worlds;
11. thick description;
12. thick interpretation;
13. researcher reflexivity;
14. representation of contradiction/divergence;
15. evidence for macro/institutional claims;
16. claims calibrated to qualitative interpretation.

---

# 38. No universal N

II has no universal participant number.

Adequacy depends on:

- biographical richness;
- number/variation of relevant episodes;
- depth of contextual evidence;
- social/institutional scope;
- whether one or several forms of epiphany are being examined.

### Critical rule

A small number of richly documented biographies can be coherent.

Small N still cannot support prevalence claims.

---

# 39. Saturation boundary

Generic thematic saturation is not a defining II requirement.

Adequacy is better assessed through:

- richness of captured experience;
- interpretive depth;
- coverage of key interactional structures;
- contextualization;
- contradictory instances.

### Rule

“No new themes” is not sufficient evidence of II completion.

---

# 40. Negative / contradictory cases

Strong II should preserve cases where:

- the same event is not experienced as epiphany;
- institutional response is interpreted differently;
- expected identity disruption does not occur;
- participant rejects dominant interpretation.

### Scientific value

Contradiction can show:

- variation in meaning;
- limits of an interpretation;
- structural conditionality.

---

# 41. Critical/post-structural Interpretive Interactionism

Sundin & Fahy proposed a **critical, post-structural interpretive interactionism** variant.

Their update responds to tensions in Denzin's earlier theoretical foundations and explicitly incorporates:

- critical social theory;
- poststructural concerns;
- power;
- discourse;
- subjectivity.

### Project classification

Treat as:

Interpretive Interactionism parent
+
critical/post-structural orientation.

### Critical rule

Do not create a new top-level parent methodology solely because the critical variant is used.

---

# 42. II vs symbolic interactionism

## Symbolic interactionism

A theoretical perspective focused on:

- meaning;
- interaction;
- interpretation;
- self.

## Interpretive Interactionism

A Denzin methodology with:

- biographical/epiphanic focus;
- six-step interpretive process;
- thick interpretation;
- private/public contextualization.

### Rule

Using symbolic interactionism as a theoretical framework ≠ using Interpretive Interactionism methodology.

---

# 43. II vs phenomenology

## Phenomenology

Primary target:
- lived-experience structure/meaning;
- lifeworld/being;
- tradition-specific phenomenological interpretation.

## II

Primary target:
- consequential interactional/biographical experience;
- epiphany;
- public/institutional context;
- private trouble ↔ public response.

### Critical rule

Both may ask about lived experience.

The micro↔macro biographical contextualization architecture distinguishes II.

---

# 44. II vs hermeneutic phenomenology

Both are interpretive and reject naive description.

But II has distinctive emphasis on:

- symbolic interaction;
- biography;
- epiphanies;
- public/social problems;
- institutional response.

### Rule

Interpretive depth alone does not make a study II.

---

# 45. II vs narrative inquiry

There is substantial overlap:

- stories;
- biography;
- temporality;
- turning points;
- identity;
- co-construction.

## Narrative inquiry

Primary object:
- story;
- plot;
- telling;
- narrative identity;
- narrative resources.

## II

Primary object:
- interactional biographical problem/epiphany;
- its interpretation;
- its relationship to social/institutional/public context.

### Project rule

A study can combine:

II methodology
+
narrative analysis.

Sparkes/Smith sport work is an important example of such overlap.

---

# 46. Turning-point analysis vs II

Narrative studies frequently analyze turning points.

A turning point alone does not establish II.

Full II requires the broader Denzin architecture.

### Rule

Code separately:

- turning-point/epiphany concept;
- full II methodology.

---

# 47. II vs grounded theory

## Grounded theory

Aim:
- generate integrated explanatory theory/process;
- constant comparison;
- theoretical sampling;
- memoing;
- category saturation.

## II

Aim:
- thickly interpret consequential biographical interaction and connect it to wider social/institutional worlds.

### Critical rule

II does not require:

- theoretical sampling;
- core category;
- substantive theory.

A conceptual interpretation is not automatically GT.

---

# 48. II vs qualitative description

QD can describe:

- what participants reported about difficult experiences.

II asks additionally:

- how these episodes transformed biography;
- what interactional meanings they carry;
- how institutions/public structures are involved.

### Rule

A list of experiences/barriers is not sufficient for II.

---

# 49. II vs case study

A single person's biography can be analyzed through II.

That does not automatically make the study a case study.

## Case study

Primary organizer:
- bounded case/system.

## II

Primary organizer:
- interactional/biographical experience and interpretation.

### Hybrid

bounded institutional case
+
II analysis

is possible.

---

# 50. II vs critical inquiry

Later II has explicit critical and political potential.

But:

> power critique alone ≠ II.

A critical qualitative study may analyze:

- ideology;
- oppression;
- discourse;
- structural inequality;

without epiphany/biographical interactional architecture.

### Rule

Classify II only when Denzin's interpretive interactional method is actually present.

---

# 51. II vs Interpretive Biography

Denzin's Interpretive Biography and II are closely related.

Interpretive Biography emphasizes:

- life documents;
- biography;
- epiphany;
- interpretation.

II more explicitly frames:

- interaction;
- personal trouble;
- public response;
- interpretive process.

### Project rule

When authors explicitly claim both, preserve both labels rather than forcing a false separation.

---

# 52. II vs autoethnography

II may include researcher personal experience and self-story.

But autoethnography centrally uses:

self
→
culture/social world.

II can center participants' biographies and does not require researcher-as-primary empirical subject.

### Rule

Researcher subjectivity ≠ autoethnography automatically.

---

# 53. Health-methodology exemplar — abused women / healthcare

The 2012 nursing methodology paper demonstrates II for healthcare experiences of women affected by domestic violence.

The paper argues that II can:

- place patient experience centrally;
- show interaction with healthcare/social systems;
- connect findings to:
  - health policy;
  - professional ethics;
  - organizational approaches to care.

### Methodological lesson

This is a strong demonstration of II's:

personal trouble
↔
institutional/public response

architecture.

---

# 54. Health evaluation relevance

Mohr's 1997 methodological paper argues that II is useful in intervention/outcomes research because it can connect:

- personal experience;
- structures;
- public policy;

through thick description and thick interpretation.

### Critical boundary

This makes II useful **within evaluation research**.

It does not make II itself an effect-estimation design.

---

# 55. Current health evidence status

Targeted 2024–2026 searches found extensive current:

- symbolic interactionism;
- interpretive ethnography;
- interpretive phenomenology;

but did **not** establish a strong, high-profile recent empirical healthcare paper using full Denzin II as explicitly as the older nursing-methodology literature.

### Project statement

**Информация не проверена** for a strong 2024–2026 empirical full-II health exemplar.

This does not affect the methodology's established health legitimacy.

# 56. Sport exemplar — rugby spinal cord injury

Sparkes & Smith studied four men who experienced spinal cord injury through rugby union.

A current systematic review classifies the study as using:

- Interpretive Interactionism;
- Interpretive Biography.

The analysis examined:

- body–self disruption;
- loss of athletic/masculine identities;
- narrative resources;
- disability;
- sport culture.

### Methodological value

This is a direct sport example where:

catastrophic embodied epiphany
+
biography
+
narrative identity
+
sport masculinity/cultural context

are analyzed together.

---

# 57. Sport exemplar — transitions as epiphanies

A study of 48 university students' sport stories used Denzin's epiphany typology.

The study identified:

- major;
- cumulative;
- illuminative/minor;
- relived;

epiphany patterns and proposed sport-specific major subtypes including bodily, life-change and success events.

### Taxonomy lesson

This is a strong example of:

> Denzin epiphany framework use.

But the publicly visible methods do not establish that the entire Denzin six-step II process was implemented.

### Rule

Classify as:

II-informed epiphany analysis

unless full architecture is documented.

# 60. Candidate fitness / rehabilitation questions suited to II

Examples:

- How does a catastrophic injury become a biographical turning point, and how do rehabilitation systems and sport culture shape its meaning?
- How does a coach's public criticism become an illuminative epiphany that changes an athlete's identity and relationship to training?
- How do repeated pain/injury episodes accumulate into withdrawal from competitive sport?
- How does a return-to-sport event relive or transform an earlier injury epiphany?
- How do gym/strength-sport institutions respond to athletes whose bodies no longer fit dominant performance identities?
- How do rehabilitation interactions transform private fear into a public/institutional problem?

### Critical condition

The question should genuinely require:

biography
+
interaction
+
turning-point meaning
+
social/institutional context.

---

# 61. Questions less suited to II

II is usually not the best parent methodology for:

- prevalence of injury fear;
- effect of rehabilitation intervention;
- hypertrophy mechanism;
- prediction of return-to-sport;
- generic barriers to exercise without biographical/interactional depth.

Route those to:

- quantitative prevalence;
- causal design;
- mechanistic study;
- prediction;
- QD/other qualitative methodology.

---

# 62. Claims II can support

II can support contextual claims about:

- biographical disruption;
- turning-point meaning;
- identity transformation;
- participant interpretation of institutional response;
- interactional construction of personal trouble;
- relationships between biography and social/cultural structure;
- policy/practice implications;
- hypotheses about social processes.

---

# 63. Claims II cannot establish by itself

II alone does not estimate:

- prevalence;
- incidence;
- risk;
- causal treatment effect;
- mediation;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism.

### Critical rule

Interpretive linkage between:

personal experience
and
institutional structure

is not automatically a causal structural effect.

---

# 64. Epiphany frequency boundary

If:

8 of 10 participants describe a major epiphany,

that supports:

> major epiphany was common in this small qualitative corpus.

It does not establish:

> 80% of the target population experiences major epiphanies.

### Rule

Epiphany types are interpretive categories, not epidemiological outcomes.

---

# 65. Transferability

II uses:

- thick description;
- contextualization;

to help readers judge transferability to other settings.

### Critical rule

Transferability ≠ statistical external validity.

The reader needs enough context to assess:

> could this interpretation plausibly illuminate a similar setting?

# 67. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| symbolic interactionism vs Interpretive Interactionism | theory vs Denzin methodology |
| memorable event vs epiphany | biographical meaning/turning-point significance required |
| epiphany typology vs full II | full architecture includes deconstruction→contextualization |
| major epiphany vs necessarily traumatic event | can be transformative without clinical trauma |
| cumulative epiphany vs one event | develops through accumulated experience |
| minor/illuminative epiphany vs trivial event | small event reveals deeper problem |
| relived epiphany vs simple memory | consequential meaning is re-experienced/reworked |
| epiphany vs causal mechanism | interpreted turning point ≠ counterfactual cause |
| six-step process vs five-phase shorthand | framing question is sometimes counted separately |
| deconstruction vs literature review | critical analysis of prior constructions |
| capture vs objective recording | empirical representation remains interpretive |
| II bracketing vs phenomenological epoché | different analytic operation |
| construction vs code aggregation | interpretive reassembly |
| contextualization vs background paragraph | context must shape interpretation |
| thick description vs long quotes | context + meaning required |
| thick interpretation vs unsupported theorizing | must remain grounded in experience/context |
| researcher subjectivity vs bias to eliminate | interpretive subjectivity must be examined |
| participant story vs narrative inquiry automatically | story can serve II's epiphany/context target |
| II vs narrative inquiry | epiphany/private-public context vs story/plot/telling |
| II vs phenomenology | biographical interaction + institutional context are distinctive |
| II vs GT | no requirement for theory generation/theoretical sampling |
| II vs critical inquiry | power critique alone is insufficient |
| II vs Interpretive Biography | closely related Denzin traditions; preserve dual labels |
| II vs autoethnography | researcher need not be primary empirical subject |
| private-public linkage vs causal effect | interpretive relation ≠ effect estimate |
| qualitative frequency vs prevalence | nonprobability interpretive corpus |
| transferability vs statistical external validity | contextual rather than probabilistic |
| intervention-evaluation usefulness vs efficacy design | II can complement evaluation, not estimate effect alone |

10. Personal trouble ↔ public/institutional response is a signature inferential movement.
