## 1. Question and scope

How can an analyst distinguish lasting change in personality from temporary state changes, changes in the situations sampled, response shift, selection and measurement error? What additional evidence is needed to attribute a change to a life transition?
Adult general-population research; nonclinical individualized inference. Claims concern longitudinal description, prediction and causal limitations, not diagnosis. Targeted literature search, not a systematic review or exhaustive reading. Questions: stability/change meta-analysis, life-event longitudinal studies, individual trajectories, measurement and replication. Competing accounts specified below. No private information used in search, examples or storage.

## 2. Evidence ledger

E1. Bleidorn et al. (2022), Personality stability and change: A meta-analysis of longitudinal studies. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000365 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35834197/
Access: indexed abstract and publisher metadata/extract; direct abstract page blocked, not full text. New longitudinal studies contributed k=189, N=178,503 for rank-order stability; k=276, N=242,542 for mean-level change. Relative stability rises earlier in life and changes little after roughly age 25; mean-level development remains possible. The age pattern is a population estimate, not a cutoff for individual change. Distinguish the two meta-analytic sets; do not add the sample sizes as unique persons.

E2. Bühler et al. (2024), Life Events and Personality Change: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. https://doi.org/10.1177/08902070231190219
Access: publisher abstract, study description, conclusion and selected results/limitations; not full end-to-end. Preregistered synthesis of 44 studies, 89 samples, 121,187 participants, 519 effects. Mean pre/post lag 2.45 years, range 0.08–15. Small event-specific associations; work-domain effects generally more consistent than love-domain effects. Sparse event-specific estimates and heterogeneous timing/designs limit interpretation; pre/post contrasts alone cannot identify a causal mechanism.

E3. Haehner, Krämer, Wright & Bleidorn, Life events and personality trait change: A coordinated data analysis, first online 2025, issue 2026. https://doi.org/10.1177/08902070251356148
Access: publisher abstract, model description, selected results, discussion and limitations inspected. Seven panels, 196,256 total (reported in indexed article copy). Time indicators capture two years before and after and more distant follow-up, accounting for age-graded trajectories under model assumptions. Approx. 12% participant overlap with E2 across events; do not treat as fully independent replication. Short self-report measures, largely Western samples, annual or less frequent measurement, coincident events and residual confounding limit individual and causal transfer.

E3c. Mandatory correction: https://doi.org/10.1177/08902070261427580 (2026). Publisher correction text and updated tables/selected replacement paragraphs inspected. A data-preparation error misencoded the indicator for assessments more than two years after an event as a linear yearly term. About 7% of estimated event dummy significance decisions changed. Corrected average absolute standardized coefficient among significant Big Five effects is .06 (original .05); self-esteem .07, life satisfaction .17. These are summaries of significant effects, NOT an unconditional mean causal effect over all outcomes or all persons. Evidence for some longer-term differences increased; overall small-effect conclusion survives. Corrected childbirth/extraversion hypothesis now supported, unlike original report; this is not a sex-specific causal estimate. Example outside relational context: corrected new-employment/conscientiousness coefficients +.06 at year one, +.05 at year two, +.04 beyond year two (99% CIs [.01,.11], [.02,.08], [.01,.07]); observational conditional estimates, not proof that the job caused change. Retain old publication date and link correction together. The prior PER-03 summary already uses .06; no numeric inconsistency there. No changes to shared cabinet.

E4. Roemer et al. (2025 issue), The base-rate and longer-term relevance of year-to-year change in personality traits. https://doi.org/10.1177/08902070241241105
Access: publisher study description, analytic methods, selected results, discussion and conclusion inspected; not full end-to-end. German probability panel (~7,000 adults, annual measures up to six years, two items per trait). Large annual raw-score movements were common: ~22% of year-to-year patterns changed by >=1 scale point. Not a prevalence estimate of permanent trait change. Much of the change later reversed; in four domains (not agreeableness), some reliable short-term shifts propagated into subsequent trajectories. Interpretation limited by coarse short scales, regression, model assumptions, lack of external behavioral criterion, and country/context. Do not convert the sample's score threshold into a universal individual diagnostic rule.

E5. Krämer et al. (2025), A replication attempt of personality change through randomized peer groups. https://doi.org/10.1177/08902070251331355
Access: publisher discussion and robustness-check descriptions inspected. Apparent assimilation to peer group personality persisted when group labels were shuffled, grand mean replaced group mean, and time order was reversed. Authors concluded that regression to the mean explains those apparent effects. Random assignment addresses selection into assigned groups but does not rescue a biased outcome/change statistic. Does not prove peer socialization impossible.

E6. Stieger et al. (2021), Changing personality traits with the help of a digital personality change intervention. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2017548118
Access: indexed primary article excerpts/abstract, not full text. Trial N=1,523, self-selected change goals, 3-month program; comparison with a one-month delayed-start group supports potential for intentional change under tested conditions. No permanent randomized untreated comparator or evidence that another person can reliably change someone's personality without their involvement.

E7. Follow-up of E6, One year later: Longer-term maintenance effects of a digital intervention to change personality traits. https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12898
Access: indexed abstract/extract. Initial N=1,523, post n=554, 3-month n=437, 1-year n=157. Large attrition and selected follow-up limit generalization about durability; this is not independent replication of E6.

E8. Haehner et al. (2026), Does the variance of personality traits change across the lifespan? https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000523 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42406478/
Access: indexed abstract only. Meta-analysis of 362 longitudinal studies, N=398,124, reports broadly stable population variance across life. This does not imply each person retains the same trait score or relative rank.

Evidence limits: no full article read end to end, no raw-data reanalysis; E2/E3/E3c/E4/E5 provide inspectable substantive text, others abstract-level support. This bounded review supports inference rules, not comprehensive developmental-mechanism certification. Direct checks of further 2026 individual-change literature were impeded by access restrictions and remain open. Search terms included "personality stability change lifespan meta analysis", "life events personality coordinated data analysis", "personality change measurement invariance", "randomized peer groups regression to mean", "digital personality change intervention". No specific private context was used.

## 3. Integrated inference model

Separate six estimands: (a) population mean change, (b) rank-order stability, (c) population variance change, (d) individual absolute change, (e) within-person state distribution/variability, (f) stability or change in context-conditioned behavior. None logically entails another.

A conceptual decomposition for repeated comparable observations, not an identified structural equation: observed difference = possible stable tendency change + temporary state difference + context composition/affordance change + measurement/response shift + residual error. Interactions and selection mean the components need not be additive in reality. A new role can alter opportunities, practiced behavior, goals, standards for self-rating and trait expression simultaneously.

Competing trajectory hypotheses:
H0 measurement fluctuation/regression to mean;
H1 temporary state shift followed by recovery;
H2 changed context mix or role-specific adaptation, stable comparable-context pattern;
H3 enduring shift in a comparable-context pattern or broader state distribution;
H4 pre-existing change/selection predates the attributed event;
H5 multiple concurrent processes. Distinguish whether change occurred from why it occurred. A role-specific durable pattern may be real and important without global trait change. Nonlinear, delayed and partly reversible development are possible; do not impose a monotone progression or treat age as mechanism.

Practical examples of discrimination: repeated baseline and follow-up measurements with matched tasks/contexts, more than one source when appropriate, comparable construct and observation window, pre-event trend, competing transitions, explicit missingness and opportunity checks. If opportunity or response standard shifts, make that a separate claim. Where scales exist, inspect reliability and longitudinal invariance; a reliable-change statistic can address some measurement noise, not all context or causal uncertainty. Without scales, preserve concrete observations and avoid numeric trait estimates. No universal time or observation-count threshold proves durability.

Analytic output: describe what changed, in which domain/context, for how long observed, evidence quality, leading alternatives, what would update them, and forecast only within supported conditions. Unobserved persistence and mechanisms remain hypotheses. Genuine changes may coexist with continuity in other domains. Population patterns are priors, not individual biographies. Never design distress, deception or coercion as a way to test change.

## 4. Practice checks — self-assessment only

Rubric: per item identify estimand, competing account and appropriate evidence boundary. Critical failure: unsupported causal/diagnostic certainty or group-to-individual transfer. Four independent-of-private-material invented scenarios; no external examiner.

A. All members of an invented cohort gain two units on a hypothetical trait scale with rank order unchanged. Rank stability and mean shift coexist. Check comparability of measurement; no causal conclusion. Three criteria met.
B. A student reports less curiosity after moving from exploratory coursework to routine work; the old hobbies remain unchanged. Possible context composition or role-specific expression rather than global trait decline. Compare similar activities across time. Three criteria met.
C. A group selected for extremely low initial ratings later approaches the population mean. Regression to mean, changed measurement and real improvement compete. Repeated baseline and appropriate comparison/control analysis are required before attributing a cause. Three criteria met.
D. Following an independently chosen creative course, a volunteer demonstrates a new habit across settings for several months. This is stronger evidence of sustained behavioral change than one rating; it is not proof of irreversible trait transformation or of a uniquely causal course effect. Assess maintenance, pre-trend and alternatives. Three criteria met.
