# Part A — Equity is not the same as diversity or heterogeneity

## 1. Equity has a fairness component

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane defines health equity as the absence of avoidable and unfair differences in health.

Therefore an observed difference between two populations is not automatically an equity issue.

Project distinction:

### Difference / heterogeneity
Groups differ.

### Applicability
Evidence may not transport well from one group/context to another.

### Equity
A difference is tied to avoidable/unfair disadvantage, access, burden, opportunity or distribution of benefit/harm.

These concepts overlap but are not interchangeable.

---

## 2. Not every subgroup variable is an equity variable

Examples of variables that may matter scientifically without being equity questions by themselves:

- training status;
- baseline strength;
- exercise experience;
- intervention duration;
- laboratory vs home setting.

They may affect:
- effect modification;
- generalizability;
- feasibility.

But they are not automatically axes of unfair disadvantage.

Project rule:
label a factor as equity-relevant only when there is a justified fairness/disadvantage pathway.

---

# Part B — PROGRESS-Plus

## 3. PROGRESS-Plus is an equity lens

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane uses PROGRESS-Plus to help identify populations that may experience inequity:

- Place of residence;
- Race/ethnicity/culture/language;
- Occupation;
- Gender/sex;
- Religion;
- Education;
- Socio-economic status;
- Social capital;
- Plus: other characteristics such as age, disability, sexual orientation and context-specific factors.

### Project consequence

PROGRESS-Plus is a **prompting framework**, not a mandatory instruction to analyze every category.

Cochrane explicitly notes that it is usually not feasible or appropriate to assess all PROGRESS-Plus characteristics.

---

## 4. Relevance must be prespecified

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

At question-formulation stage, reviewers should identify:
- which populations may experience inequity;
- which characteristics plausibly matter;
- how those characteristics will be addressed.

Project rule:
for each proposed equity factor ask:

1. What disadvantage/fairness pathway is hypothesized?
2. Could it alter access, exposure, intervention delivery, baseline risk, benefit, harm or burden?
3. Is the factor relevant to the intended users?
4. Is evidence likely to support meaningful analysis?
5. What will we do differently if the hypothesis is supported?

If there is no plausible pathway or decision consequence, do not add the factor merely for completeness.

# Part C — Context and applicability

## 5. Context can change relevance without being an equity issue

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane emphasizes that intervention relevance and effects can depend on context.

Context can include:
- health-system or service setting;
- geography;
- delivery environment;
- resource availability;
- cultural/social conditions;
- baseline risk;
- implementation environment.

Exercise translation:
- supervised lab;
- commercial gym;
- unsupervised home setting;
- limited equipment;
- high vs low coaching contact
can differ in applicability even when equity is not the main question.

---

## 6. Applicability is a target-population question

### Project synthesis grounded in Cochrane
**Confidence: HIGH**

For every decision-facing review question distinguish:

1. **study population/context**;
2. **review target population/context**;
3. **eventual application target**.

A study can be internally valid but indirect for the target application.

Project example:
evidence from highly supervised trained athletes may be scientifically sound but less directly applicable to unsupervised home training.

This is an applicability issue first; whether it is also an equity issue requires a separate fairness/disadvantage argument.

---

## 7. Specific populations may require separate scope or synthesis

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane allows:
- a broad review with prespecified subgroup/specific-population analyses;
- or a focused review for a specific population
when relevance is sufficiently distinct.

Project rule:
create a separate synthesis/question when:
1. mechanism or implementation materially differs;
2. baseline risk makes absolute effects materially different;
3. intervention is delivered differently;
4. the population has distinct benefit/harm trade-offs;
5. combining populations would obscure the actual decision.

Do not split populations merely because demographic information exists.

---

# Part D — Equity hypotheses must precede subgroup fishing

## 8. Equity-related subgroup analysis needs a rationale

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane recommends prespecifying relevant equity/specific-population analyses and interpreting subgroup analyses cautiously.

Project consequence:
do not inspect many demographic variables after seeing results and elevate whichever subgroup happens to differ.

Before analysis, record:
- hypothesis;
- direction if defensible;
- plausible mechanism/context pathway;
- decision relevance.

Detailed interaction/meta-regression standards remain for later RES topics.

---

## 9. Average effects can hide distributional differences

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane notes that average effects can obscure differences in:
- relative effects;
- absolute effects;
- baseline risk;
- access;
- feasibility;
- burden.

Project consequence:
even when relative efficacy is similar, practical benefit may differ because:
- baseline opportunity differs;
- intervention costs differ;
- access differs;
- burden differs.

This becomes especially important for population recommendations.

---

# Part E — Equity vs individual personalization

## 10. Individualization is not automatically equity analysis

### Project distinction
**Confidence: HIGH**

This project ultimately personalizes a program for one person.

Personalization variables may include:
- goals;
- training status;
- equipment;
- preference;
- tolerance;
- schedule;
- adherence characteristics.

These are not automatically equity variables.

### Rule

Use two separate fields:

1. **Applicability / personalization factors**
2. **Equity-relevant factors**

A factor may belong to both, but only with explicit justification.

---

## 11. A one-person application changes the weight of equity, not the science

### Project application
**Confidence: HIGH**

For the eventual individual decision:

- population-wide distributional equity may be less central than in a public guideline;
- applicability to the target user becomes highly central;
- equity still matters if the evidence base systematically excludes or poorly represents a population characteristic relevant to the user or if access/resource constraints create a meaningful disadvantage pathway.

Project consequence:
we do not force a population-policy equity analysis onto every fitness topic.

But we do ask:
> Is the available evidence systematically indirect because relevant populations/settings are missing?

---

## 12. Evidence absence for a group is not evidence of no difference

### Established methodological rule
**Confidence: HIGH**

If a subgroup/specific population is underrepresented or unreported, we cannot infer:
- equal effects;
- unequal effects.

Correct label:
**uncertain applicability / evidence gap**.

Project rule:
do not manufacture subgroup conclusions from absence of data.

---

# Part F — Values, burden and access

## 13. Equity can enter through implementation, not only biological effect

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane and GRADE recognize that interventions can affect equity through:
- access;
- affordability;
- feasibility;
- acceptability;
- distribution of burdens/benefits.

Exercise example:
two programs with similar physiological efficacy may differ substantially in:
- equipment cost;
- access to facilities;
- supervision requirements;
- time burden.

That can produce different real-world reach even if biological response is similar.

---

## 14. Relative and absolute effects can tell different equity stories

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane recommends considering both relative and absolute effects for specific populations when baseline risk differs.

Project translation:
for many fitness outcomes, analogous baseline-state differences may alter absolute gain or practical value even when relative effects appear similar.

Do not equate same relative effect with same practical benefit automatically.

Exact effect-measure methodology remains RES-09.

# Part G — Project fields

## 15. Add two distinct blocks to Question Card

### A. Applicability / context

1. Review target population
2. Review target setting/context
3. Eventual application target
4. Key applicability modifiers
5. Expected sources of indirectness
6. Populations/settings likely missing from evidence
7. Need for separate synthesis?
8. Rationale

### B. Equity

1. Is equity materially relevant? YES / NO / UNCERTAIN
2. Equity definition for this question
3. Population(s) potentially experiencing disadvantage
4. Relevant PROGRESS-Plus dimensions
5. Hypothesized pathway to inequity
6. Could intervention reduce/worsen inequity?
7. Equity-relevant outcomes/burdens/access issues
8. Planned separate analysis/synthesis, if any
9. Evidence limitations
10. Why omitted dimensions were not prioritized

---

## 16. Do not make PROGRESS-Plus a mechanical checklist

### Project rule
**Confidence: HIGH**

Bad implementation:
> run every topic through every PROGRESS-Plus category and create dozens of subgroup questions.

Better implementation:
1. scan PROGRESS-Plus;
2. identify plausible relevant axes;
3. justify them;
4. prespecify only those with a scientific/decision pathway;
5. document evidence gaps.

This reduces:
- multiplicity;
- subgroup fishing;
- performative complexity.

---

## 17. Current fitness-project default

For general resistance-training evidence questions, initial high-priority **applicability** factors may include:
- training status;
- age range;
- health status;
- sex when biologically/empirically relevant;
- supervision;
- training setting;
- available resistance/equipment;
- intervention duration;
- baseline performance.

These are hypotheses for applicability, not automatically equity dimensions.

Possible **equity** dimensions are only activated when there is a plausible disadvantage/access/fairness pathway.

No user-specific sensitive characteristic is stored in this public research layer.

---

## 18. Practical rules produced by PASS-07B

1. Separate equity, heterogeneity and applicability conceptually.
2. Equity requires a justified unfair/avoidable disadvantage pathway.
3. Use PROGRESS-Plus as a lens, not an exhaustive subgroup checklist.
4. Prespecify equity-relevant populations/factors when they matter.
5. Record a plausible pathway from characteristic → access/baseline risk/delivery/benefit/harm/burden.
6. Context can alter applicability without being an equity issue.
7. Distinguish study population, review target population and application target.
8. Split specific populations into separate syntheses only when the decision requires it.
9. Do not perform post-hoc demographic subgroup fishing.
10. Average effects may conceal distributional differences.
11. Keep personalization factors separate from equity factors.
12. Underrepresentation implies uncertain applicability, not evidence of equality or inequality.
13. Consider access, affordability, feasibility and burden as possible equity pathways.
14. Add distinct Applicability and Equity blocks to Question Card.
15. Do not store sensitive personal equity characteristics in the public repository.
