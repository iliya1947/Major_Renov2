# 2. Canonical scientific target

Situational Analysis asks:

**What human, nonhuman, material, discursive, institutional, historical and political elements constitute this situation, how are they related, which social worlds/arenas organize collective action, and which positions are articulated, contested, missing or silenced?**

Its target is the **dense relational situation** rather than only:

- one human action/process;
- one bounded case;
- one culture;
- one discourse;
- one set of participant themes.

### Canonical project representation

situation as analytic unit
+
heterogeneous elements
+
mapping/memoing
+
relations
+
social worlds/arenas
+
positions/silences
+
iterative empirical/theoretical sampling
+
situated interpretive claims.

---

# 3. Genealogy

SA was developed by Adele Clarke as an extension/reworking of grounded theory after the postmodern/poststructural/interpretive turn.

Its roots include:

- Straussian grounded theory;
- symbolic interactionism;
- pragmatism;
- Strauss's social worlds/arenas framework.

Clarke/Friese/Washburn additionally integrate:

- Foucauldian discourse analytics;
- feminist theory;
- poststructuralism;
- materiality/nonhuman attention;
- assemblage/rhizomatic influences.

### Critical rule

SA belongs historically to the GT family but is not simply:

> “Straussian GT plus visual diagrams.”

The analytic object and endpoint are materially different.

---

# 4. Situation as the fundamental analytic unit

In SA, **the situation itself becomes the fundamental unit of analysis**.

This is a major departure from conventional GT's emphasis on:

- basic social process;
- human action;
- core category.

The situation includes all consequential elements that make a difference within it.

### Project rule

Do not code:

> unit of analysis = participant

merely because interviews were conducted.

The analytic unit may be the **whole situation**.

---

# 5. Situation is not just context around actors

A central SA principle is that “conditions” are **inside the situation**, not external background variables framing it from outside.

Potential constitutive elements:

- people;
- organizations;
- equipment;
- buildings;
- technologies;
- guidelines;
- discourses;
- finances;
- laws;
- time;
- history;
- identities;
- absent actors;
- biological/material objects.

### Critical rule

Context ≠ decorative background.

If an element materially conditions what can happen, SA can treat it as part of the situation itself.

---

# 6. Messiness rather than premature simplification

SA deliberately begins from the assumption that social situations are:

- heterogeneous;
- contradictory;
- incomplete;
- contingent;
- locally specific;
- relational.

Its maps are designed to resist premature reduction to one central process.

### Current 2026 formulation

Christianson & Thille emphasize questions such as:

- Who and what are in the situation?
- Who and what matter?
- Which elements make a difference?

### Rule

Early complexity is not methodological failure.

The purpose of mapping is to interrogate it systematically.

---

# 7. Three major map families

Clarke's core SA architecture uses:

1. **Situational maps**;
2. **Social worlds/arenas maps**;
3. **Positional maps**.

These do different analytic jobs.

### Critical rule

Using one mind map or conceptual diagram does not establish SA.

The study should demonstrate SA-consistent mapping/relational reasoning.

---

# 8. Situational maps

Situational maps lay out the major elements within the situation.

Possible elements:

- individual human actors;
- collective human actors;
- implicated/silent actors;
- nonhuman actants;
- discourses;
- political/economic elements;
- temporal elements;
- spatial elements;
- sociocultural elements;
- symbolic elements;
- contested issues.

### Scientific purpose

Make the heterogeneity of the situation visible before reducing it.

---

# 9. Messy situational map

The **messy map** is an intentionally open inventory.

Researchers put onto the map:

- everything in the empirical material that appears potentially consequential;
- without first forcing elements into a clean categorical hierarchy.

### Value

It helps reveal:

- overlooked elements;
- unusual relations;
- hidden assumptions;
- nonhuman/material actors;
- silences.

### Rule

“Messy” does not mean careless.

It is a structured anti-premature-closure device.

---

# 10. Ordered situational map

The **ordered map** reorganizes the messy elements into provisional categories.

Clarke provides suggested headings, but these are heuristics rather than immutable ontology.

### Purpose

- ensure domains are not overlooked;
- make complexity inspectable;
- prepare relational analysis.

### Critical rule

The ordered map should not become a fixed codebook that freezes the analysis too early.

---

# 11. Relational analysis

After situational elements are identified, SA systematically explores relations among them.

Typical procedure:

take one element
→ examine relation to each other element
→ draw/describe connections
→ memo the relationship
→ repeat.

### Scientific value

This can reveal relations that would remain invisible in theme-based analysis.

### Rule

A situational map without relational interrogation is incomplete SA use.

---

# 12. Maps are analytic devices, not final illustrations

Maps can appear in publications, but their primary purpose is **analytic provocation**.

They help researchers:

- ask new questions;
- decide what to sample next;
- detect absences;
- compare relationships;
- develop memos.

### Critical rule

A polished final figure created after thematic analysis does not retroactively turn a study into Situational Analysis.

---

# 13. Social worlds

A **social world** is a collective actor organized around shared commitments, activities or discourse.

Examples in rehabilitation:

- physical therapists;
- students;
- educators;
- administrators;
- patient advocacy groups;
- insurers;
- professional associations.

### Important boundary

Social world ≠ demographic group automatically.

The group needs some form of collective commitment/action/discourse.

---

# 14. Arenas

An **arena** is a broader site/domain where multiple social worlds intersect, negotiate and contest issues.

Examples:

- clinical education;
- rehabilitation service delivery;
- return-to-work policy;
- professional regulation.

### Social worlds/arenas map asks

- Which collective actors are engaged?
- Where do their commitments overlap?
- What negotiations/conflicts occur?
- Which groups are peripheral or absent?

---

# 15. Social worlds/arenas maps are meso-level

This map particularly supports analysis of:

- organizations;
- professional groups;
- institutions;
- collectivities.

It therefore adds a meso-level architecture between:

- individual accounts;
- macro discourse.

### Critical rule

Do not reduce social worlds/arenas map to:

> stakeholder list.

Relationships, commitments and arenas of action matter.

---

# 16. Boundaries are porous

SA does not assume that social worlds have rigid fixed membership.

Individuals and groups may:

- participate in multiple worlds;
- move between worlds;
- hold contradictory commitments.

### Rule

Do not force every participant into one mutually exclusive social-world box.

---

# 17. Positional maps

Positional maps lay out positions articulated **and not articulated** around important axes of controversy/difference in the situation.

They answer:

> What positions are possible or present on this issue, including marginalized/silent positions?

### Key rule

Positional maps map **positions, not persons**.

One person or collective can hold:

- multiple;
- changing;
- contradictory;

positions.

---

# 18. Position ≠ participant type

This distinction is critical.

Wrong:
> “Coach A is the high-autonomy position.”

Better:
> The discourse contains a high-autonomy position; Coach A may articulate it in some contexts and another position elsewhere.

### Project rule

Do not transform positional maps into psychological typologies.

---

# 19. Axes in positional maps

Researchers select axes representing analytically important tensions.

Example:

x-axis:
> standardized protocol ↔ individualized judgment

y-axis:
> athlete autonomy low ↔ high.

Positions are then mapped across the discursive space.

### Critical rule

Axes should arise from empirical/analytic significance, not arbitrary Cartesian convenience.

---

# 20. Positions not taken

One of SA's most distinctive features is attention to **positions not articulated in the data**.

An empty region may indicate:

- silence;
- taboo;
- excluded possibility;
- unthinkable position;
- sampling gap;
- genuinely irrelevant position.

### Rule

Absence is a question, not instant evidence of suppression.

Researchers may need further theoretical sampling before interpreting silence.

---

# 21. Silences

Clarke emphasizes that positional maps can expose silent or silenced positions.

This can trigger:

- further data collection;
- memoing;
- cross-site comparison.

### Critical boundary

“Not found in current data” ≠ “institution actively silenced it.”

The latter needs evidence.

---

# 22. Nonhuman elements

SA explicitly takes nonhuman elements seriously.

Examples:

- technologies;
- exercise equipment;
- software;
- forms;
- rooms;
- schedules;
- guidelines;
- wearable sensors;
- media;
- medications;
- architectural spaces.

### Scientific rationale

Material properties can condition:

- actions;
- interactions;
- possibilities;
- exclusions.

### Fitness example

A barbell, squat rack, wearable readiness score or gym layout can materially alter what training practices become possible.

---

# 23. Nonhuman ≠ mystical agency

SA can treat nonhuman/material elements as consequential without claiming they possess human intentionality.

### Preferred wording

- shapes;
- conditions;
- enables;
- constrains;
- participates in relational configuration.

### Rule

Avoid anthropomorphizing equipment/software unless the theoretical framework explicitly warrants that language.

---

# 24. Discourses as constitutive elements

SA can include discourses as elements of the situation.

Examples:

- evidence-based practice;
- athletic toughness;
- patient-centered care;
- injury prevention;
- personal responsibility;
- professional expertise.

These can be analyzed alongside:

- people;
- organizations;
- technologies;
- materialities.

### Critical rule

Discourse is not merely what participants say.

It can organize which positions/actions are available or legitimate.

---

# 25. Historical and temporal elements

The situation can include:

- prior policy history;
- professional traditions;
- sequence of reforms;
- seasonal sport cycles;
- past injuries;
- technological changes.

### Rule

A situation is not necessarily a frozen present-time snapshot.

Historical forces can be constitutive if empirically relevant.

---

# 26. Implicated actors

SA can distinguish actors who are:

- physically present;
- discursively represented;
- absent but consequential.

Examples:

- patients discussed in meetings but not present;
- governing bodies whose standards shape practice;
- family members affected by rehabilitation schedules.

### Critical rule

An implicated actor should be supported by data showing relevance to the situation.

Do not populate maps with hypothetical actors simply because they could matter.

---

# 27. Empirical constraint

The 2026 methods brief stresses that elements should be found in the empirical situation.

SA is abductive and iterative:

anticipated element absent
→ gather/check more data
→ either include when supported or discard.

### Rule

Situational maps are not free-form brainstorming detached from evidence.

---

# 28. Coding in SA

SA retains useful GT practices:

- coding;
- constant comparison;
- memoing;
- iterative analysis.

But detailed GT category saturation is not the only route.

Clarke allows mapping to become a central engine of analysis.

### Current applied guidance

Careful reading and partial coding may be sufficient to begin mapping; maps then guide deeper interrogation.

---

# 29. Memoing

Memoing is central.

Memos record:

- why an element matters;
- hypothesized relations;
- contradictions;
- positional axes;
- absent voices;
- sampling questions;
- researcher reactions.

### Rule

Maps without memos risk becoming descriptive inventories rather than analytic SA.

---

# 30. Theoretical sampling

Clarke retains theoretical sampling as an important GT inheritance.

In SA, theoretical sampling can follow:

- missing element;
- uncertain relation;
- silent position;
- social world;
- contradictory discourse;
- material actor.

### Example

Positional map shows no position combining:

high athlete autonomy + strict standardized protocol.

Researcher may deliberately sample settings where that combination might emerge.

### Rule

This is empirical follow-up driven by the developing analysis.

---

# 31. Theoretical sampling does not mean sampling theories

As in GT, theoretical sampling means:

> collecting new empirical data relevant to an emerging analytic question.

It does not mean:

> selecting whichever theory best fits after analysis.

---

# 32. Difference rather than forced commonality

SA deliberately foregrounds:

- variation;
- contradiction;
- marginalized positions;
- exceptions;
- multiple social worlds.

This counters a tendency to reduce qualitative data to only the most common themes.

### Project value

A minority position can be analytically central even when numerically rare.

---

# 33. Complexity vs explanatory simplification

Conventional GT often aims toward a parsimonious theoretical process.

SA intentionally preserves more complexity where simplification would erase:

- materiality;
- discourse;
- power;
- contradictory positions;
- multi-level relations.

### Critical rule

A more complex final map is not automatically inferior to a neat core category.

The design has a different analytic objective.

---

# 34. SA vs conventional grounded theory

## Conventional GT

Typical target:
- process/action;
- categories;
- substantive theory;
- often core/basic social process.

## Situational Analysis

Typical target:
- situation;
- heterogeneous relational ecology;
- social worlds/arenas;
- positions/silences;
- human/nonhuman/discursive relations.

### Shared roots

Both may use:

- iterative collection/analysis;
- coding;
- constant comparison;
- memoing;
- theoretical sampling.

### Critical rule

SA should not be coded simply as:
> grounded theory study

when the situation/maps are the principal analytic architecture.

Better:
> Situational Analysis — GT-derived methodology.

---

# 35. SA vs constructivist GT

SA shares strong constructionist/interpretive commitments with constructivist GT.

But SA shifts the analytic center from:

> constructing a process/theory around human action

toward:

> mapping the whole situation and its heterogeneous relations.

### Rule

Charmaz GT and Clarke SA are adjacent but not interchangeable.

---

# 36. SA vs case study

## Case study

Scientific unit:
- a bounded case.

## SA

Scientific unit:
- a situation, whose relevant relations may extend across:
  - sites;
  - organizations;
  - historical periods;
  - material/discursive domains.

### Critical rule

Situation ≠ bounded case automatically.

A case study can use SA as an analysis method, but the design layers should remain explicit.

---

# 37. SA vs ethnography

Ethnography targets:

- culture;
- shared practice;
- social meaning in context.

SA can use ethnographic observations but does not require culture to be the primary object.

### Hybrid example

A rehabilitation-ward ethnography used situational analysis to analyze fieldnotes/interviews.

Correct representation:

ethnographic parent study
+
Situational Analysis analytic approach.

### Rule

Using observation does not make SA an ethnography.

---

# 38. SA vs discourse analysis

SA incorporates discourse analysis but does not reduce the entire situation to discourse.

It deliberately maps discourse alongside:

- humans;
- institutions;
- materialities;
- technologies.

### Boundary

If language/power/discourse is the central scientific object:
> discourse methodology may be parent.

If discourse is one constitutive element among many:
> SA may fit better.

---

# 39. SA vs institutional ethnography

Both may examine:

- institutions;
- texts;
- coordination;
- power.

But their architectures differ.

## Institutional ethnography

Starts from standpoint/work
→ traces local to translocal ruling relations.

## Situational Analysis

Starts from the situation
→ maps all consequential elements, worlds/arenas and positions.

### Rule

Local→translocal work tracing is IE-specific.

Situational cartography/position mapping is SA-specific.

---

# 40. SA vs actor-network-type approaches

SA's attention to human/nonhuman elements can look similar to actor-network approaches.

However SA remains grounded in:

- interactionist/GT genealogy;
- social worlds/arenas;
- positional mapping;
- Clarke's mapping method.

### Rule

Do not infer actor-network theory merely because nonhuman actants are mapped.

---

# 41. SA vs generic “situational analysis”

This is an especially important terminology trap.

In health policy and systems literature, “situational analysis” may simply mean:

- needs assessment;
- service-capacity assessment;
- environmental scan;
- descriptive review.

### Taxonomy rule

Classify as Clarke-derived SA only when evidence shows features such as:

- Clarke citation;
- situation as unit;
- situational/social-world/positional maps;
- GT-derived memoing/coding;
- relational mapping.

Otherwise:
> situational assessment — not Clarke SA.

---

# 42. Data sources

SA can use:

- interviews;
- observations;
- ethnographic fieldnotes;
- documents;
- visual materials;
- narratives;
- archives;
- websites;
- historical texts;
- digital data.

### Critical rule

No one collection method is constitutive.

The methodology is defined by the situational relational analytic architecture.

---

# 43. Multisite research

SA is particularly suited to situations extending across multiple locations.

Maps can expose:

- common worlds;
- local differences;
- cross-site materialities;
- institutional relations;
- positions that appear only in some sites.

### Critical rule

Multisite ≠ SA automatically.

The mapping logic still must be present.

---

# 44. Quality architecture

Strong SA should demonstrate:

1. clear definition/bounding of the situation;
2. sufficient empirical material for major elements;
3. inclusion of relevant human and nonhuman elements;
4. explicit mapping procedure;
5. relational analysis;
6. memoing;
7. social-world/arena reasoning where relevant;
8. positional mapping where controversy/difference warrants it;
9. attention to absent/silent positions;
10. theoretical sampling when gaps matter;
11. reflexivity;
12. transparent movement from maps to final claims;
13. preservation of contradictory evidence;
14. claims calibrated to the empirical situation.

---

# 45. Adequacy / stopping logic

There is no universal SA participant N.

Adequacy concerns whether:

- consequential elements are sufficiently identified;
- important relationships are interrogated;
- social worlds/arenas are adequately mapped;
- relevant positions/silences have been explored;
- further sampling yields diminishing analytic change.

### Critical rule

Generic thematic saturation alone is not an adequate SA stopping criterion.

---

# 46. Maps are provisional and iterative

Researchers should expect:

- multiple messy maps;
- revisions;
- alternative relational maps;
- multiple positional maps.

### Rule

One final clean map shown in a paper may conceal extensive analytic iteration.

Appraisal should seek evidence of process, not merely final graphics.

---

# 47. Reflexivity

SA explicitly requires attention to how the researcher constructs:

- the situation boundary;
- element inclusion;
- relation interpretation;
- positional axes;
- silence.

### Critical question

> Which elements did the researcher make visible or invisible through mapping choices?

### Rule

Maps are analytic constructions, not objective photographs of reality.

---

# 48. Power

SA can analyze power through:

- social worlds;
- institutions;
- discourse;
- material arrangements;
- positions available/unavailable.

But it does not require one universal theory of power.

### Critical rule

Do not treat a position as powerful simply because many participants articulate it.

Power requires contextual/relational analysis.

---

# 49. Physical-therapy exemplar — emerging embodiment of movement

Covington & Barcinas studied five physical-therapy clinical instructors and five students.

Data were collected:

- beginning;
- midpoint;
- final phase of clinical internships.

Methods included:

- interviews;
- observations;
- document analysis.

SA coding/mapping produced 11 maps, later reduced into five instructor behaviors:

- adapt;
- prepare;
- enhance;
- connect;
- develop.

### Methodological value

This is direct evidence that SA has practical use in physical-therapy education and movement-related professional practice.

### Caveat

The final output is relatively thematic/trajectory-oriented; future appraisal should check how completely all three Clarke map families were used before treating it as a maximal SA exemplar.

---

# 50. Physiotherapy / elderly-care exemplar

Öhman, Keisu & Enberg used grounded-theory Situational Analysis with:

- physiotherapists;
- occupational therapists;
- nurses;

across six elderly-care institutions.

The study identified three discursive professional positions regarding:

- professional ambition;
- organizational support;
- evidence-based practice.

### Methodological value

Shows the usefulness of positional/discursive analysis for rehabilitation professionals embedded in organizational conditions.

---

# 51. Rehabilitation-ward exemplar

A 2024 ethnographic study of psychiatric rehabilitation used:

- six months participant observation;
- interviews with 9 patients;
- interviews with 14 staff;

and analyzed fieldnotes/interviews using situational analysis.

### Design lesson

This demonstrates a hybrid architecture:

ethnographic study
+
Situational Analysis.

### Critical rule

SA can be either:
- the primary methodology;
- or an analytic methodology within a broader parent design.

Store both when applicable.

# 52. 2026 health-methods relevance

The 2026 Family Practice methods brief explicitly presents SA as useful for complex healthcare teams and emphasizes:

- social/material complexity;
- relationships;
- differences;
- neglected groups;
- differing values/preferences/evidence.

One author is affiliated with Physical Therapy.

### Fitness implication

SA is plausibly well suited to:

- interdisciplinary rehabilitation teams;
- athlete-support teams;
- coach–physio–S&C coordination;
- technology-mediated training environments.

---

# 53. Candidate fitness questions suited to SA

Examples:

- How do athlete, coach, S&C staff, wearable technologies, injury metrics and performance discourses jointly constitute return-to-training decisions?
- Which social worlds and positions shape whether pain is treated as warning, weakness or normal adaptation?
- How do equipment, space, coaching norms and gender discourses configure belonging in a strength gym?
- Which positions on athlete autonomy are present or absent across medical, coaching and performance worlds?
- How do monitoring software and organizational expectations alter athlete-load decision making?

### Critical condition

Question should genuinely require:

> heterogeneous relational situation analysis.

A simple “what do athletes think?” question does not.

---

# 54. Claims SA can support

SA can strongly support claims about:

- relational configuration of a situation;
- consequential human/nonhuman elements;
- social worlds and arenas;
- institutional/material/discursive conditions;
- positions taken/not taken;
- silences;
- contradictions;
- conditionality;
- local complexity;
- hypothesized relational pathways for further testing.

---

# 55. Claims SA cannot establish by itself

SA alone does not establish:

- population prevalence;
- incidence;
- average treatment effect;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism;
- quantitative causal mediation;
- universal frequency of a position.

### Critical rule

A relation on a situational map is an **analytic relation**, not automatically a causal edge.

---

# 56. Positional frequency boundary

Positional maps are not prevalence charts.

A frequently articulated position is not necessarily:

- most common in population;
- most powerful;
- most causally important.

A silent/rare position may be analytically central.

### Rule

Do not add percentages to positional maps unless a separate quantitative design supports them.
