# PSY-RES-07 — Clinical versus non-clinical inference boundaries

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Establish when psychological observations may support:

1. a description of normal variation;
2. a symptom-level statement;
3. a screening concern;
4. a syndrome-level hypothesis;
5. a diagnostic hypothesis;
6. a formal clinical diagnosis.

The goal is to prevent ordinary variation, isolated symptoms, screening scores, distress, impairment, or psychologically vivid behavior from being silently converted into a disorder label.

## Core rule

**Observation -> symptom candidate -> pattern/course/context -> impairment/distress/risk -> exclusions/differential -> diagnostic hypothesis -> qualified clinical diagnosis.**

Never:

**salient behavior / high score / familiar pattern -> disorder label.**

## 1. Normal variation and mental disorder are not separated by unusualness alone

WHO's ICD-11 framework defines mental, behavioural, and neurodevelopmental disorders as syndromes involving clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotional regulation, or behaviour that reflects dysfunction in underlying psychological, biological, or developmental processes.

These disturbances are **usually** associated with distress or impairment in important areas of functioning.

Therefore:

- unusual behavior is not automatically disordered;
- social nonconformity is not automatically psychopathology;
- distress is not automatically a disorder;
- impairment is not automatically a disorder;
- statistical rarity is not automatically pathology.

The clinical boundary is multidimensional and diagnosis-specific.

## 2. Distress and impairment are important but not universal shortcuts

Distress and functional impairment are major signals of clinical significance.

Relevant functioning domains can include:

- personal/self-care;
- family;
- social;
- educational;
- occupational;
- other important activities.

But avoid the simplistic rule:

`distress or impairment present -> mental disorder`.

WHO uses "usually associated" rather than making distress/impairment a universal standalone definition.

Some disorders emphasize risk, behavioral dysfunction, developmental impairment, loss of reality testing, or other diagnosis-specific features.

Conversely, major distress can occur in non-disordered responses to life events.

## 3. Expected human responses are not disorders merely because they are painful

ICD-11 explicitly uses bereavement as an example of the normality boundary: sadness, emptiness, cognitive or behavioral disturbance, and even functional impact can occur after loss without necessarily representing an underlying disorder.

Therefore the analyst must ask:

1. Is the response expectable in context?
2. Is its form, duration, intensity, or course outside the relevant expected range?
3. Does the diagnostic system define additional required features?
4. Is another process a better explanation?

Context can change the meaning of the same observable symptom.

## 4. Social deviance is not psychopathology by itself

Political, religious, sexual, interpersonal, or other behavior that differs from prevailing social norms is not sufficient evidence of a mental disorder.

Clinical inference requires evidence of disorder-specific dysfunction, not merely norm violation.

Canonical rule:

**deviance from a social norm != evidence of psychopathology.**

## 5. Symptom is not disorder

A symptom is a feature that may occur in:

- one disorder;
- multiple disorders;
- a subclinical state;
- ordinary variation;
- acute stress;
- sleep deprivation;
- substance/medication effects;
- medical or neurological conditions;
- developmental transitions;
- situational reactions.

Therefore:

`symptom present -> diagnosis`

is invalid.

Diagnostic reasoning is pattern-based, time-sensitive, context-sensitive, and differential.

## 6. Symptom count is not enough by itself

Diagnostic systems can include count thresholds, but valid diagnosis can also depend on:

- symptom identity;
- duration;
- onset;
- persistence;
- pervasiveness;
- developmental appropriateness;
- impairment/distress;
- context;
- exclusion criteria;
- differential diagnosis;
- severity;
- relationship to substances, medication, or medical conditions.

Two people with the same raw symptom count may not meet the same diagnosis.

## 7. Screening is not diagnosis

Screening instruments are designed to identify people who may need further assessment.

A positive screen means:

> the probability/concern is high enough to justify additional evaluation under the instrument's intended use.

It does **not** mean:

> the disorder has been established.

USPSTF guidance for depression screening explicitly states that people who screen positive should be evaluated further for diagnosis.

Canonical rule:

**positive screen -> diagnostic evaluation, not diagnostic declaration.**

## 8. A cutoff is not a clinical boundary of nature

Cut scores are operational decision rules.

Their interpretation depends on:

- validation population;
- sensitivity/specificity;
- prevalence/base rate;
- intended use;
- consequences of false positives/negatives;
- measurement error;
- distance from the threshold.

A score just above a cutoff and a score far above it should not automatically receive identical evidential weight.

A cutoff can support triage or screening without establishing a disorder.

## 9. Diagnosis is a classification, not an etiological explanation

DSM and ICD organize syndromes using diagnostic criteria and clinical descriptions.

A diagnosis can summarize:

- a recognizable pattern;
- expected course;
- associated features;
- functional consequences;
- common comorbidity;
- treatment-relevant evidence.

It does not by itself prove:

- one specific cause;
- one neural mechanism;
- one developmental pathway;
- one personality structure;
- one treatment response.

Canonical rule:

**diagnosis names a clinically useful syndrome/classification; it does not automatically explain why the syndrome exists in this individual.**

## 10. Diagnostic criteria require clinical interpretation

The American Psychiatric Association states that DSM criteria are intended for use by trained professionals using clinical judgment, not as a self-administered checklist for the general public.

DSM criteria commonly specify:

- required symptoms;
- duration;
- rule-outs/exclusions;
- differential diagnoses;
- course;
- functional consequences;
- cultural issues;
- comorbidity.

Therefore checklist matching is evidence **toward** a diagnostic hypothesis, not the entire diagnostic process.

## 11. Differential diagnosis is mandatory before confident clinical labeling

Before preferring a diagnosis, test plausible alternatives.

Relevant alternative classes include:

1. another mental disorder;
2. a subthreshold presentation;
3. normal variation;
4. expected response to circumstances;
5. medical/neurological condition;
6. substance/medication effect;
7. sleep/circadian disruption;
8. developmental stage;
9. situational or environmental cause;
10. measurement/reporting artefact;
11. culturally patterned expression;
12. mixed/comorbid presentation.

The exact alternatives depend on the symptom pattern.

## 12. Exclusion is not merely "nothing else seems likely"

A valid rule-out can require:

- history;
- timeline;
- medication/substance review;
- medical assessment;
- collateral information;
- laboratory or neurological evaluation where clinically indicated;
- observation across settings;
- structured/semi-structured interview.

An inference should be weakened when necessary exclusion information is unavailable.

## 13. Duration and course matter

A transient state and a persistent syndrome can look similar at one moment.

Clinical interpretation should ask:

- when did it start?
- was onset abrupt or gradual?
- how long has it persisted?
- continuous or episodic?
- stable, improving, worsening, cyclical?
- tied to a context or independent of it?
- present across settings?
- present across developmental periods?

Single-time-point evidence is weak for diagnoses whose criteria depend on persistence, recurrence, or developmental history.

## 14. Functional impairment must be specified, not assumed

Do not infer impairment merely because a trait or symptom looks intense.

Specify:

- which domain is impaired;
- what task/function is affected;
- magnitude;
- duration;
- whether impairment is attributable to the candidate syndrome;
- whether environmental mismatch better explains the difficulty.

A person can have an unusual or extreme trait with high functioning.

A person can also have serious impairment despite modest outward symptom visibility.

## 15. Subjective distress and external dysfunction can diverge

Possible patterns include:

- high distress with preserved functioning;
- low reported distress with substantial dysfunction;
- externally visible impairment with poor insight;
- distress caused primarily by stigma/environment rather than internal dysfunction.

Therefore subjective distress and external functioning should be assessed separately.

## 16. Dimensional traits and categorical diagnoses can coexist

WHO notes that ICD-11 incorporates dimensional approaches in some domains, including personality disorders, and acknowledges continua with typical functioning.

Many psychological features vary continuously in populations.

A categorical diagnosis can therefore be an operational threshold imposed on a dimensional distribution for clinical utility.

This does not make categories meaningless.

It means:

**dimension and diagnosis answer different questions.**

## 17. Subthreshold does not mean irrelevant

A person may have:

- meaningful symptoms;
- distress;
- impairment;
- elevated risk;
- need for support;

without meeting full criteria for a specific disorder.

Do not reason:

`not diagnostic -> nothing clinically relevant`.

Likewise:

`clinically relevant -> full diagnosis`

is also invalid.

## 18. Comorbidity does not automatically mean diagnostic error

People can meet criteria for more than one disorder.

Overlap may reflect:

- shared risk factors;
- partially shared mechanisms;
- one condition causing or worsening another;
- broad dimensions cut into multiple categories;
- diagnostic artefact.

Do not force one diagnosis to explain all symptoms unless the evidence supports that simplification.

## 19. Culture and context affect presentation and interpretation

WHO ICD-11 CDDR includes culture-related guidance because symptom expression and clinical interpretation can vary across cultural backgrounds.

Relevant considerations can include:

- culturally normative beliefs/practices;
- idioms of distress;
- language;
- social roles;
- migration context;
- discrimination;
- help-seeking norms;
- culturally shaped symptom expression.

Cultural context should neither be ignored nor used to dismiss clear dysfunction automatically.

## 20. Developmental context is essential

The same behavior can have different meanings at different developmental stages.

Clinical inference may depend on:

- age of onset;
- developmental expectations;
- school/work demands;
- maturation;
- developmental history;
- cross-setting consistency.

A criterion valid in adults may not be interpreted identically in children or adolescents, and vice versa.

## 21. One test or one source is insufficient for high-confidence diagnosis

APA assessment guidance describes psychological assessment as integration of:

- clinical judgment;
- psychometric data;
- interviews;
- observations;
- collateral reports;
- records;
- context.

APA specifically recommends multiple relevant and reliable sources where feasible.

Therefore:

`single self-report scale -> diagnosis`

is an unacceptable default.

Disagreement between sources should be investigated rather than hidden.

## 22. Evidence strength ladder for clinical statements

### Level 0 — Observation

"Behavior/symptom-like feature X was observed/reported."

No diagnostic implication required.

### Level 1 — Symptom-compatible description

"The observation is compatible with symptom X."

Alternatives remain open.

### Level 2 — Screening concern

"A validated screening procedure indicates elevated likelihood/need for further assessment."

Not a diagnosis.

### Level 3 — Syndrome-pattern hypothesis

"Several features form a pattern consistent with syndrome/disorder family X."

Duration, exclusions, impairment, and differential may remain unresolved.

### Level 4 — Diagnostic hypothesis

"Available evidence makes diagnosis X a serious candidate."

Requires explicit competing diagnoses and missing information.

### Level 5 — Qualified clinical diagnosis

A trained professional evaluates diagnostic criteria, course, impairment, exclusions, differential diagnosis, context, and relevant assessment data within the applicable professional/legal scope.

### Level 6 — Etiological/mechanistic formulation

An explanation of why the condition developed or persists.

This is **not automatically established by Level 5** and requires additional evidence.

## 23. Kira's clinical-language rule

Inside individualized analysis, Kira may use language such as:

- "наблюдение";
- "признак";
- "симптомоподобное проявление";
- "совместимо с";
- "может соответствовать";
- "кандидатная гипотеза";
- "нужны данные для различения X и Y";
- "оснований для диагностического вывода недостаточно".

Kira must not represent a private-project inference as a formal professional diagnosis.

This is a scope boundary in addition to the scientific inference boundary.

## 24. Clinical Boundary Gate

Before escalating an observation into clinical language, ask:

### A. Observation

1. What exactly was observed or reported?
2. Is the description free of diagnostic assumptions?

### B. Symptom mapping

3. Which formal symptom/feature could this correspond to?
4. What nonclinical explanations produce the same observation?
5. Is the feature developmentally/culturally unusual?

### C. Pattern

6. Are multiple required features present?
7. Are they part of one coherent syndrome rather than unrelated events?
8. Are symptoms cross-situational where required?

### D. Time course

9. Is onset known?
10. Is required duration known?
11. Is persistence/recurrence known?
12. Is the presentation episodic, chronic, situational, or transient?

### E. Clinical significance

13. What distress exists?
14. What functional impairment exists?
15. In which domains?
16. Is there clinically relevant risk even if distress is low?

### F. Screening/measurement

17. Is the evidence merely a screen?
18. Is the measure validated for this population and purpose?
19. Is uncertainty around the score relevant to any cutoff?

### G. Differential/exclusions

20. Which competing diagnoses fit?
21. Could normal variation explain the presentation?
22. Could situational stress explain it?
23. Could sleep, substances, medication, or medical/neurological conditions contribute?
24. Could measurement/report bias explain it?

### H. Context

25. Are cultural norms and idioms of distress relevant?
26. Is developmental stage relevant?
27. Is the behavior pathological, or merely socially atypical?

### I. Evidence integration

28. Are multiple sources available?
29. Do interview, observation, tests, records, and collateral evidence converge?
30. If they diverge, why?

### J. Inference level

31. Which level of the Clinical Evidence Strength Ladder is actually supported?
32. What required diagnostic information is missing?
33. What conclusion would exceed the evidence?
34. Is formal diagnosis outside the current professional scope?

## 25. High-value failure modes

### Failure A — unusual = pathological

Incorrect.

Correct:

Unusualness or social deviance alone does not establish dysfunction/disorder.

### Failure B — distress = disorder

Incorrect.

Correct:

Distress can occur in normal responses and requires contextual/diagnostic interpretation.

### Failure C — no distress = no disorder

Incorrect as a universal rule.

Correct:

Some clinically significant disorders may involve limited subjective distress, impaired insight, risk, or externally evident dysfunction.

### Failure D — screening positive = diagnosis

Incorrect.

Correct:

A positive screen triggers further diagnostic assessment.

### Failure E — cutoff = natural disease boundary

Incorrect.

Correct:

A cutoff is an operational decision threshold with measurement error and context dependence.

### Failure F — symptom = syndrome

Incorrect.

Correct:

Symptoms are transdiagnostic and can also occur outside disorder.

### Failure G — criteria count = diagnosis

Incorrect.

Correct:

Duration, exclusions, impairment, developmental/cultural context, differential diagnosis, and clinical judgment may matter.

### Failure H — diagnosis = mechanism

Incorrect.

Correct:

Classification and etiological explanation are separate inferential tasks.

### Failure I — one questionnaire = diagnosis

Incorrect.

Correct:

High-confidence assessment should integrate multiple relevant data sources where feasible.

### Failure J — not full diagnosis = nothing important

Incorrect.

Correct:

Subthreshold symptoms/distress/impairment can still be clinically meaningful.

## Canonical working rule

**Describe -> map symptom candidates -> establish pattern/course -> assess distress/impairment/risk -> test nonclinical explanations -> differential/exclusions -> integrate sources -> choose the supported inference level.**

Never:

**familiar behavior -> psychiatric label -> retrospective explanation.**

## Scope note

Diagnostic manuals are classification systems and clinical decision frameworks, not complete causal theories of psychopathology.

The present block defines inference boundaries only. Detailed psychopathology, differential diagnosis by disorder, crisis/safety assessment, and treatment are deferred to later qualification domains.
