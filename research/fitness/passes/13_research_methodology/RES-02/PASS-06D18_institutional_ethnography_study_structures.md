# 2. Canonical scientific target

Institutional ethnography asks:

**How are people's everyday activities and problems socially organized and coordinated by relations that extend beyond the local setting, often through texts, institutional rules, professional standards, administrative systems and dominant discourses?**

The central movement is:

everyday actuality
→ standpoint
→ problematic
→ work
→ texts / coordination
→ ruling relations
→ local-to-translocal map.

### Critical rule

Institutional ethnography is not:

> ethnography conducted in an institution.

Its scientific target is the **social organization and coordination of everyday work across local and extra-local relations**.

---

# 3. Dorothy Smith and the starting point

IE was developed by Dorothy E. Smith as an alternative sociology grounded in people's everyday worlds rather than abstracting them into externally imposed theoretical categories.

Canonical commitment:

> begin from people's actual activities and experience, then investigate how those activities are coordinated by social relations that may not be visible from the local standpoint.

### Project rule

IE should be routed only when the study actually moves from local experience/work outward into organizing relations.

A study of organizational culture alone is not automatically IE.

---

# 4. Standpoint

A **standpoint** is the social location from which the inquiry begins.

Examples:

- nurses;
- patients;
- families;
- exercise instructors;
- older exercisers;
- coaches;
- rehabilitation professionals.

The standpoint is not merely:

> participant demographic.

It gives the inquiry its epistemic starting position.

### Critical rule

Standpoint does not mean:

> the standpoint group possesses the whole institutional truth.

Their local knowledge provides the entry point for tracing relations that exceed what any one participant can directly see.

---

# 5. Standpoint vs sample

A study can include people outside the original standpoint group.

Example:

start from older exercisers' or instructors' standpoint
→ then interview certifying-body staff
→ collect curricula and policies
→ trace extra-local relations.

### Rule

The standpoint defines where inquiry begins.

It does not limit the entire sample to one group.

---

# 6. Problematic

A **problematic** is not simply a conventional topic.

It is the unresolved puzzle/tension that emerges from people's everyday actuality and directs investigation toward how that situation is socially organized.

It may initially appear as:

- frustration;
- contradiction;
- inefficiency;
- exclusion;
- mismatch;
- impossible work demand.

### Example

Official goal:
> make group exercise inclusive for older adults.

Observed actuality:
> practices and market segmentation create age exclusion.

That disjuncture can become the institutional-ethnographic problematic.

---

# 7. Disjuncture

A common IE entry signal is a **disjuncture** between:

- official/accountable version of what should happen;
- actual local work and experience.

Examples:

- policy says collaborative care;
- workflow produces fragmentation.

- guideline says individualized exercise;
- certification/training structure produces age-based standardized classes.

### Critical rule

Not every IE must begin with a dramatic contradiction, but a problematic usually directs attention to something not adequately explained from local experience alone.

---

# 8. Work

Smith uses a broad definition of **work**.

Work includes purposeful activity requiring:

- effort;
- intention;
- time;
- learned competence.

It can include:

- formal paid tasks;
- documentation;
- emotional work;
- coordination;
- self-management;
- family work;
- navigating services;
- informal troubleshooting.

### Fitness example

An older adult's "work" can include:

- choosing a class;
- interpreting instructor cues;
- modifying movement;
- navigating age-targeted programs;
- managing injury/fatigue;
- deciding whether a class feels socially appropriate.

---

# 9. Work knowledge

**Work knowledge** is what people know from actually doing the work.

It differs from official descriptions of the work.

### Example

Official exercise-instructor curriculum may state:

> adapt exercise to individual ability.

Instructor work knowledge may reveal:

- time constraints;
- class norms;
- certification rules;
- insurance concerns;
- age labels;
- participant expectations.

### Critical rule

IE values work knowledge because official accounts often omit the coordination work needed to make systems function.

---

# 10. Official account vs actuality

A central IE distinction is between:

## Accountable/official knowledge

What forms, metrics, policies or organizations say is happening.

## Actuality

What embodied people actually do to make the work happen.

### Project implication

Do not accept policy/protocol text as a direct description of practice.

Ask:

> How is the text activated, translated, ignored, adapted or worked around locally?

---

# 11. Local and extra-local

## Local

The site where embodied activity occurs.

Examples:

- gym floor;
- class;
- clinic;
- team meeting;
- rehabilitation session.

## Extra-local / translocal

Relations originating beyond the immediate site but coordinating local work.

Examples:

- professional certification bodies;
- national guidelines;
- funding rules;
- insurance requirements;
- accreditation;
- regulatory standards;
- software systems;
- corporate policies.

### Critical rule

The local setting is the starting point, not the full analytical boundary.

---

# 12. Ruling relations

Smith's **ruling relations** refer to coordinated administrative, managerial, professional and discursive relations that organize social life across sites.

They can include:

- bureaucracy;
- regulation;
- professional standards;
- funding;
- management;
- measurement systems;
- corporate structures;
- institutional discourses.

### Important boundary

Ruling relations are not simply:

> powerful people.

They are social relations through which people's activities become coordinated in standardized ways.

---

# 13. Ruling relations ≠ conspiracy

IE does not require evidence of deliberate centralized control.

Coordination can arise through:

- forms;
- protocols;
- performance indicators;
- software;
- certification rules;
- professional discourse.

Participants may reproduce ruling relations while having no knowledge of the full system.

### Rule

Avoid reducing IE to:

> institution intentionally forces people to behave this way.

The stronger claim is:

> distributed institutional relations coordinate activity in this way.

---

# 14. Texts are structurally central

Texts are unusually important in IE because they can coordinate activities across:

- people;
- sites;
- times.

Possible texts:

- policy;
- protocol;
- clinical guideline;
- training curriculum;
- checklist;
- chart;
- electronic form;
- standardized assessment;
- software interface;
- certification standard;
- website;
- media content.

### Critical rule

A document is not important merely because it exists.

The analytical question is:

> How is it activated in people's actual work?

---

# 15. Text activation

Texts do not coordinate work by themselves.

They become active when people:

- read;
- fill;
- quote;
- follow;
- enter data;
- reproduce;
- interpret;
- enforce;
- respond to them.

### Example

A fitness certification manual becomes institutionally active when:

- instructors train from it;
- exam items reproduce it;
- programming expectations reflect it;
- gyms hire based on that credential.

### Project rule

Textual analysis without tracing activation is weaker evidence for an IE ruling-relation claim.

---

# 16. Local vs extra-local texts

## Local texts

Generated/used in everyday work:

- attendance log;
- training sheet;
- local chart;
- session note.

## Extra-local texts

Produced/disseminated by broader authorities:

- professional guidelines;
- certification curriculum;
- regulatory standard;
- national physical-activity guidance.

### Analytical value

Researchers can trace how extra-local texts enter local work through local documents and routines.

---

# 17. Text-mediated coordination

Text-mediated coordination is one of IE's signature mechanisms.

A standardized text can coordinate people who:

- never meet;
- work in different places;
- work at different times.

### Example

One certification standard may shape instructor practices across hundreds of gyms.

### Critical rule

Text-mediated coordination is stronger than simply saying:

> participants mentioned a policy.

The study should trace how the policy/text becomes linked to actual activities.

---

# 18. Intertextual hierarchy

Institutional texts can be organized hierarchically.

Example:

national guideline
→ certification standard
→ instructor curriculum
→ local class plan
→ documentation/assessment.

Higher-level texts can shape lower-level texts and actions.

### Project extraction

Record:

- which texts reference which;
- which are mandatory/advisory;
- how lower-level texts reproduce higher-level categories.

---

# 19. Discourse in IE

IE may analyze dominant discourses embedded in texts.

Examples:

- efficiency;
- safety;
- productivity;
- successful ageing;
- fitness;
- age-as-decline;
- compliance.

### Boundary with CDA/FDA

IE does not stop at discourse construction.

It asks:

> How is that discourse materially organized and activated through people's work and texts?

### Rule

IE discourse analysis should remain linked to actual institutional coordination.

---

# 20. From local to translocal

Canonical IE movement:

1. begin in local actuality;
2. document what people do;
3. identify problem/disjuncture;
4. identify relevant texts/relations;
5. follow connections beyond the original site;
6. trace how remote actors/texts coordinate local work;
7. map the social organization.

### Critical rule

If analysis never leaves local cultural description, it may be ordinary ethnography rather than full IE.

---

# 21. Mapping

IE often produces conceptual/visual maps of:

- work processes;
- actors;
- texts;
- institutional pathways;
- coordinating relations.

### Important boundary

Mapping is not decorative visualization.

It is an analytic device for making otherwise invisible social organization visible.

### Rule

A process map alone is not IE unless grounded in standpoint/work/text/ruling-relations logic.

---

# 22. Sequential / iterative sampling

IE sampling is often not fixed at baseline.

Researchers may begin with standpoint informants, then follow clues toward:

- colleagues;
- administrators;
- policy makers;
- documents;
- organizations;
- remote coordinators.

### Canonical logic

standpoint data
→ clue to coordinating relation
→ sample next actor/text/site
→ trace further.

### Rule

This resembles purposive/theoretical redirection but should not be mislabeled grounded-theory theoretical sampling unless GT architecture is actually used.

---

# 23. Interviews in IE

IE interviews are commonly used to learn:

- what people actually do;
- how they know what to do;
- what happens next;
- which texts they use;
- who they interact with;
- where decisions come from.

### Critical rule

IE interview target is not primarily:

> opinions about the institution.

It is **work-process knowledge**.

---

# 24. Interviewing for actualities

Useful IE prompts are concrete:

- Walk me through what happened.
- What did you do next?
- Which form/system did you use?
- Who required that?
- What happens if you do not?
- Where does that instruction come from?
- Who receives this information?

### Rule

Abstract questions about “organizational culture” alone may miss the work coordination IE needs.

---

# 25. Observation in IE

Observation helps researchers see:

- actual workflow;
- interruptions;
- hidden tasks;
- workarounds;
- text activation;
- differences between official procedure and actual practice.

### Critical rule

Observation is useful but not universally mandatory.

IE can use interviews/texts when they adequately trace coordination.

---

# 26. Shadowing

Shadowing is especially useful for following:

- person;
- workflow;
- document;
- decision pathway.

### Example

Shadow an exercise instructor through:

- class preparation;
- participant screening;
- programming;
- modifications;
- documentation;
- interactions with management.

Then trace the institutional sources of those actions.

---

# 27. Documents/text analysis in IE

Documents are not analyzed only for thematic content.

Researchers ask:

- what category does this form create?;
- who must complete it?;
- what action does completion trigger?;
- what is left out?;
- what higher-level text created it?;
- how does it standardize local work?

### Critical rule

Document analysis in IE is relational and activity-linked.

---

# 28. Analysis begins during data collection

IE data collection and analysis are often simultaneous.

Early findings direct investigators toward:

- next participant;
- next document;
- next site;
- next organizational level.

### Rule

The analytic question is explanatory:

> How does this coordination actually happen?

not merely:

> What themes occur in interviews?

---

# 29. IE is descriptive and explanatory, but not causal-effect identification

IE can produce a strong social-organizational explanation:

> local action X is coordinated through policy/text/workflow Y.

This is more than a descriptive theme.

But it is not equivalent to:

- randomized causal effect;
- epidemiologic risk estimate;
- formal mediation.

### Project language

Prefer:

- organized;
- coordinated;
- mediated through institutional relations;
- traced to;
- linked through text activation.

Use causal-effect language only if an additional causal design supports it.

---

# 30. IE vs conventional ethnography

## Conventional ethnography

Primary target:
- culture;
- shared practices;
- meanings;
- social organization in context.

## Institutional ethnography

Primary target:
- how everyday local work is coordinated by extra-local/translocal institutional relations.

### Example

Conventional gym ethnography:
> What norms organize belonging in this gym?

IE:
> How do certification curricula, organizational standards and age/ability discourses organize instructors' and older adults' local group-exercise practices?

### Critical rule

Institutional setting ≠ institutional ethnography.

---

# 31. IE vs focused ethnography

Focused ethnography narrows:

- topic;
- field;
- setting;
- time.

It does not necessarily trace:

- ruling relations;
- text activation;
- local→translocal coordination.

### Rule

Short, targeted fieldwork inside a hospital/gym is not IE unless the IE architecture is present.

---

# 32. IE vs critical ethnography

Both can address:

- power;
- inequality;
- institutional structures.

## Critical ethnography

Often foregrounds:
- ideology;
- domination;
- emancipation;
- critical theory.

## IE

Begins from standpoint/work and **empirically traces coordination** through actual social relations and texts.

### Rule

IE is critical in implication but should not be reduced to generic “ethnography about power.”

---

# 33. IE vs case study

A case study asks:

> How/why does a bounded case function in context?

IE deliberately resists letting the local case boundary become the end of inquiry.

### Example

Case study:
> How does Gym A implement an older-adult class?

IE:
> Starting from Gym A's problematic, which extra-local certification, insurance and policy relations coordinate what happens there and elsewhere?

### Critical rule

A local site may be the entry point in IE but is not necessarily the analytic case boundary.

---

# 34. IE vs organizational ethnography

Organizational ethnography may study:

- culture;
- hierarchy;
- work;
- identity;
- practices;

inside one organization.

IE asks how local practices are coordinated **across/through institutional relations**, often outside the organization itself.

### Rule

“Organization” and “institution” are not interchangeable taxonomy labels here.

---

# 35. IE vs grounded theory

IE does not primarily seek:

- core category;
- abstract process theory;
- theoretical saturation.

Its endpoint is an **explication/map of social organization**.

### Rule

Iterative sampling in IE ≠ grounded-theory theoretical sampling automatically.

---

# 36. IE vs discourse analysis

IE may use discursive material, but discourse is tied to:

- texts;
- work;
- institutional coordination.

CDA/FDA may focus directly on:

- discourse;
- subject positions;
- ideology;
- power/knowledge.

### Rule

If the study does not trace discourse into actual work organization, discourse analysis may be the better parent route.

---

# 37. IE vs implementation research

IE can illuminate why implementation is organized as it is.

But implementation science may use:

- frameworks;
- quantitative measures;
- trials;
- mixed methods.

### Rule

IE is one possible methodology inside an implementation problem.

Implementation purpose ≠ IE design.

---

# 38. Quality architecture

Strong IE should demonstrate:

1. clear standpoint;
2. clear problematic;
3. grounding in actual everyday work;
4. work-process detail;
5. explicit attention to texts where relevant;
6. tracing beyond the local site;
7. empirical link between texts/relations and work;
8. ruling relations explicated rather than merely asserted;
9. reflexivity about researcher standpoint;
10. map/description allowing readers to follow the coordination chain;
11. claims calibrated to traced relations.

### Critical rule

Listing policies without showing their activation is not enough.

---

# 39. Adequacy / stopping logic

IE does not rely on a universal interview N or generic thematic saturation rule.

Adequacy depends on whether the inquiry has sufficiently traced:

- the problematic;
- work process;
- relevant actors;
- text pathways;
- ruling relations.

### Project rule

Use **mapping/explication adequacy**, not automatic “no new themes” saturation.

---

# 40. Reflexivity

Researcher social/professional location can influence:

- access;
- which work looks problematic;
- assumptions about official systems;
- interpretation of informant knowledge.

### IE-specific reflexive question

> Am I replacing participants' actual work knowledge with institutional or theoretical categories?

### Rule

Reflexivity should protect the standpoint-to-coordination tracing logic.

---

# 41. Theory boundary

Smith warned against beginning with abstract theory that replaces people's actual activities.

IE may engage theory later, but the inquiry should remain grounded in actual coordinated practices.

### “14th floor” problem

Theory can become so abstract that it speaks over local work rather than explicating it.

### Project rule

Do not classify theory-driven institutional critique as IE unless it actually begins from and traces people's everyday work.

---

# 42. Texts do not exhaust ruling relations

Texts are central but ruling relations can involve broader:

- managerial;
- economic;
- professional;
- discursive;
- technological;

coordination.

### Rule

Do not reduce IE to:

> document analysis.

Texts are often the material pathways through which wider relations become reproducible.

---

# 43. Technology / software as coordinating text-like infrastructure

Modern IE examples show electronic systems can organize work through:

- required fields;
- menus;
- scheduling;
- metrics;
- alerts;
- standardized categories.

### Fitness relevance

Potential examples:

- gym CRM;
- health-screening app;
- insurance eligibility system;
- exercise-prescription software;
- standardized readiness form.

### Rule

Digital systems should be analyzed through what people actually must do with them.

---

# 44. Power claims

IE can support claims about power when power is shown through:

- organization of work;
- mandatory procedures;
- text-mediated accountability;
- unequal ability to define categories;
- institutional priorities.

### Critical rule

Do not infer power only from job title.

Trace how power is enacted through coordination.

---

# 45. Fitness/exercise exemplar — older-adult group exercise

Harvey & Griffin used IE to study socially inclusive/exclusive physical cultures in:

- mainstream group exercise;
- older-adult group exercise.

Data included:

- textual analysis;
- interviews;
- field observations.

They traced local exercise practices to:

- guidelines;
- certification bodies;
- instructor training curricula;
- dominant age/ability discourses.

### Central finding

Practices intended to promote inclusivity could reproduce age-segregated/exclusive fitness markets.

### Why this is a canonical IE example

The analysis moves:

local class practice
→ instructor work
→ certification/training texts
→ broader age/ability discourse
→ institutional organization of the fitness market.

That is materially different from simply describing gym culture.

---

# 46. Fitness example — instructor work

Potential IE question:

> Why do instructors classify older participants into age-specific exercise formats even when functional ability varies widely?

Possible tracing path:

instructor decision
→ risk assessment
→ certification curriculum
→ insurance/safety policy
→ national older-adult exercise standards
→ organizational marketing categories.

### Rule

IE can reveal how a seemingly individual coaching choice is institutionally coordinated.

---

# 47. Fitness example — rehabilitation exercise

Potential IE question:

> Why does individualized exercise prescription become standardized in routine rehabilitation?

Possible standpoint:
- patient;
- physiotherapist.

Possible work:
- assessment;
- exercise selection;
- documentation;
- progression.

Possible translocal coordinators:
- reimbursement;
- clinical guideline;
- electronic record;
- performance metric;
- professional standard.

### Claim boundary

IE can trace how standardization is organized.

It cannot by itself estimate whether standardization causes worse clinical outcomes.

---

# 48. Fitness example — strength and conditioning

Potential IE question:

> Why do coaches document some athlete workload metrics but ignore others they consider practically important?

Possible tracing:
coach work knowledge
→ monitoring software
→ club reporting requirements
→ governing-body standards
→ performance-management system.

### Project value

This design is especially useful when practitioners say:

> “I know what I should do for this athlete, but the system requires something else.”

That tension is a classic IE entry point.

---

# 49. Claims IE can support

IE can strongly support claims about:

- social organization of everyday work;
- institutional coordination;
- text activation;
- local/extra-local relations;
- ruling relations;
- hidden/invisible work;
- contradictions between official and actual practice;
- mechanisms by which institutional categories organize action;
- institutional sources of local barriers.

---

# 50. Claims IE cannot establish by itself

IE alone does not establish:

- prevalence;
- incidence;
- average treatment effect;
- intervention efficacy;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism;
- causal mediation effect;
- population frequency of institutional practices.

### Important boundary

A traced institutional coordination pathway may be a strong explanatory social mechanism.

It is not automatically a quantitatively identified causal effect.

---

# 51. Population / transferability boundary

IE often aims to reveal relations that recur across multiple sites because standardized texts can coordinate many settings.

That can support broader **institutional transferability**.

But one IE study does not automatically show:

> every gym, clinic or professional acts this way.

### Rule

Broader claim strength depends on:

- how translocal the coordinating text/relation is;
- how many sites are traced;
- whether the same relation is empirically activated elsewhere.
