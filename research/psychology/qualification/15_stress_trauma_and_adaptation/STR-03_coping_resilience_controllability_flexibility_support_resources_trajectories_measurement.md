# STR-03 — Coping and resilience architecture: controllability, flexibility, support, resources, trajectories, and measurement validity

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public stress/coping/resilience science and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

STR-01 separated stressor exposure, appraisal, stress response, recovery, coping, resilience, and allostatic burden. STR-02 separated HPA/ANS/immune physiology, reactivity, recovery, habituation, and biomarker validity.

STR-03 addresses the remaining process-level dependency before integration:

**How should coping and resilience be inferred when strategy value depends on controllability, goals, resources, timing, social context, adversity exposure, and measurement method?**

High-risk errors to prevent:
- "problem-focused coping is always best";
- "emotion-focused coping is passive";
- "avoidance is always maladaptive";
- "acceptance means resignation";
- "support seeking = support received = support perceived";
- "more coping strategies = more flexibility";
- "switching strategies = good coping";
- "one coping questionnaire reveals what a person actually did in a specific stressor";
- "good mental health = resilience";
- "resilience = one stable personality trait";
- "resilience scale score = observed resilient outcome";
- "social support is a proven resilience mechanism because it correlates with adjustment";
- "positive appraisal means unrealistic optimism";
- "recovery after a daily hassle = resilience after major adversity";
- "a trajectory class is a natural psychological type";
- "protective factor = causal mechanism".

## Core rule

**Coping is a context-sensitive process for managing stressor demands, resources, meanings, or responses; resilience is defined only relative to adversity and an outcome over time. Strategy labels, coping flexibility scores, protective factors, and resilience questionnaires are not self-interpreting mechanisms.**

The valid question is not "Which coping style is best?" but:

**What stressor, what controllability, what goal, what resource constraints, what strategy implementation, what timescale, and what outcome?**

## 1. Coping is a transaction, not a fixed style

Coping can change across:
- stressors;
- phases of the same stressor;
- perceived controllability;
- available resources;
- social context;
- emotional intensity;
- new information;
- prior success/failure.

A stable self-report preference can be useful descriptively but does not prove invariant behavior.

The same person may use problem solving in one context, acceptance in another, and temporary disengagement in a third.

## 2. Coping target must be specified

A coping act can target:
1. the external stressor;
2. the probability or magnitude of harm;
3. practical resources;
4. emotional experience;
5. attention;
6. interpretation/meaning;
7. social support;
8. behavioral exposure/avoidance;
9. recovery/rest;
10. longer-term adaptation.

Two strategies with the same label can have different targets.

## 3. Coping versus emotion regulation

The 2024 scoping review found substantial conceptual and measurement overlap between coping and emotion regulation.

Both frameworks commonly measure:
- reappraisal/reinterpretation;
- active coping/problem solving;
- acceptance;
- avoidance;
- suppression.

Useful working boundary:
- **emotion regulation** primarily targets emotion generation/trajectory;
- **coping** may target the external stressor, resources, practical demands, meaning, or emotional response.

The boundary is functional rather than absolute.

Do not infer two separate mechanisms merely because instruments come from different theoretical traditions.

## 4. Problem-focused coping

Problem-focused coping attempts to change:
- the stressor;
- its consequences;
- available options;
- practical constraints.

Examples include:
- information gathering;
- planning;
- negotiation;
- direct action;
- resource acquisition.

It is most plausible when meaningful control exists.

But action can be costly, unsafe, impossible, or counterproductive.

**Problem-focused ≠ universally adaptive.**

## 5. Emotion-focused coping

Emotion-focused coping aims primarily to alter emotional response or its consequences.

It can include:
- acceptance;
- reappraisal;
- distraction;
- emotional expression;
- relaxation;
- support seeking;
- self-soothing.

This category includes both potentially helpful and unhelpful tactics.

Therefore "emotion-focused" is too broad to carry an adaptive sign.

## 6. Meaning-focused coping

Meaning-focused coping changes how a stressor is understood in relation to:
- values;
- identity;
- purpose;
- broader goals;
- long-term significance.

It may be especially relevant when the stressor cannot be removed.

Meaning-making does not require claiming that adversity was beneficial.

Do not infer growth, purpose, or positive transformation merely because someone endured a stressor.

## 7. Acceptance is not resignation

Acceptance can involve acknowledging:
- the reality of current conditions;
- internal experience;
- limits of control.

It can coexist with:
- active problem solving;
- boundary setting;
- support seeking;
- strategic withdrawal;
- long-term change.

Resignation implies giving up relevant agency; acceptance does not.

AFF-02's distinction remains canonical.

## 8. Avoidance has short- and long-horizon functions

Avoidance can:
- protect from genuine threat;
- reduce immediate overload;
- prevent escalation;
- preserve resources.

It can also:
- postpone solvable problems;
- maintain feared predictions;
- restrict behavior;
- prevent corrective learning.

Classic and modern coping evidence therefore supports boundary conditions rather than a universal sign.

The evaluation must specify:
- what is avoided;
- why;
- for how long;
- at what cost;
- with what later consequence.

## 9. Disengagement is not one construct

Distinguish:
- disengaging from a stressor;
- disengaging from an ineffective coping tactic;
- disengaging from a goal;
- behavioral withdrawal;
- cognitive avoidance;
- temporary recovery/rest.

Stopping one tactic can be adaptive flexibility rather than avoidance of the entire problem.

Cross-reference MOT-03: strategy abandonment and higher-order goal abandonment are different.

## 10. Controllability is multidimensional

Control can refer to:
- preventing the stressor;
- terminating it;
- changing intensity;
- choosing timing;
- choosing one's response;
- accessing resources;
- predicting consequences.

Perceived control can differ from objective control.

A binary "controllable/uncontrollable" label can hide these dimensions.

## 11. Strategy–situation fit

A major coping-flexibility hypothesis is that coping should match situational demands.

A common simplified prediction:
- more direct/problem-focused coping when change is feasible;
- more accommodation/emotion-focused strategies when the external situation is relatively uncontrollable.

This is a useful hypothesis, **not a universal decision rule**.

A 2024/2025 daily-diary study supported parts of strategy–controllability fit but also found that focusing on what could currently be controlled could outperform a simple fit rule.

## 12. Coping flexibility is not repertoire breadth

"Coping flexibility" has been operationalized as:
- broad strategy repertoire;
- variability across situations;
- balanced strategy profile;
- ability to abandon ineffective strategies;
- strategy–situation fit;
- perceived flexibility.

These are not equivalent constructs.

A 2026 performance-based study found situation–strategy fit predicted more favorable outcomes above existing measures, whereas broader repertoire was associated with poorer outcomes in that sample.

Therefore:

**more strategies ≠ more flexibility**.

## 13. Coping flexibility meta-analytic evidence

A 2025 three-level meta-analysis during COVID-19 synthesized:
- 89 studies;
- 33 countries;
- N = 102,304;
- 243 effect sizes.

Overall association between coping flexibility and psychological adjustment:
- r ≈ .28;
- 95% CI [.25, .31].

There was significant between-study heterogeneity, and associations varied with outcome valence and contextual pandemic severity.

Interpretation:
- coping flexibility is meaningfully associated with adjustment;
- the construct is context-sensitive;
- the association is not proof that flexibility causally produces adjustment.

## 14. Switching is not flexibility by itself

Frequent switching can reflect:
- good monitoring;
- failed strategies;
- uncertainty;
- panic;
- changing context;
- exploratory adaptation.

To infer flexibility require evidence that switching improved fit or outcome.

A person who persistently uses one well-matched strategy may be more flexible in functional terms than someone who changes tactics repeatedly without improvement.

## 15. Coping monitoring

Effective coping can involve:
1. identify demands;
2. assess controllability/resources;
3. select a tactic;
4. implement;
5. monitor outcome;
6. persist, modify, or abandon;
7. update appraisal.

This resembles AFF-02's regulation cycle but the target can include external demands/resources, not only emotion.

Failure at any stage should not be compressed into "poor coping."

## 16. Coping efficacy versus coping frequency

Frequent use can mean:
- strategy is effective;
- stressors are frequent;
- strategy is habitual;
- alternatives are unavailable;
- the person is repeatedly failing and trying again.

Therefore:
**frequency ≠ skill ≠ effectiveness**.

Outcome should be measured after accounting for stressor severity and prior state.

## 17. Coping in daily life: current measurement evidence

A 2026 systematic review of daily diary/ESM coping studies included 55 studies.

Key features:
- ~80% used end-of-day diaries;
- typical duration was 1–3 weeks;
- higher-frequency ESM was less common;
- many studies used abbreviated or single-item coping measures;
- multilevel modeling was common;
- concurrent analyses were more common than temporally ordered models.

This limits causal claims about strategy → later outcome.

## 18. End-of-day coping reports are not event-level process measures

End-of-day reports can combine:
- several stressors;
- several strategies;
- retrospective reconstruction;
- peak/salient moments.

For strategy–situation fit, event-linked or sufficiently frequent measurements are preferable.

Diary designs can be highly informative while still missing rapid strategy changes.

## 19. Within-person and between-person coping effects

Between-person:
"People who report more support seeking tend to have better outcomes."

Within-person:
"On occasions when this person seeks more support than usual, later outcome changes."

These are different questions.

A trait questionnaire cannot substitute for within-person coping process evidence.

## 20. Coping and reverse causality

High distress can:
- trigger rumination;
- increase avoidance;
- increase help seeking;
- reduce problem solving;
- lead to more strategies.

Thus a coping strategy can correlate with poor outcome because difficult situations evoke both.

Control for:
- pre-strategy distress;
- stressor severity;
- controllability;
- prior coping;
- resource availability.

## 21. Social support as a resource

Social support can include:
- emotional support;
- informational support;
- instrumental/practical support;
- companionship;
- validation.

Sources can include:
- family;
- friends;
- peers;
- coworkers;
- community/institutions.

"Support" must specify type and source.

## 22. Perceived versus received support

**Perceived support**: belief that support is available and responsive.

**Received support**: support actually provided.

They are related but not interchangeable.

A 2025 second-order synthesis of 60 meta-analyses (>2,700 studies; >2.1 million participants) found:
- overall support–adjustment association r ≈ .24;
- perceived support generally showed stronger associations than received support;
- effects varied substantially across meta-analyses;
- covariate-adjusted estimates were smaller.

Thus social support is robustly associated with adjustment without being a single causal mechanism.

## 23. Support can help through multiple pathways

Possible pathways:
- practical resource gain;
- threat reappraisal;
- emotional validation;
- information;
- increased control;
- belonging;
- reduced isolation;
- behavior change.

A correlation between support and resilience cannot identify which pathway operated.

SOC/REL will deepen interpersonal mechanisms.

## 24. Support mismatch

Support can be unhelpful when:
- unwanted;
- controlling;
- invalidating;
- poorly timed;
- mismatched to the problem;
- burdensome or dependency-inducing;
- exposing private information.

Therefore:
**more received support ≠ always better**.

Support quality and recipient goals matter.

## 25. Resource models

Stress theories often treat adaptation as dependent on access to resources.

Resources can include:
- time;
- money/material safety;
- health/energy;
- skills;
- information;
- social support;
- control;
- institutional access;
- stable environment.

Resource models are useful because coping capacity is not purely intrapsychic.

Do not moralize low coping capacity when constraints are structural or material.

## 26. Conservation-of-resources boundary

Conservation-of-resources models emphasize:
- resource loss;
- threat of loss;
- resource gain;
- loss/gain spirals.

This can explain why repeated stressors become harder to manage as resources erode.

But "resource" must be operationalized rather than defined so broadly that every desirable condition becomes a resource.

COR is a framework, not proof that all stress processes reduce to resource accounting.

## 27. Resource availability versus resource utilization

Having a potential resource does not mean:
- it is accessible;
- it is recognized;
- it is acceptable;
- it can be mobilized in time.

Support networks, skills, and money can remain functionally unavailable.

Measure effective access, not only nominal possession.

## 28. Resilience requires adversity

Resilience cannot be inferred from good functioning alone.

At minimum specify:
- adversity/stressor exposure;
- system/person/population;
- outcome;
- timeframe.

The 2026 SSRO scaffold formalizes:
**Stressor → System → Resilience resources/processes → Outcomes**.

This is a useful organizing framework, not a unique causal theory.

## 29. Resilience: trait, outcome, or process

The literature uses resilience as:
- trait/capacity;
- favorable outcome despite adversity;
- dynamic process.

A 2025 systematic review of 193 longitudinal studies (805,660 participants) found only 32% explicitly defined resilience as:
- trait (6%);
- outcome (19%);
- process (8%).

The remaining majority did not clearly state which conceptualization they used.

Therefore a "resilience result" is uninterpretable without the operational definition.

## 30. Process-oriented resilience

For longitudinal research, a useful definition is:

**dynamic adaptation in which adversity is the antecedent, functioning/health is the outcome, and internal/external protective processes can modify the adversity–outcome relation.**

This distinguishes:
- exposure;
- outcome;
- predictors/resources;
- mechanisms.

Do not put all four into one questionnaire score and call it resilience without clarification.

## 31. Resilience factor versus resilience mechanism

**Factor/resource**: associated with better-than-expected outcome under adversity.

**Mechanism**: proximal process through which that factor changes outcome.

Example:
- social support may be a factor;
- improved controllability appraisal could be one mechanism;
- practical assistance could be another.

A predictor is not automatically a mechanism.

## 32. Promotive versus protective effects

A **promotive factor** predicts better outcomes broadly, including at low adversity.

A **protective factor** specifically buffers/modifies the effect of adversity.

These are statistically and conceptually distinct.

A variable associated with mental health in the whole sample is not automatically a resilience-specific factor.

## 33. Differential susceptibility is different again

Some factors may amplify sensitivity to both adverse and supportive environments rather than simply protect against adversity.

The 2025 longitudinal resilience review found differential-susceptibility models were uncommon.

Do not label every adversity × factor interaction "resilience."

## 34. Resilience outcome must be specified

Possible outcomes:
- mental health symptoms;
- positive mental health;
- occupational functioning;
- relationships;
- physical health;
- cognitive functioning.

A person can show resilient functioning in one domain and impairment in another.

**Resilience is outcome/domain-specific unless cross-domain evidence is shown.**

## 35. Resilience is time-specific

An apparently resilient trajectory can later deteriorate.
An initially impaired trajectory can recover.

Follow-up length therefore changes the classification.

Do not convert a short-term response into a permanent resilience trait.

## 36. Background stressor exposure

A major methodological problem is under-measuring other stressors occurring:
- before the focal event;
- during follow-up.

A 2024 study demonstrated that background exposure can change trajectory classification and estimates of resilience factors.

A 2026 methodological critique reinforced that coarse exposure measurement can produce false resilience classification.

Therefore:
**focal event alone ≠ total adversity exposure**.

## 37. Trajectory classes are model-dependent

Latent class/growth-mixture approaches can identify patterns such as:
- stable good functioning;
- transient disturbance + recovery;
- delayed impairment;
- chronic difficulty.

But classes depend on:
- outcome chosen;
- number/timing of assessments;
- model specification;
- class-number criteria;
- background exposure;
- sample composition.

Do not treat latent classes as natural immutable psychological types.

## 38. "Stable low symptoms" can hide exposure differences

Two people with similarly low symptoms can have very different adversity loads.

One may show better-than-expected functioning under high exposure.
Another may have experienced little adversity.

Exposure-qualified outcome is more informative than symptoms alone.

## 39. Residualized resilience

One approach models expected mental-health burden as a function of stressor exposure and treats residual outcome as "stressor reactivity" or better-/worse-than-expected functioning.

Advantages:
- explicitly incorporates exposure.

Limitations:
- depends on model form;
- sample normative relation;
- exposure measurement;
- outcome reliability;
- residual non-independence/statistical artifacts.

Residual scores are operational tools, not direct biological resilience.

## 40. Positive appraisal style (PAS)

Recent resilience theory proposes that realistic-to-mildly-positive appraisal of stressors can be a proximal resilience factor.

A 2025 Nature Communications study found PAS:
- prospectively predicted stress-resilient outcomes in two observational samples (N=132 and N=1,034);
- was modifiable in exploratory outcomes from a randomized intervention sample (N=232);
- statistically mediated intervention-related resilience improvements.

This is unusually informative evidence, but it does not prove all resilience reduces to appraisal.

## 41. Positive appraisal is not denial

The PASTOR framework explicitly distinguishes a useful positive appraisal style from:
- catastrophizing/pessimism;
- helplessness;
- extreme threat trivialization;
- unrealistic overconfidence.

Thus:
**positive appraisal ≠ "everything is fine"**.

Accurate threat detection remains adaptive when danger is real.

## 42. Appraisal as factor versus mechanism

If positive appraisal predicts outcome, several causal models remain possible:
- appraisal changes stress responses;
- social/resource factors alter appraisal;
- prior mental health shapes appraisal;
- reciprocal effects occur.

Even mediation in observational data does not fully identify causality.

Intervention mediation strengthens but does not universalize the mechanism.

## 43. Daily resilience

A 2025 systematic review of 36 ESM studies found two common operationalizations:
1. self-reported ability to recover;
2. observed affective recovery after daily stressors.

These are not equivalent.

Daily resilience may illuminate microprocesses but should not automatically be generalized to adaptation after major adversity.

## 44. Daily recovery is not global resilience

Fast affective recovery after a minor daily stressor can coexist with poor adaptation to prolonged adversity.

Conversely, slow recovery from a major event can be normative.

Scale stressor magnitude, timescale, and outcome before transferring inference.

## 45. Resilience questionnaires often measure mixed content

A 2026 review evaluated items from **83 resilience scales**.

Across scales:
- ~31.8% of items reflected process-related content;
- ~52.0% reflected five-factor-model trait content;
- ~16.3% reflected environmental factors.

Citation-weighted results differed substantially.

Conclusion:
**same label "resilience scale" can represent different constructs.**

## 46. A resilience scale score is not an observed resilient outcome

A questionnaire can estimate:
- self-perceived coping capacity;
- optimism;
- persistence;
- flexibility;
- social/environmental resources.

It may predict later outcomes, but it does not establish that adversity was encountered or successfully adapted to.

Exposure-qualified longitudinal outcome remains a different construct.

## 47. Reliability is not resilience validity

A scale can have:
- high internal consistency;
- strong factor fit;
- test–retest reliability;

while still measuring a construct that is partly:
- personality;
- optimism;
- self-efficacy;
- social resources.

The psychometric question is:
**reliable measure of what?**

## 48. Social support is a resilience factor, not the whole process

The 2025 second-order meta-analysis found robust support–adjustment associations across many outcomes and populations.

But:
- perceived support was stronger than received support;
- cross-sectional effects were stronger;
- covariate-adjusted effects were smaller;
- effect sizes varied substantially.

Therefore social support is a credible resilience-relevant factor while causal pathways and boundary conditions remain open.

## 49. Multi-level resilience

Potential resilience-relevant resources operate at:
- individual;
- family;
- peer/social network;
- community;
- institutional;
- societal levels.

The 2025 longitudinal systematic review found protective/promotive factors across individual, family, and community levels.

Do not make resilience an exclusively individual responsibility.

## 50. Resilience can carry domain tradeoffs

Maintaining work performance may coexist with:
- poor sleep;
- interpersonal strain;
- physical cost;
- delayed distress.

A favorable outcome in one domain does not establish whole-person adaptation.

Multi-outcome assessment can reveal hidden costs.

## 51. "Growth" is not required for resilience

Resilience can mean maintaining or recovering function without:
- becoming stronger;
- finding meaning;
- reporting post-traumatic growth.

Growth claims require their own validated outcomes and cannot be inferred from survival or symptom recovery.

## 52. Coping success versus resilience

Effective coping during one episode can contribute to resilience but is not equivalent to resilience.

Resilience is assessed over adversity and time.

A single coping success is a local process outcome.

## 53. Physiological response size versus resilience

STR-02 established:
- strong reactivity can be adaptive;
- blunting can appear in psychopathology;
- habituation is system-specific and not equivalent to resilience.

Therefore resilience cannot be inferred from:
- low cortisol;
- high HRV;
- rapid habituation;
- low subjective distress

without exposure/outcome context.

## 54. Measurement triangulation

A strong coping/resilience design can combine:
- stressor exposure;
- appraisal/control;
- actual coping behavior;
- resource/support measures;
- repeated mental-health/function outcomes;
- ecological assessments;
- biological measures where justified.

Convergence improves inference but no channel is mandatory for all questions.

## 55. Coping and Resilience Inference Gate — 60 questions

### A. Stressor and control
1. What exact stressor/adversity is present?
2. Is total/background exposure measured?
3. Is the stressor ongoing or terminated?
4. What aspects are objectively controllable?
5. What aspects are perceived controllable?
6. Are probability/predictability and control separated?
7. Are resources sufficient for available actions?
8. Could structural constraints limit coping?

### B. Coping target and strategy
9. Is coping targeting the stressor, emotion, meaning, resources, or recovery?
10. Is problem-focused coping feasible?
11. Is emotion-focused coping specified beyond the broad label?
12. Is meaning-focused coping operationalized?
13. Is acceptance distinguished from resignation?
14. Is avoidance target and duration specified?
15. Is behavioral disengagement separated from abandoning one tactic?
16. Is social support strategy type specified?
17. Was strategy implementation actually measured?
18. Is frequency separated from effectiveness?

### C. Flexibility and fit
19. What definition of coping flexibility is used?
20. Is repertoire breadth separated from fit?
21. Is cross-situational variability separated from switching?
22. Is abandoning an ineffective strategy measured?
23. Is strategy–situation fit directly tested?
24. Is controllability measured rather than assumed?
25. Does switching improve outcome or merely reflect failure?
26. Are within-person and between-person flexibility separated?
27. Are concurrent strategies/polycoping allowed?
28. Is coping monitored over time?

### D. Social/resources
29. Is perceived support separated from received support?
30. What source of support is measured?
31. What type of support is provided?
32. Is support wanted and matched to need?
33. Are material/institutional resources measured?
34. Is resource availability separated from resource accessibility?
35. Could resource loss explain declining coping capacity?
36. Is a support association being treated as a unique mechanism?

### E. Resilience definition
37. Is resilience defined as trait/capacity, outcome, or process?
38. Is adversity necessary to the operationalization?
39. What system/person/population is evaluated?
40. What exact outcome is resilient?
41. Over what time horizon?
42. Is resilience domain-specific?
43. Is recovery being equated with all resilience?
44. Is daily recovery being generalized to major adversity?
45. Is stable functioning being interpreted without exposure measurement?

### F. Factors, mechanisms, trajectories
46. Is a promotive factor distinguished from a protective factor?
47. Is a factor distinguished from a mechanism?
48. Is mediation being interpreted causally without sufficient design?
49. Are trajectory classes model-dependent?
50. Is background stressor exposure included?
51. Is latent class uncertainty considered?
52. Could a short follow-up misclassify adaptation?
53. Are growth and resilience separated?
54. Are tradeoffs across outcome domains measured?

### G. Psychometrics and inference
55. What content does the resilience/coping scale actually measure?
56. Is internal consistency being confused with construct validity?
57. Does a questionnaire score predict exposure-qualified outcome?
58. Is group-level support/flexibility evidence being assigned to an individual?
59. What viable rival coping/resilience model remains?
60. What is the narrowest conclusion justified by stressor, process, outcome, and timescale?

## 56. Failure modes

A. Problem-focused coping → always adaptive: **invalid**.

B. Emotion-focused coping → passive/weak: **invalid category stereotype**.

C. Acceptance → resignation: **invalid**.

D. Avoidance → always maladaptive: **invalid without target/time/context**.

E. More strategies → more flexibility: **invalid**.

F. Switching → good coping: **invalid without fit/outcome**.

G. Coping frequency → coping skill: **invalid**.

H. Perceived support = received support: **invalid**.

I. More received support → better outcome: **not universal**.

J. Support correlation → resilience mechanism: **causal overreach**.

K. Good mental health → resilience without adversity: **invalid**.

L. Resilience questionnaire → observed resilient adaptation: **invalid construct substitution**.

M. Protective factor → mechanism: **invalid**.

N. Positive appraisal → denial/naive optimism: **invalid**.

O. Trajectory class → natural person type: **invalid model reification**.

P. Daily recovery → global resilience: **invalid transport**.

Q. Low physiological reactivity → resilience: **invalid**.

R. High reliability → valid resilience construct: **invalid psychometric inference**.

## 57. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- coping and emotion regulation substantially overlap but differ in target breadth;
- controllability/resources matter to coping interpretation;
- coping frequency does not identify coping effectiveness;
- flexibility is not reducible to repertoire size;
- resilience requires adversity/exposure and an outcome;
- trait/process/outcome resilience operationalizations are distinct;
- resilience factors are not automatically mechanisms;
- social support is robustly associated with adjustment but not a unique causal pathway;
- questionnaires and exposure-qualified longitudinal resilience are distinct constructs.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- strategy–situation fit can predict better adjustment;
- coping flexibility is positively associated with psychological adjustment;
- social support can buffer or facilitate adaptation;
- positive appraisal style may be a proximal, modifiable resilience factor;
- daily recovery measures can illuminate resilience microprocesses.

### Not established as universal
- one optimal coping strategy;
- one flexibility metric;
- one resilience trajectory taxonomy;
- one resilience scale measuring a common latent trait;
- positive appraisal as the sole resilience mechanism;
- low stress physiology as a resilience marker;
- more support/resources always producing better outcomes.

## Canonical operating rule

**Define stressor and total exposure -> identify controllability, goals, and available resources -> specify coping target/tactic -> measure implementation and strategy–situation fit -> track outcome and update/switching over time -> distinguish support/resource availability from mechanisms -> define resilience as trait/process/outcome explicitly -> require adversity-qualified longitudinal outcome -> separate factors from mechanisms and trajectory classes from natural types -> state only the narrowest supported inference.**

Never:
**strategy label = adaptiveness; repertoire = flexibility; support = mechanism; good outcome = resilience without adversity; questionnaire = resilience outcome; trajectory = person type; low physiology = resilience.**
