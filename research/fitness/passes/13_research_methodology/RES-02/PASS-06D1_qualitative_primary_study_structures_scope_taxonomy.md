# 2. Core principle

## “Qualitative” is not one atomic study design

Qualitative research is a broad family of inquiry used to understand:

- meanings;
- lived experience;
- perceptions;
- social/cultural practices;
- processes;
- context;
- interpretations;
- theory development;
- bounded cases.

The label “qualitative” therefore does not replace the need to identify:

1. scientific purpose/question;
2. qualitative approach/methodology;
3. paradigm/theoretical lens where relevant;
4. case/context;
5. sampling/selection logic;
6. data source and collection method;
7. researcher role/reflexivity;
8. analytic method;
9. data-adequacy/stopping logic;
10. relation to any parent trial/cohort/program;
11. final claim.

### Canonical rule

Never classify a paper only as:

> “qualitative interview study”

when the available report allows a more precise description.

Preferred representation:

> purpose + qualitative approach + sampling + data collection + analysis + context/reflexivity + claim.

# 3. Scientific purpose is separate from method

A scientific question can be qualitative even when several different collection methods could answer it.

Examples:

## Experience / meaning

Question:

> What is it like to experience phenomenon X, and what meaning does it have for participants?

Possible approach:

- phenomenology;
- IPA / hermeneutic phenomenology where methodologically justified.

Possible collection:

- in-depth interviews;
- diaries;
- observations;
- participant-produced material.

## Culture / shared practice

Question:

> How does a group organize, understand and enact practice X in its social/cultural context?

Possible approach:

- ethnography / focused ethnography.

Possible collection:

- participant observation;
- field notes;
- interviews;
- documents.

## Process / theory generation

Question:

> What process explains how people move through X, and how are categories related?

Possible approach:

- grounded theory.

Possible collection:

- iterative interviews;
- observations;
- documents.

## Low-inference description

Question:

> What barriers, facilitators, views or events do participants report?

Possible approach:

- qualitative description.

Possible collection:

- interviews;
- focus groups;
- documents.

## Bounded system

Question:

> How does this particular program, team, gym, clinic or community function in its real-world context?

Possible approach:

- case study.

Possible collection:

- multiple sources such as interviews, observation, documents and records.

### Rule

> research question/purpose ≠ data-collection technique.

---

# 4. Interviews / focus groups / observation are data-collection methods, not study designs

SRQR explicitly separates:

- qualitative approach/research paradigm;
- sampling strategy;
- data collection methods;
- data analysis.

JARS-Qual likewise separates researcher description, participant/data-source selection, data collection and analysis.

Therefore:

## Interview

Can occur inside:

- phenomenology;
- grounded theory;
- qualitative description;
- case study;
- ethnography;
- narrative inquiry;
- process evaluation;
- content-validity research;
- mixed-methods studies.

## Focus group

Can be used for:

- qualitative description;
- cultural/social meaning;
- intervention development;
- content validity;
- implementation;
- mixed methods.

“Focus-group study” describes collection mode unless the scientific/methodological architecture is otherwise specified.

## Observation

Can be:

- participant;
- non-participant;
- structured or less structured;
- episodic or prolonged.

Observation is especially important in ethnographic work but does not by itself make a study ethnographic.

### Canonical rule

> collection mode must not be promoted to the design axis.

# 5. Major qualitative design/methodology families at taxonomy level

This is a project taxonomy for routing RES-02 papers, not a claim that the field has one universally accepted list.

## 5.1 Phenomenology

Primary target:

> lived experience and the meaning/structure of experience.

Typical distinguishing features:

- participants selected because they have direct experience of the phenomenon;
- analysis aims beyond a simple inventory of opinions;
- interpretation is tied to a phenomenological tradition or explicit lived-experience framework.

Important boundary:

> semi-structured interviews + themes ≠ automatically phenomenology.

The phenomenological claim requires methodological alignment between question, philosophical/methodological approach and analysis.

Fitness examples:

- lived experience of trail running;
- lived experience of exercise with depression;
- experience of exercising with persistent pain.

## 5.2 Grounded theory

Primary target:

> develop an explanatory theory/model of a social or behavioral process grounded in data.

Characteristic architecture:

- iterative data collection and analysis;
- category development;
- constant comparison in many variants;
- theoretical sampling as categories develop;
- theory/category saturation or another explicitly justified adequacy criterion depending school.

Important boundary:

> “grounded-theory coding” applied to a fixed interview set is not sufficient by itself to establish a full grounded-theory design.

Exact coding procedures differ across grounded-theory traditions; the project should not require one school’s vocabulary universally.

Fitness examples:

- process of maintaining long-term physical activity;
- exercise-adherence process;
- decision pathway for participation/non-participation.

## 5.3 Ethnography / focused ethnography

Primary target:

> shared meanings, practices, values, behaviors and worldview of a social/cultural group in context.

Typical architecture:

- attention to natural setting and social context;
- field engagement;
- observation often central;
- interviews/documents can be combined with field notes.

Focused ethnography can be narrower and shorter than classic ethnography and may rely more heavily on interviews while preserving a cultural/contextual target.

Important boundary:

> interviewing members of one gym does not automatically make the study ethnographic.

Fitness example:

- shared exercise/self-care beliefs and practices of a rural older-adult community studied through interviews plus participant observation.

## 5.4 Qualitative description

Sandelowski’s defining purpose:

> comprehensive, relatively low-inference summary of events/experiences in everyday terms.

Use when the scientific goal is direct description rather than:

- essence of lived experience;
- cultural interpretation;
- generation of formal/substantive theory.

It can combine pragmatic sampling, collection and analytic techniques.

Important boundary:

> qualitative description is a legitimate methodology, not a failed phenomenology or “generic” study merely because it stays close to participants’ language.

Fitness examples:

- reported barriers/facilitators to exercise adherence;
- preferences for home-training support;
- participant views on recovery-program usability.

## 5.5 Case study

Primary target:

> intensive investigation of a bounded case/system in real-world context.

Possible cases:

- one gym;
- one training program;
- one team;
- one clinic;
- one implementation site;
- one community;
- several deliberately compared bounded cases.

Case studies commonly use multiple data sources.

Critical boundary:

> case study is not intrinsically synonymous with qualitative research.

A case study can contain qualitative, quantitative or mixed evidence. For RES-02 classification, record:

- case-study architecture;
- then separately record qualitative/quantitative/mixed data.

Also:

> qualitative case study ≠ clinical case report.

A clinical case report primarily describes a patient/event; case-study methodology investigates a bounded system/context and its processes.

## 5.6 Narrative inquiry

Recognized by SRQR among qualitative approaches.

Primary target:

> stories, temporality and how people construct experience through narrative.

Boundary:

> collecting an interview containing a story does not automatically make a study narrative inquiry.

Narrative inquiry needs a narrative-centered research question and analytic logic.

### Initial family map

| Scientific target | Canonical family candidate |
|---|---|
| lived experience / meaning | phenomenology |
| process / theory generation | grounded theory |
| culture / shared practices | ethnography |
| direct low-inference account | qualitative description |
| bounded real-world system | case study |
| story / temporality / identity through narrative | narrative inquiry |

# 6. Methodology / analysis distinction

## Thematic analysis

Thematic analysis identifies and interprets patterned meaning/themes across qualitative data.

It is best routed primarily as:

> analysis method / analytic family,

not automatically as the study design.

Braun & Clarke emphasize that multiple forms of thematic analysis exist with different philosophical and procedural assumptions.

Therefore record:

- type/version of TA where stated;
- inductive/deductive orientation;
- reflexive/codebook/coding-reliability logic where relevant;
- theoretical stance.

Do not infer:

> “used thematic analysis” → “design = thematic analysis”.

## Qualitative content analysis

Hsieh & Shannon distinguish:

- conventional;
- directed;
- summative content analysis.

These are analytic approaches/techniques concerned with interpreting textual content.

They do not, by themselves, determine whether the parent study is:

- qualitative description;
- case study;
- phenomenology;
- process evaluation;
- instrument-development research.

## Framework analysis / template analysis / other coding frameworks

Default routing at RES-02 level:

> analytic method unless the source explicitly defines a broader methodological design.

## IPA boundary

Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis is more tightly coupled to phenomenological inquiry than generic thematic analysis.

Do not flatten IPA to:

> “just themes”.

For classification, preserve its phenomenological/lived-experience purpose and its specific analytic approach.

# 7. Sampling is a separate axis

## Purposeful/purposive sampling

Used to identify information-rich cases relevant to the phenomenon.

Possible purposeful strategies include:

- criterion;
- maximum variation;
- homogeneous;
- typical case;
- extreme/deviant case;
- stratified purposeful;
- critical case;
- combinations/multistage purposeful strategies.

Exact labels vary across sources.

## Theoretical sampling

Special role in grounded theory:

> emerging categories guide what data/cases should be sampled next to develop the theory.

This is not merely:

> “we purposively recruited people with the condition.”

## Snowball / chain referral

Primarily a recruitment/selection mechanism.

It does not define qualitative methodology.

## Convenience / volunteer sampling

Possible but should be recorded explicitly because accessibility may shape the range of perspectives represented.

## Probability sampling

Not the defining norm for qualitative research and not required for qualitative inference; however, its presence would not by itself make data non-qualitative.

### Rule

Record sampling separately from:

- design;
- collection;
- analysis.

# 8. Sample adequacy: saturation is not one universal rule

## 8.1 Theoretical saturation

Historically grounded in grounded theory.

Target:

> further data no longer add properties/insights needed to develop the emerging conceptual categories/theory.

It is linked to:

- iterative analysis;
- theoretical sampling;
- category development.

## 8.2 Generic “data saturation”

Used widely outside grounded theory, but definitions and operationalization vary.

Hennink et al. demonstrate an important distinction:

### Code saturation

> enough data to identify the range of thematic issues/codes.

### Meaning saturation

> enough data to develop richer understanding, nuance and dimensions of those issues.

Their empirical study reached these at different sample sizes.

Therefore:

> “saturation reached” without definition and assessment logic is weak metadata.

## 8.3 Information power

Malterud et al. propose sample adequacy based on how much relevant information the sample provides.

Five factors:

1. study aim;
2. sample specificity;
3. use of established theory;
4. quality of dialogue;
5. analysis strategy.

General implication:

> no universal number of interviews is methodologically valid across all qualitative studies.

### Project rule

For every qualitative paper, extract:

1. planned sample logic;
2. achieved sample;
3. sampling strategy;
4. stopping/data-adequacy criterion;
5. definition of saturation if claimed;
6. evidence for how adequacy was assessed.

Do not award methodological strength merely because the word “saturation” appears.

# 9. Context, reflexivity and researcher role are structural metadata

Qualitative findings are produced through interaction among:

- question;
- participants/data;
- context;
- researcher;
- analytic framework.

SRQR and COREQ therefore require explicit attention to:

- researcher characteristics;
- qualifications/experience;
- relationship with participants;
- assumptions/presuppositions;
- setting/context;
- how researcher characteristics may influence questions, data collection, analysis and transferability.

JARS-Qual similarly asks researchers to describe prior understandings and how those understandings influenced or were managed in the study.

### Project interpretation

Reflexivity is not equivalent to:

> “researcher bias successfully removed.”

It is better recorded as:

> explicit examination/documentation of the researcher’s position, assumptions, relationships and influence on knowledge production.

For observation-based studies additionally record:

- researcher role in the setting;
- degree/duration of engagement;
- whether participants knew the researcher role;
- field-note/data-production process.

# 10. Qualitative scientific claims: what they can and cannot answer

Qualitative designs can directly support claims about:

- meanings;
- experiences;
- perceptions;
- barriers/facilitators;
- social/cultural practices;
- context;
- how participants understand mechanisms/processes;
- generation/refinement of theory;
- implementation experience;
- explanation of unexpected patterns;
- acceptability/usability.

They do not by themselves estimate:

- population prevalence;
- risk/probability;
- diagnostic sensitivity/specificity;
- prediction calibration/discrimination;
- average intervention-effect magnitude;
- dose-response effect size;
- population causal effect.

### Important nuance

Qualitative research can contribute strongly to causal explanation/theory and reveal candidate mechanisms.

But:

> participant explanation of why an intervention worked is not by itself identification of a causal effect or formal causal mediation.

Route quantitative causal-effect claims back to the appropriate experimental/causal design branch.

# 11. Boundary with mixed methods

NIH OBSSR defines mixed methods around intentional use and integration of qualitative and quantitative data/results.

Critical distinctions:

## Multiple qualitative methods

Example:

> interviews + observation.

This is:

> multimethod qualitative research,

not mixed methods merely because there are multiple methods.

## Qualitative + quantitative collected but never meaningfully integrated

Presence of both data types alone is insufficient for strong mixed-methods classification under current integration-focused definitions.

## Mixed methods

Requires intentional relationship/integration between qualitative and quantitative components.

NIH examples of integration include:

- merging;
- connecting;
- embedding.

### Project rule

Record separately:

1. qualitative component;
2. quantitative parent design;
3. timing;
4. priority;
5. integration point;
6. integration purpose.

Detailed mixed-methods structures are deferred to the dedicated next RES-02 branch after qualitative family coverage.

# 12. Boundary with content-validity / instrument-development studies

COSMIN’s current content-validity framework defines key targets as:

- relevance;
- comprehensiveness;
- comprehensibility.

These can be assessed using qualitative methods such as:

- focus groups;
- interviews;
- Delphi-type procedures.

Therefore a paper can simultaneously be:

> qualitative data collection/analysis + measurement-instrument development/content-validity purpose.

### Critical rule

Do not classify:

> “focus groups about questionnaire items”

as generic qualitative inquiry only.

Also record:

- instrument-development/content-validity purpose;
- target population;
- expert involvement;
- which content-validity property is being assessed.

## Delphi boundary

Delphi is a structured consensus/elicitation method and may use:

- quantitative ratings;
- qualitative free text;
- both.

Therefore:

> Delphi ≠ automatically qualitative design.

Detailed instrument-development methodology remains owned by measurement topics.

---

# 13. Boundary with implementation / process evaluation

MRC process-evaluation guidance focuses on:

- implementation;
- mechanisms of impact;
- context.

Process evaluations may use:

- quantitative data;
- qualitative data;
- mixed methods.

Qualitative methods are particularly useful for:

- implementation experience;
- unexpected mechanisms;
- context;
- adaptation;
- participant/provider perspectives.

### Critical rule

> process evaluation ≠ qualitative study design.

Correct representation:

> process-evaluation purpose + qualitative/quantitative/mixed method architecture + parent intervention/program context.

A qualitative process evaluation embedded in an RCT remains:

- qualitatively analyzed process evidence;
- embedded within a randomized parent trial.

The RCT does not randomize the qualitative findings themselves.

# 16. Canonical classification router

## Step 1 — What is the scientific target?

- lived experience?
- culture/practice?
- process/theory?
- direct description?
- bounded case?
- narrative/story?
- implementation/process?
- instrument content?

## Step 2 — What qualitative methodology is actually claimed and implemented?

Do not infer design from the title alone.

## Step 3 — What is the unit/case/context?

Identify:

- individual;
- group;
- setting;
- organization;
- program;
- community;
- event;
- document/media corpus.

## Step 4 — How were data sources selected?

Record:

- purposive strategy;
- theoretical sampling;
- convenience;
- snowball;
- other.

## Step 5 — How were data collected?

Record separately:

- interview;
- focus group;
- observation;
- field notes;
- diaries;
- documents;
- audio/video/images;
- multiple sources.

## Step 6 — How was adequacy decided?

- theoretical saturation?;
- code saturation?;
- meaning saturation?;
- information power?;
- predetermined sample?;
- pragmatic stopping?;
- not reported?

## Step 7 — What was the researcher’s role?

- relationship with participants;
- positionality/reflexivity;
- observer/participant role;
- prior assumptions/experience.

## Step 8 — How were data analyzed?

- thematic analysis;
- qualitative content analysis;
- IPA;
- grounded-theory analytic procedures;
- narrative analysis;
- framework/template analysis;
- other.

## Step 9 — Is this part of another design?

- RCT?;
- cohort?;
- feasibility study?;
- process evaluation?;
- instrument development?;
- mixed methods?

## Step 10 — Calibrate the claim

Use the strongest claim supported by the qualitative architecture without silently converting:

- participant accounts → population frequencies;
- perceived mechanism → causal mediation;
- local context → universal generalization.

# 17. “Do not confuse” matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| qualitative research vs interview | interview is collection method |
| focus group vs design | focus group is collection format |
| observation vs ethnography | ethnography requires cultural/contextual methodological logic |
| themes vs phenomenology | thematic output alone does not establish phenomenology |
| grounded-theory coding vs grounded-theory design | full GT requires theory-development architecture, usually iterative sampling/analysis |
| purposive sampling vs qualitative design | sampling is a separate axis |
| theoretical sampling vs generic purposive sampling | theoretical sampling responds to emerging categories in GT |
| “saturation” vs proven adequacy | saturation has multiple definitions and must be operationalized |
| code saturation vs meaning saturation | issue range and interpretive depth can require different data amounts |
| information power vs statistical power | different logics |
| reflexivity vs removal of researcher influence | reflexivity documents/examines influence rather than proving its absence |
| thematic analysis vs methodology | TA is primarily an analytic family usable across designs |
| content analysis vs study design | it is primarily an analysis technique/family |
| case study vs case report | bounded-system inquiry vs descriptive patient/event report |
| case study vs automatically qualitative | case studies can use qualitative, quantitative or mixed evidence |
| multimethod qualitative vs mixed methods | multiple qualitative sources are not qual+quant integration |
| qual + quant present vs integrated mixed methods | mixed methods requires intentional integration/connection |
| process evaluation vs qualitative design | process evaluation can be qual, quant or mixed |
| focus groups for item development vs generic qualitative study | scientific purpose is content validity/instrument development |
| Delphi vs automatically qualitative | Delphi can be quantitative, qualitative or mixed |
| perceived mechanism vs causal mediation | subjective explanation is not formal causal identification |
| local qualitative finding vs population prevalence | transferability is not statistical prevalence estimation |

# 18. Confidence of current taxonomy

## HIGH

Supported consistently by SRQR / COREQ / JARS-Qual and methodology literature:

- approach, sampling, data collection and analysis are separate dimensions;
- interviews/focus groups are not atomic study designs;
- context/reflexivity are important structural metadata;
- phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography and case study are distinct methodological families;
- thematic/content analysis should not automatically be treated as parent designs;
- mixed methods requires qualitative + quantitative integration;
- process evaluation can use qualitative, quantitative or mixed methods;
- content-validity work can use qualitative methods without becoming merely “generic qualitative research”.

## MODERATE-HIGH

- the project’s exact canonical family list;
- the proposed routing architecture;
- treating qualitative description as its own canonical family for RES-02.

Reason:

> qualitative methodology has multiple traditions and no single universal taxonomy; the project taxonomy is intentionally pragmatic while preserving the strongest stable distinctions.

# 20. Status after PASS-06D1

## Established

1. Qualitative research is a broad primary-study family, not one atomic design.
2. Scientific purpose must be separated from data-collection method.
3. Interviews, focus groups and observation are methods/data sources.
4. Major canonical families currently include:
   - phenomenology;
   - grounded theory;
   - ethnography;
   - qualitative description;
   - case study;
   - narrative inquiry.
5. Sampling is a separate design axis.
6. Saturation is methodology-dependent and must be defined/operationalized.
