# PHY-15 — Respiratory exercise physiology

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-18**  
Область: 03 — Physiology

## 0. Core decision

Exercise respiration is an integrated ventilation–gas-exchange–transport system.

Canonical model:

`exercise metabolic demand`
`→ neural + chemical ventilatory drive`
`→ VT + breathing frequency`
`→ minute/alveolar ventilation`
`+ pulmonary perfusion/V-Q + diffusion`
`→ O2 loading / CO2 removal`.

Whole-body oxygen delivery then additionally depends on:

`arterial O2 content + cardiac output + tissue perfusion/diffusion + mitochondrial utilization`.

The respiratory system therefore cannot be understood from VE alone.

## 1. Minute ventilation

`VE = VT × fB`.

Minute ventilation is total ventilated volume per minute.

It is not identical to effective alveolar ventilation.

## 2. Alveolar ventilation

Approximate relationship:

`VA = (VT - VD) × fB`.

Physiological dead space means equal VE can produce different alveolar ventilation.

Project rule:

**VE ≠ VA.**

## 3. Tidal volume and breathing frequency

Exercise VE rises through both:

- deeper breaths;
- faster breathing.

Typical pattern:

- VT expands strongly from rest;
- mechanical constraints progressively limit further VT expansion;
- fB contributes more at high ventilatory demand.

Exact pattern is individual and task dependent.

## 4. Pulmonary gas exchange

Pulmonary gas exchange requires:

- alveolar ventilation;
- pulmonary perfusion;
- V/Q matching;
- alveolar-capillary diffusion;
- adequate transit/contact conditions.

Project rule:

**gas exchange is not a ventilation-only process.**

## 5. Whole-body oxygen transport

Canonical cascade:

`inspired O2`
`→ alveolar ventilation`
`→ pulmonary diffusion/V-Q`
`→ arterial O2 content`
`→ cardiac output`
`→ tissue diffusion`
`→ mitochondrial utilization`.

Project rule:

**high ventilation alone does not establish high VO2 or high O2 delivery.**

## 6. CO2 removal

Exercise increases VCO2 and requires increased effective ventilation.

VE/VCO2 reflects how much ventilation is required for a given CO2 production.

Interpretation depends on:

- dead space;
- V/Q matching;
- PaCO2 regulation;
- exercise phase;
- analytic method.

Project rule:

**VE/VCO2 is ventilatory efficiency, not a generic fitness score.**

## 7. Exercise hyperpnea

Exercise ventilation is controlled by interacting:

- feedforward/central exercise drive;
- chemoreflexes;
- locomotor-muscle afferents;
- respiratory-muscle afferents;
- pulmonary/mechanical feedback;
- CO2/H+ signals;
- contextual inputs.

Project rule:

**there is no single universal exercise-ventilation controller.**

## 8. Ventilatory thresholds

VT1/VT2/RCP-type landmarks are useful exercise-response constructs.

They are not one literal instantaneous biochemical switch.

Their estimates depend on protocol, measurement and criteria.

Detailed use belongs to TST/GPP/PRO.

## 9. Respiratory muscle work

The diaphragm and accessory respiratory muscles perform meaningful work.

With high ventilation:

- pressure generation rises;
- expiratory muscles become active;
- breathing has a measurable metabolic cost;
- intrathoracic pressure interacts with cardiac filling/ejection;
- respiratory-circulatory competition becomes more relevant.

Project rule:

**breathing is not free at high exercise demand.**

## 10. Respiratory muscle fatigue

Both inspiratory and expiratory muscles can fatigue during severe-intensity exercise.

Fatigue can appear before task failure and increase toward intolerance.

Project rule:

**respiratory muscle fatigue is real but not universal across exercise sessions.**

Subjective dyspnea is not a direct measurement of muscle fatigue.

## 11. Acute aerobic response

Integrated pattern:

`VO2/VCO2 demand ↑`
`→ ventilatory drive ↑`
`→ VT/fB/VE ↑`
`→ alveolar ventilation + pulmonary perfusion/diffusion adjust`
`→ arterial gases usually maintained`.

At very high demand respiratory work/mechanical limits can become important.

## 12. Acute resistance-exercise response

RT respiratory demand depends on:

- set duration;
- repetitions;
- muscle mass;
- metabolic accumulation;
- rest;
- breathing strategy;
- Valsalva/breath holding;
- BFR/occlusion.

Project rule:

**steady-state aerobic ventilation rules cannot be copied directly to RT.**

## 13. Ordinary endurance-training adaptation

Endurance training improves whole-body aerobic capacity.

Healthy athletes often show high lung-function/diffusing-capacity values, but current evidence does not prove that ordinary endurance training proportionally enlarges/improves resting lung function.

Cross-sectional athlete differences can reflect:

- selection;
- anthropometry;
- developmental factors;
- sport specialization;
- training.

Project rule:

**better aerobic fitness ≠ proven proportional lung enlargement.**

## 14. Targeted IMT/RMT

Inspiratory/respiratory muscle training directly targets respiratory muscles.

Evidence supports:

- increased inspiratory strength;
- possible diaphragm hypertrophy/thickness increase.

Performance transfer is less certain because current athlete evidence is small, heterogeneous and low-certainty.

Project rule:

**targeted respiratory-muscle adaptation is real; universal ergogenic transfer is not established.**

## 15. Respiratory-system limitation

Most healthy non-elite adults usually have substantial respiratory reserve.

At very high endurance demand possible limits include:

- expiratory flow limitation;
- high work of breathing;
- respiratory-muscle fatigue;
- exercise-induced arterial hypoxemia;
- diffusion/V-Q limitations.

Project rule:

**the healthy respiratory system is usually sufficient but not universally non-limiting.**

## 16. Exercise-induced arterial hypoxemia

EIAH is documented particularly in highly trained endurance athletes.

Potential contributors include:

- relative hypoventilation;
- V/Q mismatch;
- diffusion limitation.

Project rule:

**exercise desaturation is not automatically physiological EIAH; unexplained desaturation requires clinical evaluation.**

## 17. Dyspnea / clinical boundary

Hard exercise can normally cause substantial breathlessness.

PHY-15 does not diagnose:

- asthma/exercise-induced bronchoconstriction;
- COPD;
- interstitial disease;
- pulmonary vascular disease;
- dysfunctional breathing;
- inducible laryngeal obstruction;
- cardiac/anemic causes of dyspnea;
- unexplained desaturation.

Clinical interpretation belongs to MED.

## 18. Canonical project rules

1. `VE = VT × fB`.
2. Minute ventilation ≠ alveolar ventilation.
3. VT and fB are distinct contributors.
4. Gas exchange requires ventilation + perfusion/V-Q + diffusion.
5. Pulmonary O2 loading ≠ whole-body O2 delivery.
6. VE alone ≠ VO2.
7. VE/VCO2 is a ventilatory-efficiency construct.
8. Exercise hyperpnea is multi-controller.
9. Ventilatory thresholds are constructs, not one literal biochemical switch.
10. Respiratory muscles perform meaningful work.
11. Respiratory-muscle fatigue is real but context dependent.
12. RT respiratory response is intermittent and breathing-strategy dependent.
13. Ordinary endurance training ≠ proven proportional lung enlargement.
14. IMT/RMT is a distinct respiratory-muscle intervention.
15. Healthy respiratory reserve is substantial but not unlimited.
16. EIAH can occur in highly trained endurance athletes.
17. Unexplained dyspnea/desaturation belongs to MED.

## 19. Training-relevant implications

1. **Interpret VE with VT/fB and dead-space context.**
2. **Do not use ventilation as a surrogate for VO2 or oxygen delivery.**
3. **Do not interpret VE/VCO2 as a generic performance score.**
4. **Use ventilatory thresholds only with method/protocol context.**
5. **Expect respiratory muscle work to matter most at high ventilatory demand.**
6. **Do not assume every hard session creates respiratory muscle fatigue.**
7. **Treat RT breathing separately from steady-state endurance breathing.**
8. **Do not describe endurance training as simply “making the lungs bigger.”**
9. **Treat IMT/RMT as a distinct intervention rather than a default requirement.**
10. **Route abnormal dyspnea/desaturation to MED.**
