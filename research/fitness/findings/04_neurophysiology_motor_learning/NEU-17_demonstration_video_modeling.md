# NEU-17 — Demonstration / video / modeling

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-19**  
Область: 04 — Neurophysiology / Motor Learning

## 0. Core decision

Observational learning is useful, but “show a model” is not one intervention.

A defensible project model is:

`model identity/quality`
+
`observation target`
+
`timing`
+
`playback format`
+
`physical practice`
+
`learner information needs`
+
`individual morphology/task constraints`
→
`modeling strategy`.

Project rule:

**use demonstration/video to make task-relevant relations observable, pair observation with physical practice, and verify delayed independent performance.**

---

## 1. Visual demonstration

Visual demonstration can improve motor-skill learning compared with no observation.

But it does not replace physical practice.

Project rule:

**demonstration establishes/refines task representation; physical attempts are still required.**

---

## 2. Expert model

Expert models provide a clear successful reference.

They can show:

- task goal;
- temporal structure;
- successful coordination;
- stable execution.

But expert models are not universally superior to self/peer/novice models.

Project rule:

**use expert models when a successful reference is the missing information.**

---

## 3. Peer / novice model

Peer/novice models can provide:

- common errors;
- correction progression;
- contrast between unsuccessful and successful attempts.

They are not inherently inferior.

Project rule:

**use peer/novice models when error contrast itself teaches a relevant relation.**

---

## 4. Mixed expert + novice modeling

Mixed modeling can combine:

- a correct task template;
- an error-comparison opportunity.

Task-specific evidence supports better stability/generalization in some conditions.

Project rule:

**mixed modeling is a useful option, not a universal hierarchy.**

---

## 5. Self-modeling

Self-modeling uses the learner's own performance as the observed model.

Potential advantages:

- direct mapping between observed and felt movement;
- visible progress;
- high self-relevance.

Current evidence:

- positive post-test effect;
- high heterogeneity;
- no robust demonstrated one-week pooled benefit;
- methodological limitations in power, blinding and delayed testing.

Project rule:

**self-modeling is promising but not established as a universally superior durable-learning strategy.**

---

## 6. Self-modeling ≠ video feedback

Self-modeling and video feedback are different functions.

### Self-modeling
Own performance is the model/reference.

### Video feedback
Own recent attempt is inspected for current error/performance information.

A single replay can serve both functions.

Project rule:

**classify video by instructional purpose, not modality alone.**

---

## 7. Video feedback

Video can reveal movement information not reliably available from first-person sensation.

Direct novice squat evidence shows:

- improved acquisition;
- selected retention for several days to one week.

But universal video superiority is not established.

Project rule:

**use video when it reveals information needed for a justified correction.**

---

## 8. Slow-motion review

Slow motion can make brief/fast events easier to inspect.

But randomized evidence does not show universal superiority over real-time viewing.

Project rule:

**use slow motion diagnostically when it exposes a relevant relation; keep real-time viewing for true temporal structure.**

---

## 9. Interactive replay

Pause, rewind, slow motion and learner-controlled timing form a combined information-access strategy.

Recent evidence suggests learner-controlled interactive replay can improve retention in some tasks.

This is not evidence that slow motion alone is causal.

Project rule:

**treat interactive playback as a multifactorial intervention.**

---

## 10. Observation before execution

Pre-practice demonstration can establish:

- task goal;
- broad sequence;
- spatial/temporal structure.

Project rule:

**show unfamiliar task structure before first attempts when useful.**

---

## 11. Observation between attempts

Re-observation can become more informative after the learner has performed an attempt.

It can support:

- own-attempt/model comparison;
- error updating;
- task-relation refinement.

No universal observation frequency exists.

Project rule:

**repeat observation when it adds new comparison information, not automatically after every attempt.**

---

## 12. What to observe

“Watch the model” is insufficient.

Observation should target a specific relation/event such as:

- task outcome;
- implement path;
- timing relation;
- segment relation;
- critical event;
- error-success contrast.

Project rule:

**direct attention to the task-relevant information the model is intended to teach.**

---

## 13. Observation and error detection

Observation can help build error-detection/reference mechanisms.

It can also bias those mechanisms if:

- the model is misleading;
- model feedback is misleading;
- the learner extracts the wrong reference.

Project rule:

**model/reference quality matters.**

---

## 14. Camera angle / viewpoint

Viewpoint changes what information is visible.

Evidence shows camera angle can alter acquisition without necessarily affecting retention.

Project rule:

**choose viewpoint by the relation being inspected; no universal best camera angle exists.**

---

## 15. Anthropometry / morphology

Body proportions, mobility and strength can alter movement geometry.

Examples include:

- squat stance-width effects;
- ankle-dorsiflexion-related compensations;
- strength/flexibility relationships.

But anthropometry is not a deterministic technique formula.

Project rule:

**copy task constraints and useful coordination relations, not another lifter's exact joint geometry.**

---

## 16. Expert geometry ≠ universal geometry

A skilled model demonstrates one successful solution.

Motor abundance means multiple task-successful solutions may exist.

Project rule:

**do not teach exact kinematic imitation unless that geometry itself is a justified task/safety criterion.**

---

## 17. Direct resistance-exercise evidence

Direct long-term observational-modeling evidence in resistance exercise is sparse.

Strongest checked direct learning evidence:

- own-video feedback for novice squat acquisition/retention.

Therefore:

**general observational-learning evidence constrains coaching logic, but exact model/video protocol for squat/hinge/press remains downstream work.**

---

## 18. Practical coaching synthesis

For later resistance-exercise technique work:

1. show a successful task example before first attempts when movement structure is unfamiliar;
2. tell the learner what relation to observe;
3. combine observation with physical attempts;
4. repeat demonstration in early practice when comparison adds information;
5. use expert models for successful reference;
6. use peer/error examples when contrast is informative;
7. use own-video when first-person sensation misses relevant information;
8. use slow motion only when it reveals a fast event;
9. preserve real-time viewing;
10. verify delayed model-free execution;
11. adapt demonstrated geometry to the learner's morphology, mobility, load and goal.

Status:

**PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not a universal modeling algorithm.

---

## 19. Canonical non-equivalences

1. Observation ≠ physical practice.
2. Demonstration benefit ≠ identical learning mechanism.
3. Expert model ≠ universally superior.
4. Peer/novice model ≠ inherently inferior.
5. Self-modeling ≠ video feedback.
6. Self-modeling post-test benefit ≠ durable learning.
7. Video feedback ≠ universally superior.
8. Slow motion ≠ universally better.
9. Interactive replay ≠ slow-motion effect alone.
10. Pre-practice observation ≠ sufficient for all learning.
11. Interspersed observation ≠ universally superior.
12. More demonstrations ≠ proportionally more learning.
13. Camera-angle acquisition effect ≠ retention effect.
14. Expert geometry ≠ universal geometry.
15. Anthropometry effect ≠ deterministic prescription.
16. Video analysis ≠ laboratory biomechanics.

---

## 20. Measurement boundaries

### Demonstration
Observation of another model to establish/refine task representation.

### Self-modeling
Own performance used as the model/reference.

### Video feedback
Own recent attempt inspected for current performance/error information.

### Feedforward
Modified self-representation of a not-yet-achieved performance.

### Model identity
Expert / peer / novice / self.

### Model quality
Successful / error-containing / mixed.

### Playback
Real time / slow motion / interactive.

### Timing
Pre-practice / interspersed / learner-controlled.

### Observation target
Specific relation/event being inspected.

### Acquisition
Practice-period improvement.

### Retention
Delayed independent performance.

### Transfer
Changed task/load/context.

---

## 21. Confidence

### HIGH

- observational learning is useful vs no observation;
- observation does not replace physical practice;
- self-modeling and video feedback are distinct functions;
- slow motion is not universally superior;
- post-test improvement ≠ durable learning;
- exact expert geometry should not be copied universally.

### MODERATE-HIGH

- expert models provide useful successful references;
- peer/novice models can support error comparison;
- mixed expert+novice modeling can be useful;
- directed visual attention can improve observational learning;
- own-video can support novice squat retention.

### MODERATE

- self-modeling immediate/post-test benefit;
- learner-controlled interactive replay;
- interspersed demonstration during early practice;
- observation-based error-detection learning.

### LOW / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- universal model hierarchy;
- exact demonstration frequency;
- exact playback speed/dose;
- exact camera angle for compound lifts;
- anthropometry-matched model selection.

### NOT ESTABLISHED AS UNIVERSAL

- expert > self/peer;
- self-modeling > other modeling;
- slow motion > real time;
- video > other feedback;
- one timing schedule > others;
- exact-copy expert geometry.
