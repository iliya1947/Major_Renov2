# 2. Canonical scientific target

Autoethnography uses the researcher's own experience as empirical material to understand and analyze a wider:

- cultural;
- social;
- institutional;
- professional;
- relational;

world.

Ellis, Adams and Bochner's canonical formulation is:

> systematic analysis of personal experience for understanding cultural experience.

### Canonical project representation

researcher-as-experiencing participant
+
systematic reflexive/analytic use of self-experience
+
explicit connection to culture/social world
+
ethical handling of implicated others
+
variant-specific representation/analysis.

### Critical rule

Personal writing alone is not enough.

The **self–culture analytic connection** is constitutive.

---

# 3. Researcher as participant / data source

In autoethnography, the researcher is not only:

- observer;
- interviewer;
- analyst.

The researcher is also a principal:

- participant;
- experiential data source;
- embodied knower;
- narrator.

Possible research material includes:

- remembered experience;
- contemporaneous journal;
- field notes;
- correspondence;
- photographs;
- medical/training records;
- social-media material;
- artifacts;
- observations;
- conversations;
- interviews with others.

### Critical rule

Researcher-as-source does not imply:

> only memory is allowed.

Autoethnographies may use multiple forms of evidence.

---

# 4. Self is not the endpoint

Autoethnography is not methodologically complete if it ends with:

> "this is what happened to me."

It asks:

> What does this experience reveal about a social/cultural world?

Potential cultural objects:

- pain normalization;
- athletic identity;
- masculinity/femininity;
- disability;
- coaching authority;
- medical culture;
- performance expectations;
- body norms;
- rehabilitation systems;
- sport institutions.

### Rule

The "auto" must remain connected to the "ethno."

---

# 5. Autobiography / memoir vs autoethnography

## Autobiography / memoir

Primary target may be:

- life story;
- recollection;
- personal meaning;
- literary expression.

It does not necessarily require systematic cultural analysis or research framing.

## Autoethnography

Requires explicit research work connecting personal experience to:

- cultural processes;
- social structures;
- institutional practices;
- theoretical/literature context.

### Critical rule

First-person voice ≠ autoethnography.

A moving memoir can be excellent writing without being a research autoethnography.

---

# 6. Autoethnography vs ordinary reflexivity

Most qualitative research should be reflexive.

But:

> reflexive researcher presence ≠ autoethnography.

In ordinary qualitative research, reflexivity supports the study of something else.

In autoethnography, the researcher's own experience is itself a substantive empirical route into the cultural phenomenon.

### Example

Ethnographer writes:
> I noticed my coaching background shaped what I saw.

That is reflexivity.

Autoethnographer asks:
> How did my own socialization as a coach reproduce, resist and reveal the culture of toughness I am studying?

That can be autoethnographic analysis.

---

# 7. Evocative autoethnography

Evocative autoethnography foregrounds:

- lived subjectivity;
- narrative;
- emotion;
- embodiment;
- aesthetics;
- reader immersion;
- resonance;
- vulnerability.

The objective is often not to abstract away from the experience but to make cultural experience **felt and intelligible**.

Representational forms may include:

- stories;
- scenes;
- dialogue;
- poetry;
- layered accounts;
- creative nonfiction.

### Critical rule

Evocative AE should not be judged solely by criteria designed for coding-reliability or conventional realist ethnography.

---

# 8. Evocation is not an excuse for no cultural analysis

Even in evocative AE, personal story should illuminate:

- social relationships;
- cultural narratives;
- institutions;
- norms;
- identities.

### Failure mode

A highly emotional personal account that never connects self to culture may be:

- memoir;
- personal essay;
- testimony;

rather than a fully developed autoethnography.

### Rule

Emotional intensity ≠ methodological adequacy.

---

# 9. Analytic autoethnography

Anderson's analytic autoethnography is a distinct variant aligned more closely with analytic/realist ethnographic traditions.

Anderson identifies five key features:

1. complete-member-researcher status;
2. analytic reflexivity;
3. narrative visibility of the researcher's self;
4. dialogue with informants beyond the self;
5. commitment to theoretical analysis.

### Scientific target

Use the researcher's insider experience to contribute to broader theoretical understanding of social phenomena.

---

# 10. Complete-member-researcher status

For Anderson's analytic AE, the researcher is a full member of the social world under study.

Membership may be:

## Opportunistic

Membership existed before the research.

Examples:

- athlete;
- coach;
- disabled sport participant;
- clinician.

## Convert

Researcher enters for research and becomes a full member.

### Critical caveat

This complete-membership criterion belongs specifically to **analytic autoethnography as Anderson defines it**.

It should not be imposed on every autoethnographic variant.

---

# 11. Analytic reflexivity

Analytic AE requires sustained examination of:

- researcher's position;
- reciprocal influence;
- relationships with others;
- how membership shapes data;
- how analysis changes the researcher.

### Critical rule

Analytic reflexivity is relational.

It is not self-absorption detached from other actors and the social setting.

---

# 12. Narrative visibility of self

In analytic AE, the researcher's membership should remain visible in the publication.

### Boundary

A researcher can be an insider and use insider experience while hiding that presence in the final text.

That may be insider ethnography.

It is not necessarily analytic autoethnography under Anderson's criteria.

---

# 13. Dialogue beyond the self

This is a major distinction between analytic and strongly self-focused AE.

Anderson requires engagement with other members/informants.

Possible sources:

- interviews;
- observations;
- conversations;
- other participants' accounts;
- institutional materials.

### Why

One person's experience cannot by itself justify broad claims about an entire social world.

### Project rule

For analytic AE, check whether the author actually moves beyond self-experience.

---

# 14. Commitment to theoretical analysis

Analytic AE aims to develop/refine broader theoretical understanding.

### Example

Researcher's disability experience could be used to analyze:

- liminality;
- identity;
- stigma;
- institutional belonging;

rather than only to communicate personal suffering.

### Critical rule

Theory-driven/generalized social analysis is a feature of analytic AE.

It is not a mandatory aim of every evocative AE.

---

# 15. Evocative vs analytic is not simply art vs science

The field contains a long-running debate between evocative and analytic traditions.

A safer project representation is:

## Evocative pole

- emotional/narrative;
- resonance;
- subjective experience;
- reader involvement.

## Analytic pole

- explicit theoretical analysis;
- dialogue beyond self;
- analytic ethnographic commitments.

### Critical rule

Do not rank them automatically as:

> evocative = weak;
> analytic = rigorous.

They pursue partly different epistemic and representational goals.

Quality criteria must follow the declared variant.

---

# 16. The field is more plural than two variants

Current 2026 scoping review in medical education identified labels including:

- analytic;
- evocative;
- performance;
- narrative;
- critical;
- reflexive;
- meta-autoethnography;
- unspecified autoethnography.

It also found collaborative autoethnography to be common.

### Project rule

Do not force all autoethnography into only:

> evocative vs analytic.

Record the explicit subtype when given.

But evocative/analytic remains the most important foundational contrast for current taxonomy.

---

# 17. Collaborative autoethnography (CAE)

Collaborative autoethnography involves multiple researchers/participants jointly examining and interpreting their own experiences.

Potential architecture:

individual experiences
→ shared reflection/dialogue
→ comparison
→ collective cultural interpretation.

### Strengths

Can:

- reduce single-self closure;
- expose divergent experiences;
- surface shared professional/cultural structures;
- create dialogic reflexivity.

### Critical rule

Multiple authors ≠ collaborative autoethnography.

They must jointly use their own experience as data.

---

# 18. Collaborative AE still requires self–culture analysis

A group of professionals having a reflective discussion does not automatically become CAE.

The study should show:

- whose experiences are being analyzed;
- how reflections were recorded;
- how individual accounts became collective data;
- how analysis connected experiences to culture/institution.

### Rule

Group reflection ≠ methodology by itself.

---

# 19. Critical autoethnography

Critical AE typically connects personal experience to:

- power;
- inequality;
- race;
- gender;
- disability;
- coloniality;
- institutions;
- social justice.

### Project classification

autoethnography
+
critical orientation.

### Critical rule

The critical label should be supported by actual analysis of power/structure, not only by politically important subject matter.

---

# 20. Performance / arts-based autoethnography

Some autoethnographies use:

- performance;
- poetry;
- theatre;
- visual forms;
- embodied presentation.

These are representational/methodological variants.

### Rule

Creative representation does not eliminate research obligations around:

- culture;
- evidence;
- reflexivity;
- ethics.

---

# 21. Narrative autoethnography

Autoethnography frequently uses narrative.

But autoethnography and narrative inquiry are not identical.

Narrative AE uses personal story as a route to cultural analysis.

Narrative inquiry can study other people's stories without the researcher being the substantive empirical subject.

### Project rule

If:

researcher's own storied experience + culture link

→ autoethnography candidate.

If:

participants' stories/plots/identity trajectories are the central object

→ narrative inquiry.

Hybrid labels can be preserved when explicitly justified.

---

# 22. Embodied autoethnographic knowledge

Sport/exercise autoethnography can access experiences difficult to reconstruct through detached observation alone:

- pain;
- fatigue;
- disability;
- movement;
- skill acquisition;
- fear;
- embodiment;
- body-image change.

### Critical rule

Embodied access is an epistemic resource, not proof that interpretation is complete.

Insider embodiment can also normalize assumptions that require reflexive scrutiny.

---

# 23. Retrospective memory as data

Autoethnography often relies on memory.

Memory is:

- selective;
- reconstructive;
- shaped by later identity;
- sensitive to current meaning.

Ellis et al. explicitly reject the premise that narrative is an exact replay of past events.

### Project rule

Record whether accounts are:

- contemporaneous;
- retrospective;
- reconstructed from records;
- mixed.

Do not treat remembered detail as independently verified fact.

---

# 24. Contemporaneous records

Possible strengthening sources:

- diaries;
- journals;
- training logs;
- emails/messages;
- photographs;
- medical records;
- videos;
- competition records;
- field notes.

These can:

- trigger memory;
- establish chronology;
- reveal discrepancies;
- provide contextual evidence.

### Critical rule

Records can support factual reconstruction but do not eliminate interpretive mediation.

---

# 25. Selective storytelling

The autoethnographer necessarily chooses:

- what to tell;
- what to suppress;
- how to sequence;
- how to frame;
- what language to use.

This is analytically relevant.

The 2026 wheelchair-basketball autoethnography explicitly foregrounds this selective storytelling.

### Rule

Selection is not automatically a flaw.

But it should be reflexively acknowledged where it materially shapes the account.

---

# 26. Researcher vulnerability

Autoethnography can expose:

- illness;
- trauma;
- shame;
- disability;
- conflict;
- sexuality;
- mental distress;
- professional mistakes.

Potential risks persist after publication:

- reputational impact;
- professional consequences;
- unwanted recognition;
- inability to retract public disclosure.

### Project rule

Researcher consent to self-disclosure should not be treated as trivial merely because researcher = author.

---

# 27. Relational ethics

Autoethnography almost never involves only the self.

A personal story can reveal:

- family;
- teammates;
- coaches;
- clinicians;
- supervisors;
- partners;
- patients;
- colleagues.

Ellis describes **relational ethics** as responsibility to people embedded in the researcher's life and narrative.

### Critical rule

"I am only writing about myself" is often false in relational terms.

---

# 28. Implicated others

An implicated other may appear in the story even when not formally recruited as a participant.

Risks include:

- identification;
- embarrassment;
- disclosure of sensitive information;
- damaged relationships;
- professional consequences.

### Project extraction

Record:

- who is represented?;
- were they consented?;
- could they be recognized?;
- were details altered?;
- were composites used?;
- was continued consent considered?

---

# 29. Internal confidentiality

Even when names are changed, members of a:

- team;
- family;
- clinic;
- club;
- department;

may recognize who is being described.

Tolich emphasizes internal-confidentiality problems in autoethnography.

### Fitness relevance

A "former head coach at my small national team" may be identifiable despite a pseudonym.

### Rule

Pseudonymization ≠ guaranteed anonymity.

---

# 30. Prospective ethics vs retrospective consent

Tolich criticizes reliance on ethics solutions only after a manuscript has been written.

Problem:

A relative/colleague asked for consent after reading a completed story may face:

- relational pressure;
- conflict of interest;
- limited realistic ability to refuse.

### Project rule

Where other people are foreseeably central to the research story, relational ethics should be considered from project design onward.

---

# 31. Consent can be ongoing

Current applied ethics guidance treats consent in autoethnography as potentially dynamic.

This is particularly relevant when:

- long-term projects evolve;
- more sensitive material emerges;
- an initially peripheral person becomes identifiable.

### Rule

One initial permission does not necessarily settle every later representation.

---

# 32. Altering identifying details

Researchers may change:

- name;
- place;
- occupation;
- chronology;
- demographic details;
- circumstances.

### Trade-off

Protection can reduce identification risk.

But altering details can also:

- change meaning;
- distort cultural context;
- compromise factual traceability.

### Rule

Report material fictionalization/compositing where ethically possible.

Do not present heavily altered narrative as literal documentary fact.

---

# 33. Composite characters / scenes

Autoethnographic work may use composites to protect people or represent recurring experiences.

### Critical boundary

Composite representation must be clearly distinguished from:

- verbatim event reporting;
- literal chronology;
- individual factual case history.

### Project rule

Extract:
- composite used?;
- why?;
- what was transformed?;
- what claim type remains supported?

---

# 34. Ethical review boundary

Because the researcher is also the subject, some authors historically assumed autoethnography does not need conventional ethics oversight.

This is unsafe as a universal rule.

### Why

The study may include:

- identifiable others;
- patients;
- colleagues;
- sensitive institutional information;
- linked records.

### Project rule

Ethics requirements depend on jurisdiction, institution and research material.

Do not infer exemption from the methodology label alone.

---

# 35. Quality in evocative autoethnography

Ellis et al. discuss quality using concepts such as:

- narrator credibility;
- verisimilitude;
- coherence;
- resonance;
- usefulness;
- reader engagement.

### Important caveat

These criteria belong to an evocative/narrative tradition.

They are not universal autoethnography metrics.

### Rule

A vivid story is not automatically good research if the self–culture analytic link and ethics are absent.

---

# 36. Quality in analytic autoethnography

For Anderson-style analytic AE, stronger criteria include:

- complete membership;
- analytic reflexivity;
- visible self;
- dialogue beyond self;
- theoretical analysis;
- connection to broader social science evidence/theory.

### Critical rule

Applying only "resonance" to analytic AE would underappraise its theoretical goals.

Applying only theoretical abstraction to evocative AE would underappraise its representational goals.

---

# 37. Cross-variant quality architecture

Across variants, useful common checks include:

1. explicit autoethnographic purpose;
2. transparent subtype/orientation;
3. substantive self–culture connection;
4. reflexivity;
5. clarity of empirical materials;
6. transparency about memory/fictionalization/composites;
7. ethical accountability to implicated others;
8. representation coherent with the variant;
9. claims calibrated to evidence;
10. contribution beyond private self-expression.

### Critical rule

This is a project appraisal router, not a universal published checklist.

---

# 38. Reader resonance and generalization

Ellis et al. frame autoethnographic generalizability differently from statistical generalization.

Potential contribution occurs when a specific account:

- helps readers recognize cultural processes;
- illuminates unfamiliar lives;
- creates resonance or transferable insight.

### Critical rule

Reader resonance ≠ population representativeness.

A story resonating with many readers does not estimate:

- prevalence;
- risk;
- frequency;
- effect size.

---

# 39. Analytic generalization boundary

Analytic autoethnography can contribute to broader theory.

But theory generation from insider experience should remain proportionate to:

- dialogue with others;
- existing theory;
- field evidence;
- contextual scope.

Anderson explicitly warns against generalizing an entire complex social world from one self alone.

### Rule

N=1 self-experience can illuminate a cultural process.

It does not automatically establish that the process is universal.

---

# 40. Autoethnography vs conventional ethnography

## Ethnography

Researcher studies a social/cultural world through field engagement.

Researcher may be participant but need not be the substantive center.

## Autoethnography

Researcher's own experience/membership is an explicit empirical and representational center.

### Critical rule

Insider ethnography ≠ autoethnography automatically.

The self must be systematically used as data.

---

# 41. Autoethnography vs narrative inquiry

## Narrative inquiry

Scientific object:
- stories;
- temporality;
- plot;
- identity;
- telling.

## Autoethnography

Scientific architecture:
- researcher's self-experience;
- reflexive self-analysis;
- culture/social-world connection.

### Overlap

An autoethnography can be deeply narrative.

But:

> first-person story alone is not enough.

---

# 42. Autoethnography vs phenomenology

## Phenomenology

Targets lived-experience structure/meaning across an explicitly phenomenological methodology.

## Autoethnography

Uses the researcher's own experience to illuminate cultural/social structures.

### Example

Phenomenology:
> What is lived experience of disability after cancer treatment?

Autoethnography:
> How does my own post-cancer disability and entry into wheelchair basketball reveal cultural processes of identity, sport and disability?

---

# 43. Autoethnography vs case report

A medical case report may describe:

- diagnosis;
- treatment;
- course;
- outcome.

Autoethnography asks how the researcher's lived story connects to:

- culture;
- identity;
- social world;
- institution.

### Critical rule

A physician writing about their own disease course is not automatically conducting autoethnography.

---

# 44. Autoethnography vs self-study / reflective practice

Professional self-study/reflection may be used to improve:

- teaching;
- coaching;
- clinical practice.

It becomes autoethnographic only when personal reflection is systematically connected to a wider cultural/social analytic target.

### Rule

Reflective journal ≠ autoethnography by itself.

---

# 45. Collaborative AE vs focus group

A collaborative autoethnography group may engage in group discussion.

But the members are not simply participants answering researcher questions.

They are co-researchers using their own experiences as shared analytic material.

### Critical rule

Group interview/focus group ≠ collaborative autoethnography.

---

# 46. What autoethnography can establish

Depending on variant, autoethnography can support claims about:

- lived insider meaning;
- identity formation/disruption;
- embodied experience;
- cultural norms;
- institutional practices;
- power/social relations;
- professional socialization;
- cultural scripts;
- reflexive/theoretical interpretation of social processes;
- how one situated life reveals a larger cultural issue.

---

# 47. What autoethnography cannot establish by itself

Autoethnography alone does not estimate:

- population prevalence;
- incidence;
- average treatment effect;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism;
- causal mediation;
- population frequency of a cultural pattern.

### Critical rule

"My recovery changed after wheelchair basketball" does not establish:

> wheelchair basketball caused recovery in comparable patients.

It supports experiential/identity interpretation and potentially hypothesis generation.

---

# 48. Current sport exemplar — wheelchair basketball

A 2026 Disability and Rehabilitation study used **evocative autoethnography**.

The first author's own experience formed the primary empirical basis.

The work used nine short stories spanning:

- life before diagnosis;
- cancer treatment;
- acquired disability;
- identity disruption;
- wheelchair basketball;
- disability-identity reconstruction.

Co-authors contributed:

- methodological guidance;
- dialogic reflection;
- critical-friend work.

### Methodological lesson

This is a clear example of:

self-experience
+
sport culture/community
+
identity reconstruction
+
evocative narrative.

### Claim boundary

It can illuminate how wheelchair basketball supported **this situated identity reconstruction**.

It does not estimate the treatment effect of wheelchair basketball.

---

# 49. Current sport exemplar — concussion and role exit

A 2026 Frontiers study used autoethnography to examine a Chinese student-athlete's:

- sport-related concussion;
- recovery;
- social interaction;
- sociocultural norms;
- role exit;
- identity reconstruction.

### Methodological value

It shows the self–culture link directly:

individual injury experience
↔
sport norms
↔
academic/athletic roles
↔
identity.

### Critical rule

The account can expose cultural pressures around injury.

It cannot establish how common those pressures are in all student-athletes.

---

# 50. Current sport exemplar — beginner tennis

A 2026 positive autoethnography examined a beginner Korean tennis player's:

- learning;
- growth;
- resilience;
- hope;
- participation in club culture.

### Methodological lesson

Autoethnography can study ordinary sport participation, not only trauma or marginalization.

The analytic value comes from connecting personal development to:

- club culture;
- social learning;
- sport context.

---

# 51. Sport sociology precedent

Sparkes' 2000 Sociology of Sport Journal article explicitly addressed autoethnography/narratives of self in sport and debated what criteria should be used to judge this research.

### Project implication

Autoethnography is not a marginal method imported recently into sport.

Sport/exercise research has a long-standing methodological conversation about:

- embodiment;
- identity;
- self-narrative;
- appropriate quality criteria.

---

# 52. Candidate fitness questions suited to autoethnography

Examples:

- How did my own transition from athlete to coach expose norms of authority in strength training?
- What did my rehabilitation reveal about pain culture in competitive lifting?
- How did disability sport reshape my embodied identity after injury?
- How did my own use of performance-enhancement practices interact with gym masculinity and risk norms?
- How did becoming a novice again reveal hidden status hierarchies in a sport club?

### Critical condition

The question must go beyond:

> What happened to me?

toward:

> What does my situated experience reveal about the culture/social system?

# 57. Status after PASS-06D17

## Established

1. Autoethnography is a distinct qualitative methodology family using the researcher's own experience as empirical material to understand culture/social life.
2. The self–culture analytic link is constitutive; personal writing alone is insufficient.
3. Researcher-as-data-source does not imply memory-only data.
4. Autobiography/memoir, ordinary reflexivity and insider ethnography must be separated from autoethnography.
5. Evocative AE prioritizes narrative, affect, embodiment, resonance and reader engagement.
6. Analytic AE follows Anderson's five-feature architecture: complete membership, analytic reflexivity, visible self, dialogue beyond self and theoretical analysis.
7. Collaborative AE uses multiple researchers' own experiences as jointly analyzed data.
8. Other named variants include critical, performance, narrative and reflexive forms, but terminology is heterogeneous.
9. Memory is reconstructive; contemporaneous records can strengthen chronology without eliminating interpretation.
10. Relational ethics is structurally central because implicated others may be identifiable even when not formal participants.
11. Pseudonymization does not guarantee internal confidentiality.
12. Consent/ethics may need to be prospective and ongoing; autoethnography is not automatically exempt from ethics review.
13. Quality criteria must follow variant: resonance/verisimilitude are more central to evocative work; theory/dialogue beyond self are central to analytic AE.
14. Autoethnographic contribution can be culturally/theoretically transferable but does not establish prevalence or causal effects.
15. Current sport/exercise research provides direct examples in wheelchair basketball/disability identity, concussion/role exit and beginner tennis learning/culture.
