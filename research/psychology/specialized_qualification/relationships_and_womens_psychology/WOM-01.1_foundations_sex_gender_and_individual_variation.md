# WOM-01.1 — Foundations of the psychology of women: constructs, levels and heterogeneity

Date: 2026-11-25. Status: bounded research note complete; WOM-01 IN_PROGRESS.
Scope: methodological foundations for studying women's psychology, not a general description of all women or a biological/clinical qualification.

## 1. Question and baseline

How should sex-related characteristics, gender identity, gender roles, social position, lived experience and individual traits be distinguished in psychological research on women? Which group-level findings can inform individual hypotheses, and how do measurement and context limit their transfer?
The psychology of women is a dedicated field of inquiry encompassing women's experiences, motives, emotions, development and social conditions, not merely comparisons with men. The objective is to establish what is supported at each level, including real differences where demonstrated, similarities, and variation among women.

## 2. Evidence review

Targeted public searches on 2026-11-25: National Academies measuring sex gender identity; gender similarities and differences metasynthesis; gender differences personality nuances nations; measurement invariance IPIP-NEO facets; recent cross-national personality evidence; within-women diversity and intersectionality methods. This is a selected evidence review, not systematic, exhaustive or a reanalysis of data. Access depth indicated per source.

E1. National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2022). Measuring Sex, Gender Identity, and Sexual Orientation. https://doi.org/10.17226/26424 ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK581047/
Access: directly inspected executive summary from heading through final substantive paragraph; not full report. Consensus guidance separates sex-related traits, gender identity, expression and social position, and stresses precise construct-relevant measurement, voluntary disclosure, necessity and confidentiality. Gender categories alone do not measure anatomy, hormones, roles or lived experience. Biological and sociocultural influences can be intertwined. Guidance primarily developed for US research/data settings; not itself an empirical estimate of psychological differences or universal measurement validation. Reproductive-stage variables are deferred to WOM-06; actual relevance must be established rather than inferred from gender category.

E2. Zell, Krizan & Teeter (2015). Evaluating gender similarities and differences using metasynthesis. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25581005/
Access: indexed primary abstract and extracts; direct PubMed access blocked. 106 meta-analyses, 386 meta-analytic effect estimates; typical differences across the studied psychological variables were small, with variation across domains. This is an older broad summary, not an estimate for every construct or present-day population. Absolute mean differences across outcomes are not an individual-level predictor; heterogeneous and overlapping source samples, selection of studied constructs and historical measures require attention. No quantitative reanalysis or full-text audit.

E3. Hofmann, Rozgonjuk, Soto, Ostendorf & Mõttus (2025). There are a million ways to be a woman and a million ways to be a man: Gender differences across personality nuances and nations. Journal of Research in Personality 115, 104582. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2025.104582 ; final published copy https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/files/489785319/HofmannEtalJRP2025ThereAreAMillion.pdf
Access: author-institution metadata, PDF abstract, selected analysis and results, and limitations text inspected. Attempted page screenshot failed (cache miss); not a full article read or visual validation of all figures. Data from 74 countries and four inventories. Mean AUC across tested inventory models predicting binary reported gender from trait domains .73, facets .80, items .86; US-trained cross-country models averaged .67/.73/.77 respectively for domains/facets/items. Most univariate trait differences small or very small; average absolute d approximately .25 for domains and .24 for facets. Results varied across instruments and countries, with many narrower differences. Mean AUC across multivariate models predicts group labels from numerous characteristics, not a person's traits, motives or behavior from a group label. AUC is rank discrimination, not percentage correctly classified or individual posterior probability. Long inventories, gender-related item content, sample selection (authors flag WEIRD bias), translation and measurement comparability complicate interpretation. Different cross-country and inventory analyses use different data designs; their AUCs must not be merged. AUC direction, model purpose and construct are explicit. No causal conclusion about biology, socialization or their relative contributions.

E4. Temizyürek, Richardson & Brown (2024). Comparability of personality facets between men and women: a test of measurement invariance in IPIP-NEO facets in 49 countries. Journal of Research in Personality 113, 104551. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2024.104551 ; https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/30957
Access: institutional abstract, selected published PDF results and discussion directly inspected. Screenshot request failed (cache miss); no full end-to-end reading. Initial data 831,849 across 49 countries; reported within-country multigroup comparisons include 42 countries. About 48% of evaluated facet-by-country cases reached scalar invariance under their specified criteria. Self-consciousness reached threshold in 41/42 countries, self-efficacy and assertiveness in 40/42; depression, excitement seeking and cheerfulness facets in none. This is evidence about specific IPIP-NEO-120 measurements in these samples, not about absence of real psychological differences in those constructs. Configural, metric and scalar invariance are different conditions; partial invariance and practical impact can permit qualified analysis where justified. Fit thresholds and model assumptions are not absolute truth tests. Selected online data, factor structure and cross-country sample composition constrain interpretation. E3 and E4 draw in part on IPIP-NEO data; overlap is possible and not quantified here. Do not treat them as wholly independent evidence. E4 is a qualification of E3's interpretation, not a refutation of its classification results.

Access: indexed publisher metadata/abstract snippets, including representative adult sample description (N=143,313, 27 nations); full publisher access forbidden and preprint DOI failed. E6. Else-Quest & Hyde (2016). Intersectionality in Quantitative Psychological Research, Parts I and II. https://doi.org/10.1177/0361684316629797 ; https://doi.org/10.1177/0361684316647953
Access: indexed publisher abstracts/excerpts; Part II direct access failed. An approach for studying combined social positions, experiences and context rather than treating women as one uniform population. It is not a single falsifiable causal theory or a guarantee that every interaction is significant. Methods require explicit hypotheses, adequate representation, appropriate comparison and attention to structural conditions. Within-group variation is not reducible to demographic labels.

Small, selected sample, heterogeneous categories and context-specific measures; does not establish universal subgroup characteristics or causal mechanisms. An illustration of empirical questions within women's psychology rather than only female–male comparison.

## 3. Conceptual map and practical distinctions

Separate six analytical objects:
1. Sex-related variables: relevant biological characteristics measured specifically where needed, not assumed from a category or a life-stage stereotype.
2. Gender identity and its personal significance: self-identification differs from strength/salience/content of identity; no inference about someone's identity from appearance.
3. Social roles, norms and social position: distinguish actual expectations, perceived expectations, role constraints, voluntary endorsement, internalization and public compliance.
4. Individual biography and development: learning, accumulated experience, values, relationships, resources, current roles and developmental trajectories.
5. Psychological constructs: trait, motive, emotion, appraisal, cognition, observed behavior and self-report are distinct targets with their own measurement requirements.
6. Context and time: culture, cohort, opportunities, interpersonal setting and relevant life transitions can modify both exposure and psychological responses.

These objects can interact. Observed group differences are compatible with multiple biological, social, developmental and measurement explanations; category association alone does not identify the causal contribution of each. A null or small average difference does not rule out important differences in a specific outcome or condition. Conversely a large multivariate difference does not imply two internally uniform psychological types. Analyze women in their own right: compare experiences, trajectories, priorities and variability within women and across contexts when that is the actual question. Avoid turning group labels into personality explanations or assuming that all women share the same needs or strategies.

## 4. Quantitative illustration and transfer guardrail

Under a hypothetical equal-variance normal model only, two distributions with standardized mean difference d=.25 overlap by about 90% (2*Phi(-abs(d)/2)); probability that a randomly selected member of the higher-mean group exceeds a randomly selected member of the other is about 57% (Phi(abs(d)/sqrt(2))). These are model-based illustrations, not actual observed distributions or predictive probabilities for an individual. Variances, tails and nonnormality can change overlap. Distinguish univariate overlap from multivariate discrimination; multiple weak signals can combine to yield a larger AUC without making any single trait predictable from membership. E3 results illustrate this distinction. Do not convert AUC into accuracy at a fixed threshold, or reverse a classifier to infer unmeasured characteristics.

Before transferring any finding: identify measured variable and target construct; check population, age, culture, role context, method, group definitions, invariance, effect/uncertainty and heterogeneity; distinguish within- from between-person evidence; evaluate independent person-specific observations under INT-02. Treat population evidence as a qualified hypothesis prior, not a person-specific conclusion.

## 5. Generic practice checks

Self-assessment only, invented examples unrelated to private material.
A. In a fictional survey two groups have different mean scores but an item has a different interpretation across groups. Compare measurement models and alternative item content before claiming a latent difference; a reliable scale alone is not sufficient.
B. A classifier distinguishes two groups using a hundred questionnaire items with high AUC. It does not follow that membership predicts any particular item accurately or that the model identifies a causal mechanism. Check univariate distributions and actual prediction target.
C. Women in two fictional workplaces report different willingness to speak at meetings. Compare opportunity, role, tenure, perceived norms and individual preferences; neither gender identity nor one context is sufficient to identify motive. Do not infer a stable trait without comparable observations.
