## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a bounded model of detraining, residual adaptation, retraining and human skeletal-muscle memory sufficient for later programming, return-to-training, testing and individualization without:

- assuming all adaptations disappear at the same rate;
- equating training cessation with bed rest/immobilization;
- assuming strength loss equals muscle-size loss;
- assuming short breaks cause immediate full reversal;
- assuming retained adaptation is all-or-none;
- treating VO2max, strength, muscle size and architecture as sharing one detraining clock;
- assuming retraining always reproduces initial training at the same rate;
- treating “muscle memory” as one mechanism;
- claiming myonuclei are universally or indefinitely permanent in humans;
- claiming retained myonuclei prove faster hypertrophy during retraining;
- treating epigenetic/proteomic memory as proven performance advantage;
- treating a returning trainee as physiologically identical to an absolute novice.

### Question type

Longitudinal physiology of training cessation and re-exposure.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 11 exact Section 20 items must be covered at content level; strength, muscle size, architecture, neural and cardiorespiratory detraining must remain separate; residual adaptation must be outcome/time dependent; retraining must be distinguished from first training; myonuclear, epigenetic and proteomic memory must be evidence-graded separately from functional “regain”; causal uncertainty must be explicit.

### Evidence rule

“Detraining” is not one biological clock.

“Muscle memory” can refer to:
- faster functional regain;
- retained myonuclei;
- epigenetic/transcriptional priming;
- retained proteins/proteomic state;
- neural/skill memory;
- residual structural adaptations.

These are related hypotheses/phenomena, not interchangeable constructs.

## 3. Detraining: core model

Detraining is the partial or complete loss of training-induced adaptations after an insufficient training stimulus.

The rate and extent of loss depend on:

- prior training history;
- adaptation type;
- duration of cessation;
- whether activity is fully stopped or merely reduced;
- age/training status;
- tissue/outcome;
- nutrition/energy balance;
- ordinary daily activity;
- illness/injury/immobilization.

Important boundary:

**training cessation in an otherwise active person ≠ bed rest, limb immobilization or severe disuse.**

Disuse evidence can inform biological direction but should not be transferred quantitatively to ordinary training breaks.

---

## 4. Dynamics of strength loss

Strength can decline during detraining.

However the trajectory is not identical to muscle-size loss.

Human evidence shows:

- after 10 weeks of RT followed by 10 weeks detraining, 1RM leg press and biceps curl declined but were rapidly regained during retraining;
- after only 14 days without training in elite powerlifters, some neuromuscular performance variables and EMG patterns changed while muscle thickness/CSA did not show significant decline;
- systematic disuse evidence shows strength can decrease more than measured muscle mass, consistent with neural/functional contributions.

Strength loss can reflect changes in:

- neural drive;
- coordination/skill;
- rate coding/recruitment;
- antagonist coactivation;
- muscle size/architecture;
- tendon/mechanical properties;
- task familiarity.

Project rule:

**strength loss ≠ direct readout of muscle loss.**

No universal “X% strength per week” rule is supported.

---

## 5. Dynamics of muscle-mass loss

Muscle size generally declines with sufficiently long training cessation, but:

- short breaks may produce little measurable structural loss;
- longer detraining produces larger losses on average;
- different muscles and populations vary;
- measurement method matters.

The periodic-RT trial showed significant decreases in vastus lateralis and biceps-brachii CSA over 10 weeks detraining, followed by rapid regain.

In older adults, meta-analysis showed a clear post-training decline overall, with greater loss during longer 31–52 week cessation than shorter 12–24 week cessation.

The 2026 broad RCT meta-analysis found residual effects after detraining to be outcome- and time-dependent; muscle mass/lean body mass did not remain significantly improved across all follow-up windows.

Project rule:

**muscle loss is duration- and context-dependent, not immediate or uniform.**

---

## 6. Neural detraining

Neural adaptations contribute substantially to strength acquisition and can regress with reduced practice.

Current human evidence:

- 4 weeks unilateral strength training changed corticospinal excitability/inhibition and strength;
- 4 weeks detraining produced partial retention of strength and altered corticospinal responses;
- elite powerlifters after 14 days detraining showed changes in EMG activation/coactivation and rate-of-force-development-related performance without clear structural muscle loss.

This supports early functional loss occurring before large measurable atrophy in some contexts.

Limits:

- neural measures are method- and task-specific;
- TMS/EMG changes are not one universal “neural detraining” biomarker;
- loss of skill-specific performance is not necessarily loss of maximal force capacity.

Project rule:

**neural/skill detraining can contribute to early performance loss independently of large muscle-size loss.**

---

## 7. Muscle architecture during detraining

Architecture does not behave as one variable.

Potentially changing features include:

- fascicle length;
- pennation angle;
- muscle thickness/CSA;
- serial sarcomere number.

Current eccentric-training studies show:

- BFlh fascicle length and serial sarcomere number can decrease within 3 weeks of detraining after Nordic hamstring training;
- in another intervention, fascicle length and pennation angle returned toward baseline while hamstring ACSA remained elevated after detraining.

Therefore:

**architecture components can decay on different clocks from muscle mass and strength.**

A preserved CSA does not imply preserved fascicle architecture, and vice versa.

---

## 8. Cardiorespiratory detraining

VO2max and endurance-related adaptations can decline relatively quickly after training cessation.

Systematic/meta-analytic evidence in athletes shows significant VO2max reductions after both short- and long-term cessation, with greater pooled decline after longer cessation.

Mechanisms can include:

- plasma/blood-volume decline;
- lower stroke volume;
- reduced maximal cardiac output;
- reduced peripheral oxidative capacity;
- altered a-vO2 difference;
- metabolic changes.

Residual cardiorespiratory adaptation may persist.

The 2026 broad RCT meta-analysis found VO2max could remain above pre-intervention baseline across follow-up categories despite regression from the trained state.

Project rule:

**detraining can reduce VO2max without erasing all prior cardiorespiratory adaptation.**

---

## 9. Residual adaptations / retention

Adaptations are not all-or-none.

Possible states after cessation:

`trained peak`
`→ partial regression`
`→ residual adaptation above original baseline`.

Residual state depends on outcome and duration.

Examples:

- some VO2max benefit can remain above baseline despite detraining;
- strength can remain partly retained while neural measures change;
- muscle CSA may regress while specific molecular/proteomic signatures remain;
- some architecture changes can reverse faster than CSA;
- residual capillarization/oxidative features can outlast central cardiorespiratory changes.

Reduced maintenance training can preserve a large fraction of previous adaptations in some protocols. One 2024 trial found one concurrent-training session every 7 days largely maintained strength, muscle CSA and aerobic power for 12 weeks, while every 14 days retained roughly 90–95% of those adaptations.

Boundary:

**this specific frequency result is not a universal maintenance-dose prescription.**

Detailed minimum-effective maintenance dose belongs to PRO/GPP.

## 10. Retraining

Retraining after a break commonly produces rapid regain of previously acquired strength/size.

The 10-week train → 10-week detraining → 10-week retraining trial showed:

- strength and muscle size declined during detraining;
- the first 5 weeks of retraining produced larger gains than a comparable later 5-week period of continuous training;
- by the end, periodic and continuous training produced similar overall adaptations.

This supports fast regain after a short-term break.

But retraining rate depends on:

- detraining duration;
- residual adaptation;
- prior training duration;
- age;
- training status;
- task/skill;
- injury/illness context;
- program re-entry dose.

Project rule:

**retraining often regains lost adaptations faster than building from zero, but the advantage is not universal or infinitely persistent.**

---

## 11. “Muscle memory” is not one mechanism

At least four mechanistic families are plausible in human skeletal muscle:

1. retained myonuclei;
2. persistent epigenetic/transcriptional changes;
3. retained proteomic state;
4. neural/skill memory and residual structural adaptation.

These mechanisms may coexist.

No current evidence establishes one as the sole cause of faster retraining.

Project rule:

**functional muscle memory and molecular memory must be distinguished.**

---

## 12. Human myonuclear memory

Human evidence has strengthened, but the causal interpretation remains bounded.

A 2024 unilateral human study found:

- myonuclei increased during strength training;
- fibre CSA decreased during detraining;
- myonuclei were maintained over 16 weeks of detraining;
- previously trained muscle retained more type-II myonuclei than the contralateral control;
- during retraining, type-II fCSA was larger in the previously trained muscle, but the delta increase was not significantly greater.

Therefore the study supports **myonuclear retention/permanence over the observed detraining period**, but does not prove that retained nuclei caused faster hypertrophy.

Broader systematic evidence has been less uniform, and a 2025 review concludes that indefinite permanence/removal dynamics remain uncertain.

Project rule:

**retained human myonuclei are supported; indefinite permanence and a proven causal retraining advantage are not.**

---

## 13. Epigenetic / transcriptional memory

Human skeletal muscle can retain molecular marks after training.

Evidence includes:

- resistance-training studies showing retained DNA-methylation/transcriptional signatures after detraining;
- 2024/2025 HIIT study showing retained hypomethylation and gene-expression profiles through 3 months detraining and retraining.

Important boundary:

In the HIIT study, VO2max improved similarly in training and retraining; molecular memory did not produce a clearly superior physiological retraining response.

Therefore:

**epigenetic memory exists at the molecular level, but practical performance/hypertrophy benefit is not established simply by detecting retained methylation.**

---

## 14. Proteomic memory

A 2025 human repeated-RT study found:

- many training-induced proteins returned toward baseline during 10 weeks detraining;
- several proteins remained elevated;
- retained proteins included contraction/cytoskeletal/calcium-related factors;
- the second RT period reproduced many proteomic adaptations.

This supports a retained **proteomic signature** after training.

It does not establish that any single retained protein is the causal source of faster regain.

Project rule:

**retained proteomic memory is evidence of biological history, not proof of a master retraining mechanism.**

---

## 15. Returning trainee vs absolute novice

A returning trainee is generally not equivalent to a true novice because some combination of the following can persist:

- partial strength;
- residual muscle mass/architecture;
- motor skill/technique familiarity;
- neural coordination;
- myonuclear content;
- epigenetic/transcriptional state;
- proteomic state;
- tendon/cardiovascular/metabolic residual adaptations.

However:

- the amount retained depends strongly on break duration/context;
- some molecular memory can persist without a measurable functional advantage;
- a long enough interruption can erase many observable adaptations;
- injury/illness can change the return trajectory.

Project rule:

**previous training history is a real biological state variable, but it does not justify assuming a fixed retraining multiplier or skipping progressive re-exposure.**

Detailed return-to-training progression belongs PRO/REC/MED/IND.

## 17. Established project-level model

### Detraining

`trained state`
`→ reduced/absent stimulus`
`→ outcome-specific decay on different clocks`.

Possible sequence:

`neural / plasma-volume / metabolic changes`
may appear before
`large structural muscle loss`.

But this ordering is not universal across all contexts.

### Residual state

`post-detraining state`
can retain:
- performance;
- structure;
- neural skill;
- cellular/molecular memory

to different degrees.

### Retraining

`residual state + renewed stimulus`
`→ rapid reacquisition in many outcomes`.

The speed advantage can reflect multiple retained mechanisms and does not identify one causal mechanism.

## 18. Training-relevant implications

1. **Do not treat a short training break as complete loss of adaptation.**
2. **Do not infer muscle loss from strength loss alone.**
3. **Expect some neural/skill and cardiorespiratory changes before large atrophy in some contexts.**
4. **Treat muscle architecture as multiple variables with separate decay clocks.**
5. **Do not assume VO2max, muscle size and strength decay at the same rate.**
6. **Residual adaptation is outcome specific.**
7. **Retraining often restores recent losses quickly, but not by a fixed multiplier.**
8. **Do not claim myonuclear permanence indefinitely in humans.**
9. **Do not claim retained myonuclei cause faster hypertrophy unless the outcome demonstrates it.**
10. **Treat epigenetic/proteomic memory as mechanistic evidence, not a direct performance prescription.**
11. **A returning trainee should not automatically be programmed as an absolute novice, but return progression still depends on current capacity and break context.**
12. **Minimum maintenance dose and return-to-training dose belong to PRO/GPP/REC/IND.**

## 20. Confidence / unresolved issues

### HIGH

- detraining causes outcome-specific loss rather than synchronized total reversal;
- strength loss is not identical to muscle-size loss;
- VO2max declines with training cessation;
- short-term residual adaptations can persist;
- retraining can rapidly regain recent strength/size losses;
- molecular and functional muscle memory are distinct constructs.

### MODERATE-HIGH

- early neural/functional loss can precede large structural loss in some strength-trained populations;
- muscle architecture can regress on a different clock from CSA;
- reduced training can preserve substantial adaptation with less dose than development in some populations;
- retained molecular/proteomic signatures exist after human training/detraining.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- exact rate of strength or muscle-size loss for a given person;
- exact relative speed of neural vs structural detraining;
- duration of human myonuclear retention;
- degree to which retained myonuclei cause faster hypertrophy;
- practical magnitude of epigenetic/proteomic memory;
- duration for which a returning trainee retains a meaningful advantage;
- exact minimum maintenance dose.
