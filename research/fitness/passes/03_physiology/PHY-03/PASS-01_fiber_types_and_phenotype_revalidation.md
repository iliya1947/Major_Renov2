## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a bounded model of adult human skeletal-muscle fiber phenotype sufficient to interpret later evidence on:

- contraction modes;
- acute resistance-exercise response;
- chronic skeletal-muscle adaptation;
- hypertrophy;
- strength/power;
- fatigue;
- exercise selection/programming;
- individualization.

The model must avoid:

1. treating type I / IIA / IIX as perfectly discrete immutable boxes;
2. calling normal adult human fibers “type IIb” when the intended human isoform is IIx;
3. treating MyHC isoform as a complete surrogate for metabolism, fatigue resistance or whole-muscle performance;
4. assuming athlete cross-sectional fiber composition proves that training caused all observed differences;
5. assuming resistance training necessarily converts type I ↔ type II in a large predictable way;
6. using sex or age group averages to classify an individual;
7. prescribing training loads from guessed fiber type;
8. moving motor-unit recruitment detail into PHY-03 instead of NEU.

### Question type

Mechanism / phenotype / training adaptation.

### Critical output

A human-specific model of:

`MyHC identity + contractile kinetics + metabolic phenotype + fatigue phenotype + hybrid state + training plasticity`

with explicit boundaries on what can and cannot be inferred for training prescription.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 11 exact Section 5 items must be covered at content level; human type nomenclature, hybrid fibers, training plasticity, training-status interpretation, age and sex modifiers must be handled without overgeneralization; no project-relevant content blocker should remain before separate closure audit/synthesis.

## 3. Human fiber-type nomenclature

### 3.1 Type I

Adult human type I fibers predominantly express slow MyHC I, encoded by **MYH7**.

Canonical phenotype:

- slowest shortening/contractile kinetics among the three major adult human types;
- high oxidative specialization on average;
- relatively high fatigue resistance;
- lower ATP cost for sustained force/shortening relative to faster phenotypes in comparable conditions.

Project rule:

**type I = slow/oxidative-biased phenotype, not “weak fiber.”**

Whole-fiber force still depends on size, activation, length, velocity and experimental condition.

### 3.2 Type IIA

Adult human type IIA fibers predominantly express MyHC IIa, encoded by **MYH2**.

Canonical phenotype:

- faster contractile kinetics than type I;
- substantial oxidative capacity plus glycolytic capacity;
- intermediate fatigue resistance;
- capable of high force/power while remaining more fatigue resistant than IIx on average.

Project rule:

**IIA is not merely a halfway label; it is a major adaptable human fast-fiber phenotype.**

### 3.3 Type IIX

Adult human type IIX fibers predominantly express MyHC IIx, encoded by **MYH1**.

Canonical phenotype:

- fastest major adult human limb-fiber phenotype;
- lower oxidative / greater glycolytic bias on average;
- high shortening-rate/power potential;
- lower fatigue resistance on average.

Project rule:

**human IIx is the relevant very-fast adult phenotype; “IIb” terminology should not be imported from rodents as if it were the normal human equivalent.**

## 4. Human “IIb” terminology audit

Contemporary human skeletal-muscle classification recognizes three main adult limb/torso MyHC types:

1. MyHC I / MYH7;
2. MyHC IIA / MYH2;
3. MyHC IIX / MYH1.

True MyHC IIB / MYH4 is characteristic of several non-human mammalian muscles and is not a normal major adult human limb-fiber isoform.

Older human literature sometimes labeled a histochemical fast category as “IIb”; modern interpretation generally maps this historical category to **IIx**, not true rodent-like MyHC IIb.

Canonical rule:

**for this project, normal adult human fast fibers are IIA and IIX; do not create a human IIB prescription category.**

---

## 5. Myosin heavy chain isoforms and phenotype

MyHC isoform is a central and useful classifier because it strongly relates to cross-bridge kinetics and maximum shortening velocity.

But fiber phenotype includes more than MyHC:

- thin-filament/regulatory isoforms;
- Ca²⁺ handling proteins;
- mitochondrial abundance/function;
- glycolytic enzymes;
- capillary supply;
- substrate stores;
- fiber size;
- proteomic/transcriptional state;
- innervation/activity history.

Canonical rule:

**MyHC classification is a strong contractile anchor, not a complete molecular description of a fiber.**

---

## 6. Contractile-speed differences

At project level, the major adult human phenotype continuum is:

`Type I → Type IIA → Type IIX`

from slower to faster contractile kinetics.

MyHC motor properties contribute directly to this ordering.

However:

- whole-muscle contraction speed is not determined by fiber type alone;
- architecture, tendon mechanics, neural activation and task mechanics also matter;
- hybrid fibers occupy intermediate positions.

Canonical rule:

**fiber phenotype contributes to contraction speed; it does not uniquely determine whole-body movement speed.**

---

## 7. Metabolic-profile differences

Classic human pattern:

### Type I
More oxidative-biased:
- higher mitochondrial/oxidative specialization on average;
- suited to sustained ATP turnover.

### Type IIA
Mixed oxidative-glycolytic:
- high glycolytic capacity;
- substantial oxidative capacity;
- flexible intermediate phenotype.

### Type IIX
More glycolytic-biased:
- lower oxidative specialization on average;
- suited to rapid high-rate ATP turnover for brief intense contractions.

Critical boundary:

**MyHC identity and metabolic phenotype are correlated but not identical.**

Training can alter mitochondrial/proteomic/metabolic characteristics without requiring a complete MyHC-type conversion.

This matters because a fiber can become more oxidative while remaining classified by the same major MyHC isoform.

---

## 8. Fatigability differences

Average phenotype ordering:

`Type I = most fatigue resistant → Type IIA = intermediate → Type IIX = least fatigue resistant`

But fatigability is not a single immutable fiber-type constant.

It also depends on:

- activation frequency;
- contraction mode;
- duty cycle;
- Ca²⁺ handling;
- substrate availability;
- mitochondrial function;
- metabolite accumulation;
- temperature;
- prior fatigue;
- task/measurement endpoint.

Canonical rule:

**fiber type biases fatigue behavior; task and state determine observed fatigue.**

Detailed fatigue mechanisms remain PHY-10.

---

## 9. Hybrid fibers and phenotype continuum

Human fibers can co-express multiple MyHC isoforms, commonly including:

- I/IIA;
- IIA/IIX;
- less commonly broader hybrids.

Single-fiber methods detect hybrid states more reliably than bulk homogenate approaches.

Hybrid fibers often show contractile/metabolic properties intermediate between neighboring pure MyHC phenotypes.

Therefore the useful human sequence is better represented as a continuum:

`I → I/IIA → IIA → IIA/IIX → IIX`

rather than three perfectly isolated boxes.

Canonical rule:

**hybrid fibers are biologically meaningful states, not necessarily measurement error.**

They are particularly relevant during phenotype transition/remodeling.

Canonical boundary:

**PHY-03 classifies fiber phenotype; it does not authorize a fixed %1RM threshold for recruiting a fiber type.**

## 11. Training-induced phenotype plasticity

Adult human fiber phenotype is plastic.

Plasticity can occur at several levels:

1. MyHC isoform expression;
2. hybrid ↔ pure-fiber state;
3. oxidative/glycolytic enzyme profile;
4. mitochondrial proteome/content;
5. Ca²⁺ handling machinery;
6. fiber cross-sectional area;
7. broader fiber-specific proteome.

These levels do not need to change in lockstep.

### 11.1 IIx ↔ IIA transitions

The most reproducible exercise-associated MyHC shift in typical human training studies is movement away from a relatively untrained/IIx-rich phenotype toward more IIA expression.

Resistance training, endurance training and concurrent training can all reduce IIx/IIA-IIx hybrid representation in favor of IIA-dominant states depending on program and baseline.

A 2024 concurrent resistance + interval study observed reduced IIx expression in both sexes after 12 weeks.

Project rule:

**IIx → IIA is a common training-associated direction, not evidence that training makes muscle “slow.”**

IIA remains a fast phenotype.

### 11.2 Type I ↔ Type II transitions

Slow-fast conversion across the broader I ↔ II boundary is possible, but the size, direction and reproducibility of change are more context-dependent than IIx ↔ IIA shifts.

Evidence depends strongly on:

- training modality;
- duration;
- baseline phenotype;
- muscle sampled;
- measurement method;
- hybrid-fiber detection.

Canonical rule:

**do not promise large predictable I ↔ II conversion from ordinary resistance training.**

---

## 12. Resistance training and fiber phenotype

Resistance training can produce:

- hypertrophy in type I and type II fibers;
- fiber-specific proteomic remodeling;
- reduced IIx / increased IIA representation in many contexts;
- changes in metabolic/structural phenotype without necessarily changing major MyHC class.

Recent 2026 human proteomics found substantial fiber-type-specific remodeling after 8 weeks of resistance training, with greater numbers of regulated proteins in type II than type I fibers.

This is evidence of **fiber-specific adaptation**, not evidence that one fiber type is universally the only fiber that grows or matters.

Canonical rule:

**RT adaptation is fiber-type-specific in some molecular/structural respects, but both slow and fast fibers are trainable.**

---

## 13. Load-specific fiber hypertrophy

A 2026 systematic review/meta-regression examined whether resistance-training load produces reliably different hypertrophy by fiber type.

Eight studies / 195 participants were included.

Although exploratory estimates suggested possible load-dependent patterns, all reported pairwise fiber-type contrasts had 95% confidence intervals that included zero.

Project rule:

**current evidence does not justify choosing load zones solely to “target” type I vs type II hypertrophy.**

This does not mean load is irrelevant to strength or task specificity; those belong to PHY-08/PRO.

---

## 14. Training status

Cross-sectional athlete literature often shows:

- endurance athletes with greater type I representation;
- sprint/power athletes with greater fast-fiber representation.

But athlete phenotype reflects a mixture of:

- long-term training;
- genetic predisposition;
- sport selection/self-selection;
- muscle sampled;
- sex;
- age;
- prior activity history.

Therefore:

**athlete group differences are phenotype associations, not clean causal estimates of what training alone will convert in an arbitrary person.**

Longitudinal training evidence receives greater weight for causal plasticity claims.

---

## 15. Age differences

The 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis of young vs older adults found a more nuanced pattern than “aging converts all type II to type I.”

Main findings included:

- higher relative MyHC I protein expression with age;
- lower fast MyHC II / IIA relative expression;
- similar type I fiber size on average;
- smaller type II / IIA fiber size with age;
- fiber-type distributions were not uniformly different across age groups.

Canonical rule:

**aging prominently affects fast-fiber size and MyHC expression, but simple universal “II → I conversion” language overstates the evidence.**

Age-related denervation/reinnervation and sarcopenia mechanisms belong to later age/safety/individualization work.

---

## 16. Sex differences

The 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis included 6222 healthy adults from 156 studies.

Group-average findings:

- males had larger type I and type II fiber CSA;
- males showed somewhat greater type II distribution/proportional area;
- females showed somewhat greater type I distribution/proportional area.

Effects were moderated in part by age and muscle group.

Project interpretation:

1. these are population averages;
2. distributions overlap substantially at the individual level;
3. they do not create male/female-exclusive fiber types;
4. they do not justify sex-specific load prescriptions from fiber type alone;
5. fiber-specific molecular responses can differ by sex, but recent proteomic data are emerging rather than a complete prescription framework.

Canonical rule:

**sex is a modifier of group-average fiber phenotype, not an individual diagnostic label.**

---

## 17. Training status × age × sex interaction caution

The phenotype observed in one biopsy is the product of interacting influences:

`genetic background + muscle identity + activity/training history + age + sex + current physiology + measurement method`

No single modifier should be interpreted in isolation.

Examples of prohibited inference:

- “woman → type I dominant”;
- “man → type II dominant”;
- “older adult → all fibers converted to type I”;
- “power athlete → training caused all type II abundance”;
- “high type II estimate → must train heavy.”

These are overgeneralizations.

---

## 18. Measurement limitations

Fiber-type composition can be expressed as:

- percentage of fiber count;
- proportional cross-sectional area;
- MyHC protein abundance;
- single-fiber phenotype;
- histochemical/immunohistochemical classes.

These are related but not identical metrics.

Sampling limitations:

- biopsy samples only a small region;
- fiber composition varies by muscle and location;
- hybrid fibers can be missed depending on method;
- bulk protein measurements lose single-fiber information.

Project rule:

**“fiber-type percentage” is not one universal measurement; always check method and denominator.**

## 19. Noninvasive estimation boundary

A 2024 survey of high-performance practitioners showed substantial use/interest in noninvasive estimates such as jump tests and practitioner judgment.

The authors explicitly noted that some commonly used noninvasive estimation methods lack scientific validity.

Project rule:

**do not classify Ilya's fiber composition from performance anecdotes, jump tests, repetition tests or visual impressions as if this were biopsy-confirmed biology.**

If fiber typing ever becomes decision-critical, measurement validity must be addressed in TST/IND.

## 21. Established project-level model

Human skeletal-muscle fiber phenotype is represented as:

`contractile MyHC identity + hybrid state + metabolic machinery + Ca²⁺ handling + fiber size + proteomic state + activity history`

with a useful but simplified contractile continuum:

`I → I/IIA → IIA → IIA/IIX → IIX`

Training can change:

- MyHC distribution;
- hybrid state;
- oxidative/glycolytic machinery;
- fiber size;
- fiber-specific proteome

without requiring all components to shift together.

Confidence: **HIGH for the phenotype continuum and plasticity principle; MODERATE for exact magnitude/direction of specific slow-fast conversions under a given training prescription.**

## 22. Training-relevant implications

1. **Type I and type II fibers are both trainable.**
2. **IIx → IIA shift is common with repeated training and does not mean loss of all fast capability.**
3. **MyHC type does not completely define metabolic phenotype.**
4. **Fiber type does not uniquely determine whole-muscle force, power or fatigue.**
5. **Training status influences phenotype, but cross-sectional athlete data contain selection/genetic confounding.**
6. **Age and sex modify group-average phenotype but cannot classify an individual.**
7. **No robust evidence supports prescribing load solely from presumed fiber type.**
8. **Recruitment questions must be answered in NEU, not inferred from the fiber label.**
9. **Noninvasive guessed fiber typing is not an adequate basis for personal prescription.**
