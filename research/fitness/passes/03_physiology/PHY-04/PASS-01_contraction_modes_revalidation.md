## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a bounded physiological model of concentric, eccentric, isometric and isokinetic muscle actions sufficient to interpret later evidence on:

- strength;
- hypertrophy;
- power;
- fatigue;
- muscle damage / DOMS;
- exercise selection;
- resistance-training programming;
- stretch-shortening-cycle use.

The model must avoid:

1. treating concentric/eccentric/isometric as simple labels detached from muscle length change;
2. assuming external joint motion exactly equals fascicle behavior;
3. interpreting isokinetic as a natural free-weight mode rather than an apparatus-controlled condition;
4. concluding “eccentric = always better” from higher force capacity;
5. concluding “eccentric = always more damaging” regardless of dose/training status/repeated-bout effect;
6. treating acute metabolic cost as chronic adaptation;
7. ignoring mode-specificity of strength adaptation;
8. claiming isometric training transfers equally across all joint angles/tasks;
9. treating stretch-shortening-cycle performance as purely a contraction-mode phenomenon;
10. collapsing force–length / force–velocity physiology into a universal programming rule.

### Question type

Mechanism / physiology / training adaptation.

### Critical output

A project model of:

`muscle length behavior + force capacity + velocity + metabolic cost + fatigue + damage + adaptation specificity`

that can support later PHY-05/06/07/08/09/10, MEC, TEC and PRO.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 10 exact Section 4 items must be covered at content level; force–length and force–velocity foundations must be explicit; acute and chronic effects must remain separated; no project-relevant content blocker should remain before separate closure audit/synthesis.

## 3. Terminology: muscle action vs external movement

For this project:

### Concentric
An **active muscle/fascicle shortening action** while producing force.

### Eccentric
An **active muscle/fascicle lengthening action** while producing force.

### Isometric
An action with **no intended macroscopic change in muscle-tendon unit/joint position** at the task level.

Important nuance:

**an externally isometric joint task does not guarantee every fascicle, tendon and aponeurosis remains exactly the same length internally.**

Tendon/aponeurosis compliance and fascicle behavior can redistribute length inside the muscle-tendon unit.

Therefore:

**concentric/eccentric/isometric labels are task-level simplifications tied to active length behavior, not perfect descriptions of every internal structure.**

---

## 4. Concentric action

During concentric action:

- activated muscle shortens against an external load;
- shortening velocity rises as relative load/force demand falls;
- at a given activation state, maximal force capacity generally declines as shortening velocity increases.

Mechanical role:

**positive mechanical work** is produced by the active muscle-tendon system.

Typical training examples:

- rising phase of a curl or squat when the target agonist shortens;
- acceleration phase of a press.

Project rule:

**concentric does not mean “lifting phase” universally; the label depends on what the active target muscle is doing.**

---

## 5. Eccentric action

During eccentric action:

- activated muscle lengthens while resisting/absorbing external mechanical energy;
- force capacity can exceed maximal concentric force and can exceed isometric force under many controlled conditions;
- metabolic cost per unit force/work is lower than during concentric action;
- force generation involves cross-bridge mechanics plus history-dependent/passive structural contributions, including titin-related mechanisms.

Mechanical role:

**negative muscle work / energy absorption.**

Project rule:

**eccentric = active lengthening, not passive stretching.**

---

## 6. Isometric action

During isometric action:

- external joint angle / muscle-tendon unit length is intentionally held approximately constant;
- force is produced without gross external shortening/lengthening movement;
- internal fascicle/tendon behavior may still change.

Isometric training is highly useful for:

- angle/task-specific force production;
- situations where movement is constrained;
- testing maximal voluntary force.

Current 2025 meta-analysis found greater gains in isometric strength after isometric training than dynamic training, while isokinetic strength gains were not clearly different.

Canonical rule:

**isometric strength is trainable and strongly task-specific; transfer exists but is incomplete.**

---

## 7. Isokinetic category

Isokinetic exercise is an **apparatus-controlled dynamic condition** in which joint angular velocity is held approximately constant by a dynamometer while resistance varies to match applied torque.

Project boundary:

1. it can include concentric or eccentric muscle actions;
2. it is not a separate sarcomeric contraction mechanism;
3. constant joint angular velocity does not guarantee constant fascicle shortening/lengthening velocity;
4. it is especially useful for controlled testing/research and some rehabilitation/performance settings.

Canonical rule:

**“isokinetic” describes the control condition of movement velocity, not a fourth fundamental contractile mechanism.**

---

## 8. Force–velocity relationship

### Concentric branch

As shortening velocity increases, maximal force capability generally decreases.

Practical implication:

- very heavy loads move slowly because required force is high;
- high shortening velocities are achievable only at lower external resistance.

### Isometric point

At zero macroscopic shortening/lengthening velocity, the system is at the isometric point of the force–velocity relation.

### Eccentric branch

During active lengthening, force can exceed isometric force, but the exact eccentric force–velocity curve is more complex and less standardized than the concentric branch.

Recent 2026 work emphasizes that:

- starting length;
- temperature;
- cross-bridge state;
- titin/serial elastic behavior

can materially alter measured eccentric force–velocity behavior.

Canonical rule:

**force–velocity is mode-dependent; do not model eccentric force as a simple mirror image of concentric force.**

---

## 9. Force–length relationship

Active force depends on muscle/sarcomere length.

Relevant components:

1. actin–myosin overlap / cross-bridge geometry;
2. passive elastic structures;
3. titin-related activation-dependent effects;
4. architecture and tendon compliance at whole-muscle level.

Consequences:

- isometric force depends on joint/muscle length;
- eccentric force can be influenced by starting length and stretch history;
- concentric output also depends on the length region from which shortening begins.

Canonical rule:

**contraction mode cannot be interpreted independently of muscle length.**

Detailed joint torque, moment arms and external exercise geometry remain MEC.

---

## 10. Force-production differences by mode

Controlled physiology broadly supports:

`eccentric force capacity ≥ isometric force capacity > concentric force capacity`

under many matched maximal conditions.

But this hierarchy is not a universal fixed ratio.

It depends on:

- velocity;
- muscle length;
- activation;
- familiarization;
- apparatus;
- task;
- neural inhibition;
- training status;
- measurement method.

Current training evidence adds an important distinction:

**higher eccentric force capacity does not automatically translate into universally superior chronic strength or hypertrophy.**

Mode-specific strength adaptations are substantial.

A 2025 meta-analysis found eccentric-only training superior for eccentric strength, but similar concentric and isometric strength changes compared with concentric-only training.

---

## 11. Metabolic cost differences

At the same mechanical output or comparable force demand, eccentric work generally has:

- lower oxygen consumption;
- lower ATP turnover;
- lower activation requirement per unit force;
- lower energetic cost

than concentric work.

This is one of the most robust physiological distinctions between lengthening and shortening actions.

However:

**lower metabolic cost per unit eccentric force does not mean every eccentric training session creates low systemic fatigue.**

Session-level cost still depends on:

- total work;
- loading;
- volume;
- tempo;
- rest;
- novelty;
- muscle damage;
- exercise selection.

Slow/long eccentric tempos can increase time under tension, lactate and perceived effort even though the intrinsic force-cost relationship remains favorable.

---

## 12. Fatigue differences

Fatigue is mode- and protocol-dependent.

Controlled work generally shows:

- concentric work can produce greater metabolic/peripheral fatigue for a given mechanical output;
- eccentric work can sustain high force with lower metabolic demand;
- eccentric exercise can still create substantial neuromuscular performance loss and prolonged recovery when loading is high/novel.

Therefore:

**“eccentric is less metabolically fatiguing” ≠ “eccentric never causes more recovery cost.”**

Acute fatigue must be separated into:

- metabolic/peripheral fatigue;
- neural/neuromuscular fatigue;
- mechanical performance loss;
- delayed damage-related loss.

Detailed mechanisms remain PHY-10.

---

## 13. Muscle damage and DOMS

Novel or high-strain eccentric exercise is strongly associated with:

- larger delayed soreness;
- strength loss;
- swelling;
- creatine-kinase elevations;
- structural stress

in many protocols.

But the relationship is not:

`eccentric action → inevitable severe damage`

because magnitude depends on:

- novelty;
- load;
- muscle length/strain;
- volume;
- velocity/tempo;
- training status;
- prior exposure.

Repeated exposure produces a robust **repeated-bout effect**, reducing later:

- strength loss;
- DOMS;
- CK response.

Current 2026 meta-analysis supports substantial RBE protection across these outcomes.

Canonical rule:

**eccentric damage/DOMS is exposure-dependent and rapidly conditioned; DOMS is not a required marker of effective eccentric training.**

---

## 14. Long-term strength adaptation

Strength adaptation shows substantial **mode/task specificity**.

Current evidence:

1. eccentric-only training tends to improve eccentric strength more than concentric-only training;
2. concentric strength can improve after both modes;
3. isometric strength can improve after dynamic training, but transfer is smaller than task-specific dynamic gains;
4. isometric training produces especially strong isometric gains;
5. isokinetic test outcomes are influenced by test velocity/mode.

Canonical rule:

**the tested contraction mode is part of the strength outcome.**

“Strength” must not be treated as one context-free quantity.

---

## 15. Long-term hypertrophy adaptation

Evidence does not support a simple universal hierarchy:

`eccentric > concentric > isometric`

for hypertrophy.

### Eccentric vs concentric

The 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis in apparently healthy adults found no statistically clear overall hypertrophy difference between eccentric and concentric training, with substantial heterogeneity and very-low GRADE certainty.

The 2026 ACSM overview reports eccentric overload as one variable that can enhance hypertrophy across the review literature, but this should not be interpreted as proving universal eccentric-only superiority.

The 2026 accentuated-eccentric-loading meta-analysis found no clear superiority over constant-load RT for muscle cross-sectional area.

### Isometric

Isometric training can produce hypertrophy, especially when trained at longer muscle lengths and with sufficient effort/dose.

A 2025 resistance-trained within-subject trial found broadly similar quadriceps hypertrophy between long-length isometric and full-ROM isotonic training over six weeks, with uncertainty in regional differences.

Canonical rule:

**all major resistance modes can support hypertrophy; mode interacts with loading, muscle length, volume and task, and no universal mode-only hypertrophy ranking is justified.**

---

## 16. Architectural adaptation

Contraction modes may bias structural adaptation.

Examples reported across studies include differences in:

- fascicle length;
- pennation angle;
- regional hypertrophy.

However:

- direction and magnitude depend on protocol;
- recent AEL meta-analysis did not show clear superiority for fascicle length/angle;
- mode and loading strategy are often confounded.

Canonical rule:

**architectural adaptation can be mode-sensitive, but it is not sufficiently uniform to infer a simple fixed “eccentric = fascicle length, concentric = pennation” prescription rule.**

---

## 17. Isometric transfer / joint-angle specificity

Isometric training is specific to:

- contraction type;
- tested task;
- muscle/joint length.

Transfer to nearby joint angles and dynamic strength occurs, but is not complete.

Modern systematic evidence shows:

- strong isometric strength gains;
- dynamic/isokinetic transfer can occur;
- dynamic training improves dynamic strength substantially more than untrained isometric strength.

Canonical rule:

**isometric training is neither “only useful at one exact angle” nor universally transferable across the full ROM/task.**

Exact angle prescription belongs to TEC/PRO after MEC.

---

## 18. Stretch-shortening cycle boundary

A stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) combines:

1. active/preactivated lengthening;
2. rapid transition/amortization;
3. subsequent shortening.

Performance enhancement can involve:

- elastic energy storage/recovery;
- preactivation;
- reflex/neural contributions;
- muscle-tendon interaction;
- history-dependent contractile effects such as residual force enhancement.

Current 2026 review argues that history-dependent muscle properties may contribute substantially alongside classical elastic/neural explanations.

Project boundary:

**SSC is not “eccentric followed by concentric” in a purely descriptive sense; its performance effect is an integrated muscle-tendon-neural phenomenon.**

Detailed mechanics → MEC.  
Detailed reflex/preactivation/neural control → NEU.  
Programming/use → TEC/PRO.

---

## 19. Acute response ≠ chronic adaptation

Critical project safeguard:

- higher eccentric force;
- lower eccentric oxygen cost;
- greater soreness;
- higher CK;
- more lactate;
- greater acute EMG;
- slower eccentric tempo

do **not** individually prove superior long-term hypertrophy or strength.

Current 2026 AEL synthesis illustrates this directly:

- some acute variables increased;
- chronic strength/architecture outcomes were often similar.

Canonical rule:

**contraction-mode physiology constrains interpretation but does not replace longitudinal training evidence.**

## 21. Established project-level model

### Concentric
`active shortening → positive work → lower maximal force capacity at faster shortening velocities → relatively higher metabolic cost per unit force/work`

### Isometric
`active force with no intended gross length change → zero external shortening velocity → strong task/angle specificity`

### Eccentric
`active lengthening → negative work/energy absorption → high force capacity → lower intrinsic metabolic cost per unit force → greater novelty-dependent damage potential`

### Isokinetic
`apparatus-controlled constant joint angular velocity` applied to dynamic concentric/eccentric actions.

Confidence:

- **HIGH** for definitions, force/cost distinctions and mode specificity;
- **MODERATE-HIGH** for broad chronic strength conclusions;
- **MODERATE** for universal hypertrophy/architecture superiority claims because protocols and evidence remain heterogeneous.

---

## 22. Training-relevant implications

1. **Eccentric force capacity is high, but higher force does not automatically mean better chronic adaptation.**
2. **Eccentric work is metabolically economical per unit force, but high-load/novel eccentric sessions can still have large recovery cost.**
3. **Concentric performance is constrained strongly by the force–velocity relation.**
4. **Isometric training can meaningfully improve strength, especially in the trained/tested contraction mode.**
5. **Strength adaptations are mode/task specific; outcome testing must match the claim.**
6. **DOMS is not a quality metric for eccentric training.**
7. **Repeated eccentric exposure reduces later damage response.**
8. **All three major modes can contribute to hypertrophy; no simple universal winner is established.**
9. **Isokinetic testing is useful but should not be confused with free-weight/isoinertial training mechanics.**
10. **SSC effects belong to integrated muscle-tendon-neural physiology, not contraction mode alone.**

## 24. Confidence / unresolved issues

### HIGH

- concentric = active shortening;
- eccentric = active lengthening;
- isometric = no intended gross length change;
- isokinetic = apparatus-controlled constant joint angular velocity;
- eccentric force capacity generally exceeds concentric;
- eccentric metabolic cost per unit force/work is lower;
- mode/task specificity of strength;
- novel eccentric loading can increase EIMD/DOMS;
- repeated-bout protection;
- isometric training can substantially increase isometric strength.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- exact eccentric vs concentric hypertrophy superiority;
- exact architectural adaptation by mode;
- exact fatigue ranking across whole sessions;
- exact transfer of isometric training across joint angles/dynamic tasks;
- exact magnitude of SSC contributions from elastic, neural and history-dependent contractile mechanisms.

### Current non-blocking uncertainty

No evidence supports a universal rule that one contraction mode is globally superior for hypertrophy, strength, fatigue management and power simultaneously.

That uncertainty should remain explicit.
