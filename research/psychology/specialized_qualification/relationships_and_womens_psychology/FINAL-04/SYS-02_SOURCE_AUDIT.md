## 3. Scientific clarifications

R1 — Objectives and choice-set size. Keeney distinguishes values/ends from the alternatives generated to realize them. Frederick et al. review divergent discount-rate estimates; a single immediate choice does not identify time preference independently of constraints. The 2010 choice-overload synthesis reports a near-zero mean with heterogeneous effects; the 2015 synthesis identifies complexity, task difficulty, preference uncertainty and decision goal as moderators. Different study inclusion, overlap and modeling prevent treating the two syntheses as independent contradictory experiments. Null mean does not prove no overload; moderator-adjusted associations do not establish that all choice sets should be shortened. No restriction of another person's autonomous alternatives.

R2 — Opportunity cost and sunk cost. Maguire direct Results/Discussion confirms d=.22 overall and d=.16 [.10,.22] after excluding Frederick et al. 2009; reduced log OR .29 [.177,.40]. Exp(.29)=1.336, consistent with reported point OR/rate change 1.34. However exp(.40)=1.492, not reported upper transformed bound 1.70, and exp(.177)=1.194 versus reported lower 1.21. Unless a different interval construction is documented, the printed transformed CI cannot be recovered by exponentiating the stated log-OR endpoints. Retain both reported quantities and flag mismatch; do not treat this as corrected source data. The reported exclusion also changes funnel-asymmetry tests; this is a sensitivity analysis, not proof all publication bias was removed. Much evidence from hypothetical/consumer tasks; no personalized bias magnitude. Roth's utilization/progress distinction cautions against treating escalation as a unitary effect. Unrecoverable costs are distinct from current assets, obligations, learning, switching costs and feasible future payoffs produced by previous investments. Continued effort can be rational; stopping can be rational; compare forward consequences rather than mechanically favor one.

R3 — Decision quality, realized outcome and reversibility. Aiyer's preregistered replication supports outcome-dependent evaluation within its hypothetical medical task; the different design and much larger sample preclude direct comparison of effect magnitudes with the original experiment without attention to estimands. Evaluate ex ante information/alternatives separately from learning after outcomes. Bao direct abstract explicitly describes an unedited manuscript; an undo option shortened initial deliberation and improved final task performance. Indexed results for redo versus do sequences report IRR .75 [.71,.79], a task-specific contrast, not a standardized welfare effect or general causal mechanism. Gilbert/Ebert report lower satisfaction in a changeable-choice condition despite preference for the opportunity to revise. This is not a contradiction to Bao: satisfaction and objective performance are different endpoints and tasks. Reversibility is not costless and does not remove lasting consequences; preserve the right to revise or refuse, distinguish voluntary choice from whether undo is feasible.

R4 — Deep uncertainty and criterion dependence. Lempert's framework uses plausible futures to examine strategy vulnerabilities; scenario counts are not probabilities. Anderson/Zachary show minimax decisions can be sensitive to extreme scenarios and regret to the feasible menu; their abstract supports the caution but full proofs were not audited. An explicit hard constraint is different from an analyst-invented weighting. Test whether the decision survives plausible changes to assumptions and available alternatives. New synthetic option-set counterexample below verifies one generic mechanism independently of the article.

R5 — Information value is conditional. ISPOR reports distinguish research value and cost, using specified uncertain states, available actions, utilities and probabilities. Cost-free optional signals have nonnegative gross decision value under coherent fixed decision assumptions because the decision-maker can ignore them. The assertion does not cover attention costs, delay, new constraints, misinformation, behavioral responses or intrinsic benefits of information. Information that leaves the same action optimal has zero instrumental gross value in the fixed model, yet may matter to other explicit objectives. Net value subtracts acquisition and associated costs; distinguish a single test from a sequential policy when signals are complementary. No probabilities, preference weights or personal values inferred from generic studies.

R6 — Search adaptation and stopping. Spektor/Wulff indexed original record supports multiple sequential drivers of information search; task sampling decisions are dependent within participants. No claim that more search is necessarily better or that these lottery-task processes explain specific individuals. Hu indexed original abstract confirms association with metacognitive sensitivity and absence of detected improvement in sensitivity from search training; this is not proof all training is ineffective or that a predictor has a uniquely identified causal mechanism. McCaughey direct abstract reports N=67,79,131,160,318 in five experiments; sum 755 is sum of reported experiment Ns, not independently verified unique participants. Direct Methods clarify Experiment 2 recruited 148, whereas abstract analyzes/reports 131; do not conflate recruitment with analytic N. All groups received feedback in block 1, only assigned groups continued feedback in later blocks; the relevant finding concerns additional external feedback after initial familiarization, not never receiving feedback. Discussion identifies internal monitoring and prior planning as candidate explanations, not independently identified mechanisms. Experiments 3a/b did not establish a universal null effect of feedback. Monetary sampling paradigm, known changes and finite horizons restrict transfer. Stop/continue is a comparison of admissible actions and expected/conditional consequences, not a fixed universal duration or number of observations.

R7 — Preference clarification and decision-aid endpoints. Stacey official abstract confirms 209 randomized studies, 107698 participants, values-choice congruence RR 1.75 [1.44,2.13] in 21 studies/9377 participants; decision-regret MD -1.23 [-3.05,.59] in 22 studies/3707 participants. No significant regret difference is not exact equivalence. Witteman 2021 original abstract confirms 33 articles, 43 methods, risk difference for values-incongruent choices -.04 [-.06,-.02], conflict SMD -.20 [-.29,-.11]. Only 14/43 trials contribute complete conflict data; selective measurement/availability and overlap with Cochrane review constrain interpretation. Final published source labels this effect decisional conflict, not regret (a discrepant preprint abstract wording is not the controlling version). Subgroup findings for tradeoff requirement do not establish superiority of compulsory tradeoffs; measurement of congruence may partly depend on how method and outcome are defined. Witteman 2020 reports six sample sizes 1346,456,840,1178,841,2033 (sum 6694 reported observations across experiments, no cross-study identity audit), and differentiated effects by method; less decisional conflict does not necessarily improve values alignment. The 2015 factorial trial concerns vaccination intentions and not verified uptake; E2 Table 3 reports no congruence benefit from the tested clarification component. The heart-disease prevention trial reports no detected incremental benefit; original discussion gives some evidence against a simple floor-effect explanation, so floor effects remain a hypothesis rather than established cause. These trials are included in E2 and cannot be counted again as independent syntheses. Slovic's preference-construction concept cautions against treating elicited preferences as invariant measurements. Health and hypothetical tasks do not validate personal decision aids outside those domains. Preserve the decision-maker's stated priorities and distinguish satisfaction, regret, confidence, conflict, values alignment and realized outcomes.

## 4. Mathematical checks

All examples are abstract invented data, not empirical or individualized.
- SYS-02.1 scores A=(8,3), B=(6,7), C=(5,4). B dominates C; A/B equal when 3+5w=7-w, w=2/3. w=.5 gives 5.5,6.5,4.5; w=.8 gives 7,6.2,4.8. Verified using JS; assumes additive utility and commensurable scales, not generally guaranteed.
- SYS-02.2 equiprobable payoffs A=(10,-2), B=(4,4). Immediate max expectation 4, perfect information value 7, EVPI 3. Symmetric .8 signal: favorable A expectation 7.6, unfavorable A expectation .4 vs B=4; EVSI 1.8. Costs 1 or 2 yield net .8 and -.2. Verified by arithmetic.
- A=(12,0,0), B=(5,5,5), C=(8,3,3): worst utilities 0,5,3 and max regrets 5,7,4. Maximin selects B; minimax regret C. No scenario likelihood implied.
- New regret-menu check: A=(10,0), B=(4,4), max regrets (4,6), A selected. Adding C=(0,12) changes scenario maxima from (10,4) to (10,12); regrets (12,8,10), B selected although C is not selected. Verified enumeration. This illustrates option-set dependence, not proof minimax regret is always unsuitable.
- SYS-02.3 parity example: immediate expected value .5, observing one bit only .5, both 1. With each observation costing .1, two-step net .8, gain .3 relative to stopping now. One-step comparison incorrectly favors stopping if it ignores subsequent complementary information. This is a finite counterexample to myopic stopping under stipulated costs, not an argument for indefinite search.
- Other checks: log-OR conversion exp(.29), exp(.177), exp(.40) and sample-size sums above. No replication of original analyses claimed.

## 5. Operational rules

Define the actual decision, fundamental and intermediate goals, horizon, hard constraints and feasible actions before choosing strategy. Separate one's own available conduct from others' voluntary choices. Compare relevant forward consequences, alternatives displaced, reversibility and future options. Retain uncertainty about probabilities, model structure and preferences; avoid numerical precision without justified inputs. If ranking changes under plausible tradeoffs, explain conditional ranking rather than substituting analyst priorities. Evaluate decision-process quality separately from the outcome and update on new facts. Compare act now, legitimate provisional action, wait, stop or revise; assess acquisition and waiting costs and whether information changes a feasible decision or serves another explicit goal. Respect consent, privacy, safety; no intrusive monitoring, engineered tests or pressure. Review triggers must be grounded in meaningful observations, actual costs and feasibility, not arbitrary universal deadlines.
