E1. Keysar, Barr, Balin & Brauner (2000), Taking perspective in conversation: the role of mutual knowledge in comprehension. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00211 ; https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/1467-9280.00211/ . Full abstract directly inspected on APS site. Two experiments used eye tracking during object-manipulation instructions. Listeners considered referents unavailable to the speaker, so shared knowledge was not always used to exclude inappropriate alternatives at initial interpretation. Supports a possible egocentric interpretation process, not a universal inability to take perspective. Sample sizes, timing estimates and full methods not checked. Task comprehension is not equivalent to inferring a romantic motive.

E2. Hanna, Tanenhaus & Trueswell (2003), The effects of common ground and perspective on domains of referential interpretation. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749596X03000226 . Indexed publisher abstract excerpt inspected; direct 403. Experimental results indicated immediate effects of common ground and perspective on reference resolution, despite interference from privileged information. Counterweight to a universal sequential egocentric-first model. Different task conditions and measures may explain apparent disagreement with E1. Exact sample, estimates and task contrasts not verified. Do not infer a single processing sequence for all conversations.

E3. Kruger, Epley, Parker & Ng (2005), Egocentrism over e-mail: can we communicate as well as we think? https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.89.6.925 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16393025/ . Indexed author abstract and original paper excerpts; direct PubMed CAPTCHA and DOI cache miss. Five experiments reported overestimation of success in conveying intended tone via email; later experiments linked overconfidence to perspective-taking difficulty. Distinguish subjective certainty from measured success; reduced nonverbal cues can alter decoding. No exact rates or sample totals verified; historical email task does not establish modern messenger error rates, universal negative bias or absence of meaningful digital communication.

E4. Rules for Mediated Romance: A Digital Exploration of How Couples Negotiate Expectations (2022). https://doi.org/10.1093/jcmc/zmac007 ; https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/27/3/zmac007/6567657 . Direct publisher methods, selected results and discussion inspected. Semistructured joint interviews with 18 committed couples (36 adults aged 19–42); convenience and snowball recruitment; interviews conducted 2018–2019. Qualitative themes distinguish explicit agreements from implicit expectations, changing norms of channel use and reply timing, and contextual appraisal of deviations. Joint interviews and retrospective accounts may underrepresent disagreement, coercion or unreported motives. Not a population estimate, causal test or normative response-time threshold. Do not treat a unilateral expectation as a mutually accepted obligation.

E5. Steinebach, Stein & Schnell (2025), Messenger-based assessment of empathic accuracy in couples' smartphone communication. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-025-02483-9 ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40359-025-02483-9 . Publisher abstract and selected discussion/limitations directly inspected; indexed methods excerpt. Preregistered ecological messenger-based assessment, 102 participants/51 couples; reported 57% dropout and relaxed inclusion threshold. Both tracking of partner-reported affect and assumed-similarity bias were detected. Greater messaging frequency was associated with better arousal tracking, not valence tracking; experience with messenger use was associated with stronger reliance on own valence. These small moderation findings are weak evidence given low power, multiple tests without correction and restricted cultural sample. Criterion is partner self-report, not direct access to mental state. Neither frequent messaging nor experience establishes accurate reading of motives. No intervention effect or full coefficient audit.

E6. Lavner, Karney & Bradbury (2016), Does couples' communication predict marital satisfaction, or does marital satisfaction predict communication? https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12301 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4852543/ . Indexed original abstract and method/discussion extracts; direct PMC CAPTCHA and UC repository blocked. 431 low-income, ethnically diverse newlywed couples initially assessed, four assessments at nine-month intervals, observed communication and self-reported satisfaction. Concurrent links were more consistent than prospective links; satisfaction more consistently predicted subsequent communication than the reverse in reported analyses. Does not establish satisfaction as a universal cause or exclude behavior effects. Exact model sample, attrition, coefficients and confidence intervals not checked. Possible shared cohort with REL-X03.1 VSA sources; do not count as independent replication of those analyses.

E7. Johnson et al. (2022 issue), Within-couple associations between communication and relationship satisfaction over time. https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672211016920 ; https://ucrisportal.univie.ac.at/en/publications/within-couple-associations-between-communication-and-relationship/ . Complete author abstract directly inspected at institutional repository; indexed publisher discussion extracts. Across three longitudinal studies, within-person deviations in negative communication were consistently associated with concurrent satisfaction deviations, but lagged associations in either direction were inconsistent across studies. Positive communication rarely showed within-person associations with satisfaction. Counterevidence to a general communication-first causal explanation and to equating between-person findings with change within a couple. Null paths are not equivalence; measurement, intervals, time-varying confounders, shared functioning and selection may matter. Sample sizes, exact models, effect precision and possible overlap with E6 not independently verified. No intervention inference.

## 3. Construct and sequence model
Distinguish the exact observable message (including channel and surrounding turns), semantic content, pragmatic implication in a particular context, communicative act (e.g., question, request, acknowledgment, proposal, refusal), intended meaning, underlying motive, recipient interpretation, emotional effect and subsequent action. Literal content can be clear while pragmatic force is ambiguous. A conventional implication is not necessarily a speaker's private intention. Acknowledgment is not agreement; understanding is not validation by definition; validation does not require acceptance of another person's factual claims or a request. A message can have several functions simultaneously. Do not treat a speculative implicature as stronger evidence than a clear explicit choice.

Candidate sequence: context and goals -> speaker's utterance -> recipient's interpretation given actual available cues and expectations -> recipient response -> changed context for the next turn. This is an analytic representation, not an identified causal graph. Common ground concerns what is shared or believed shared; distinguish actual shared information, each person's assumptions and privately known information. Channel affordances (timing, text, prosody, visible expressions, opportunity to clarify) influence available cues but do not establish a sender's motive. Absence of a cue is not necessarily an intentional omission. Silence, delay, message length and frequency are behaviors or metadata, not direct measurements of affection, interest, deception or commitment. Consider channel access, workload, conversational norms, topic, message complexity, explicit agreement, baseline behavior, preference and recipient appraisal.

Separate tracking accuracy (association of judgments with variation in target reports), level bias, assumed similarity and actual similarity. Accuracy and bias can coexist; a correct inference may arise through shared state rather than independent detection. Target report is a criterion with limitations, not an infallible measure. Communication quality, quantity, perceived responsiveness, relationship quality and actual relationship choice are distinct. At a group level, association across couples can reflect stable differences; within-couple concurrent covariance can reflect shared causes; prospective prediction may depend on lag and outcome. None alone establishes a mechanism or individual future trajectory.

## 4. Operational inference procedure
1. Define the question, speaker, recipient, target relationship, context, time window and evidence channel. Preserve exact wording and relevant preceding/following turns when legitimately available; distinguish transcript from paraphrase or reconstruction.
2. Separate explicit proposition/act, plausible contextual implications, recipient experience, and hypothesized motives. Do not collapse these into a single narrative. An explicit boundary or refusal remains actionable even if pragmatic nuances are uncertain.
3. Generate competing meanings that fit the evidence, including ordinary literal interpretation, difference in assumed knowledge, channel ambiguity, practical constraints, differences in expected communication and genuine preference. Do not assume ambiguity proves concealed intent.
4. Compare interpretations against subsequent freely expressed clarification, conversational uptake and repeated ordinary observations in comparable situations. Differentiate an unacknowledged message from evidence of intentional avoidance. If rival interpretations predict the same observations, do not claim discrimination. Missing context stays unknown.
5. Assess reporter and channel effects, baseline, temporal ordering, actor/partner/shared influences and estimand. A response-time norm is not universal; mutually accepted expectations differ from unilateral demands. Correlation and prediction are not causation.
6. Prefer straightforward, respectful clarification when needed and welcome; do not pressure for replies, covertly monitor, manufacture ambiguity or exploit uncertainty. Acknowledgment and understanding do not imply consent, agreement or willingness to alter a relationship.
7. Revise only the supported claim, preserve alternate explanations and uncertainty, and avoid invented numeric probabilities. No diagnosis or claim of reading unexpressed mental states. Conflict patterns and breach-specific trust repair require separate evidence.

## 5. Internal synthetic transfer checks
Rubric fixed before review: construct separation, competing explanation, distinguishing evidence, inference boundary. Critical errors: unsupported motive/diagnosis, deterministic transfer, causal inflation, ignoring contrary evidence, coercion or private-data exposure. Six self-authored neutral examples, not independent or held-out.

A. In a shared workspace a request to move a folder refers to an object the listener cannot identify. Response: semantic reference and shared knowledge differ; multiple referents and unequal visual access compete. Ordinary clarification can resolve the referent. No inference of evasiveness.
B. A colleague reads a brief congratulatory text as sarcasm. Response: intended tone and received tone are distinct; ambiguity, prior expectations and missing prosody may contribute. Voluntary clarification and context can discriminate. No claim of hostility solely from tone impression.
C. A recreational group member replies several hours later than another expects. Response: actual delay, personal expectation and explicit agreement differ; access, competing activity and preference may explain timing. Compare established norms and volunteered context. No automatic disrespect inference.
D. A participant accurately judges a partner's tension when both experience the same external problem. Response: direct tracking and shared-state inference differ; actual similarity can improve apparent accuracy. Compare independent target reports across differing ordinary circumstances. No mind-reading claim.
E. A panel study finds lower negative communication and higher satisfaction at the same wave, but no consistent subsequent prediction. Response: concurrent covariance and lagged change differ; shared context, reverse direction and measurement may explain association. Compare matched within-person models and precision. No causal conclusion.
F. A listener accurately summarizes a proposed plan but declines participation. Response: comprehension, acknowledgment, endorsement and choice differ; constraints and preference remain possible. Respect explicit choice; further information only if freely offered. No hidden agreement inferred.
