# BHC-02 — Psychological intervention evidence architecture and BHC sufficiency audit: psychotherapy families, common/specific factors, alliance/expectancy/provider effects, comparative efficacy, harms, preferences, matching, and school-level limits

Status: **COMPLETE_FOR_CURRENT_SCOPE — 20/20 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Close the remaining BHC qualification dependency after BHC-01.

BHC-01 established how any intervention result must be interpreted:

- efficacy != effectiveness != implementation;
- package != component != mechanism;
- mediator != mechanism;
- moderator != mediator;
- fidelity, receipt, enactment and adherence are distinct;
- maintenance and sustainment are separate;
- average effect != individual treatment effect.

BHC-02 asks a different question:

> What does the current evidence actually justify about psychological intervention families, psychotherapy schools, common and specific factors, therapist/alliance/expectancy effects, comparative efficacy, harms, preferences, and treatment matching?

The goal is not to learn a therapy-school catalogue or treatment manual.

The goal is to prevent:

- therapy-brand reification;
- school loyalty replacing evidence;
- "all therapies are equal" as a universal law;
- "common factors explain everything";
- "specific techniques explain everything";
- alliance correlation being treated as causal proof;
- therapist effects being treated as fixed therapist traits;
- expectancy effects being treated as placebo-only noise or a universal mechanism;
- small average school differences being converted into no-difference claims;
- one disorder's comparative ranking being generalized to all disorders;
- preferred therapy being treated as necessarily more effective;
- algorithmic matching being treated as validated personalized care without prospective evidence;
- absence of reported harms being treated as safety.

Canonical rule:

`clinical target -> evidence-supported intervention families -> comparator -> package/component specification -> common/specific process evidence -> therapist/alliance/context -> preference/feasibility -> harms -> heterogeneity -> calibrated choice claim`.

Never:

`therapy school -> mechanism truth`

or:

`similar average outcomes -> mechanisms are the same`.

# II. What psychotherapy-family labels mean

## 12. Therapy family is an intervention class, not one treatment

Labels such as:

- cognitive-behavioral;
- behavioral activation;
- psychodynamic;
- interpersonal;
- family/systemic;
- humanistic/person-centered;
- dialectical behavior therapy;
- mentalization-based treatment;
- EMDR;
- motivational interviewing;
- problem-solving therapy;
- third-wave approaches

describe intervention traditions/packages.

Within-family variation can be substantial.

## 13. Current guideline synthesis confirms multiple evidence-based families

A 2024 consensus-guideline synthesis identified multiple recommended evidence-based psychotherapies/psychosocial interventions across adult psychiatric disorders, including:

- CBT;
- family therapy;
- contingency management;
- DBT;
- EMDR;
- IPT;
- MBT;
- motivational interviewing;
- problem-solving therapy;
- psychoeducation;
- short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy;
- 12-step facilitation.

Therefore:

`evidence-based psychotherapy = CBT only`

is false.

## 14. Family labels differ in theory, tasks and targets

Different families emphasize different hypothesized pathways:

- cognition/behavior;
- exposure/learning;
- interpersonal patterns;
- emotion regulation;
- attachment/mentalization;
- reinforcement contingencies;
- relationship/system processes.

But theoretical target != proven mechanism.

## 15. Same family can contain different active content

CBT itself may include:

- cognitive restructuring;
- exposure;
- behavioral activation;
- skills training;
- problem solving;
- relapse prevention.

Thus:

`CBT works -> cognitive restructuring caused outcome`

is invalid.

## 16. Different families can share techniques

Examples:

- exposure-like practice;
- behavioral experiments;
- emotion processing;
- goal setting;
- psychoeducation;
- interpersonal feedback.

Therefore school labels are imperfect component maps.

# III. Psychotherapy effectiveness in aggregate

## 17. Psychological treatments are effective across many conditions

A 2025 synthesis of 1,029 studies and 85,952 patients across 12 mental-health problems found average benefits across all examined categories, with magnitude varying by condition.

This supports:

**psychotherapy/psychological interventions have broad evidence of benefit.**

It does not support:

- same magnitude everywhere;
- same therapy for all conditions;
- same mechanism everywhere.

## 18. Condition matters

The same broad psychotherapy label can have:

- strong evidence in one disorder;
- smaller effects in another;
- different comparator sensitivity;
- different long-term evidence.

Therefore "psychotherapy effect size" is not one universal constant.

## 19. Evidence base is geographically skewed

The 2025 synthesis found most evidence still came from high-income/Western settings.

Transport beyond those contexts requires explicit attention.

---

# IV. Comparative efficacy and the "Dodo bird" problem

## 20. Similar average outcomes do not prove identical treatments

When bona fide therapies show small or nonsignificant differences, possibilities include:

- genuinely similar efficacy;
- shared active processes;
- different mechanisms yielding similar outcomes;
- low power for small differences;
- broad outcome measures;
- therapist allegiance/fidelity variation;
- heterogeneity across patients.

## 21. "All therapies are equal" is too strong

Current comparative evidence often finds many active psychotherapies perform similarly on average.

But this pattern is:

- disorder-specific;
- comparator-dependent;
- outcome-dependent;
- follow-up-dependent.

## 22. Bona fide comparisons typically produce smaller differences

A 2026 umbrella review of meta-analytic comparisons among bona fide psychological therapies found small average differences and derived non-inferiority margins around SMD ~0.16–0.17 for large trials.

This supports:

**many bona fide therapies differ only modestly on average.**

It does not prove exact equivalence.

## 23. Non-significance != equivalence

If treatment A is not significantly different from B:

`A = B`

does not follow.

Equivalence/non-inferiority requires appropriate design/margin.

## 24. Disorder-specific exceptions matter

Some clinical problems show stronger support for particular techniques/families.

For example:

- exposure/response-prevention-related CBT has strong relevance in OCD;
- trauma-focused approaches/EMDR have strong evidence in PTSD;
- behavioral activation/CBT/IPT and others have evidence in depression.

Thus:

`small average school differences generally -> specific treatment selection never matters`

is invalid.

---

# V. Depression as a comparative example

## 25. Multiple psychotherapies are efficacious for depression

Large network meta-analysis evidence has found multiple approaches superior to care-as-usual/waitlist.

Commonly studied families include:

- CBT;
- behavioral activation;
- IPT;
- problem-solving therapy;
- psychodynamic therapy;
- third-wave therapies.

## 26. Differences among active depression psychotherapies are usually limited

The large 2021 network meta-analysis found few consistent differences among major psychotherapy types for acute adult depression.

This is one basis for emphasizing:

- preference;
- availability;
- clinical context.

## 27. Newer network analyses can produce rankings

A 2024 network meta-analysis of psychotherapy ± medication found outcome differences by:

- treatment;
- medication combination;
- baseline severity.

Rankings can be useful.

But ranking uncertainty and network assumptions matter.

## 28. Rank order is not a universal treatment hierarchy

Network meta-analysis ranking can change with:

- comparator network;
- included studies;
- risk of bias;
- outcome;
- severity;
- follow-up.

Never use one ranking as a timeless school leaderboard.

---

# VI. Anxiety/OCD/PTSD show why condition-specificity matters

## 29. GAD

A 2024 network meta-analysis found:

- CBT;
- third-wave CBT;
- relaxation therapy

outperformed treatment-as-usual initially.

After high-risk-of-bias studies were removed, evidence narrowed, and CBT had the clearest long-term support.

## 30. OCD

A 2026 network meta-analysis of 68 trials / 4,019 patients found multiple psychotherapies superior to several control conditions.

No significant differences between psychotherapy approaches were detected in that network, but:

- power was limited;
- heterogeneity substantial;
- many trials had high risk of bias.

Therefore:

`no significant difference -> all OCD therapies equally established`

is too strong.

## 31. PTSD

Meta-analytic evidence supports trauma-focused CBT and EMDR particularly strongly for PTSD.

A 2025 review of 55 meta-analyses reported long-term evidence strongest for TF-CBT and EMDR, while evidence for other approaches was less established.

Therefore:

**therapy-family comparability is not uniform across clinical targets.**

# VII. Common versus specific factors

## 32. Common-factor hypothesis

Common-factor accounts emphasize processes shared across therapies, such as:

- therapeutic relationship;
- rationale;
- hope/expectancy;
- collaboration;
- emotional engagement;
- corrective interpersonal experience.

## 33. Specific-factor hypothesis

Specific-factor accounts emphasize intervention-specific procedures/processes, such as:

- exposure;
- behavioral activation;
- cognitive restructuring;
- contingency management;
- specific skills training.

## 34. These are not necessarily mutually exclusive

A therapy can work through:

- common factors;
- specific techniques;
- interactions between them;
- context;
- client-driven processes.

## 35. Similar treatment outcomes do not prove common-factor causality

If two schools show similar average efficacy, this does not identify why.

Different mechanisms can converge on similar outcomes.

## 36. Independent component effects argue against "specific factors do nothing"

Component meta-analyses can find differential contributions of specific intervention elements.

BHC-01 already established this.

Therefore a pure "all technique effects are negligible" position is unsupported.

## 37. But technique efficacy does not prove school theory

A specific component can work through a mechanism different from the school's stated theory.

## 38. Current state-of-the-art conclusion

The 2026 World Psychiatry review states that common-versus-specific mechanisms remain difficult to establish because process/mediator research is largely correlational.

The scientifically calibrated position is:

**both common and specific processes can matter; their causal contributions remain incompletely identified.**

---

# VIII. Process-outcome evidence

## 39. Process-outcome literature is large

A 2026 umbrella review synthesized:

- 60 meta-analyses;
- 24 psychotherapy processes;
- 1,981 effect sizes;
- 993 unique primary studies.

## 40. Average process-outcome association

Overall association:

- r ≈ .24.

This is a meaningful association.

It is not a mechanism effect size.

## 41. Process categories

The umbrella review grouped processes into:

- relational;
- technical;
- patient;
- therapist.

## 42. Relational processes had larger correlational associations than technical-process measures on average

Approximate umbrella estimates:

- technical: r ≈ .10;
- patient: r ≈ .23;
- relational: r ≈ .27;
- therapist: r ≈ .29.

This is evidence about association, not causal decomposition of treatment effects.

## 43. Technical-process measures may be disadvantaged by measurement/design

Potential reasons include:

- lower reliability;
- restricted range under fidelity-controlled trials;
- coding burden;
- construct mismatch;
- timing.

Therefore:

`larger relational correlation -> relational processes causally matter more than techniques`

is not established.

---

# IX. Working alliance

## 44. Alliance is one of the most robust psychotherapy-process correlates

Working alliance typically includes:

- bond;
- agreement on goals;
- agreement on tasks.

## 45. Large meta-analytic association

The 2026 state-of-the-art review cites the largest alliance meta-analysis at approximately:

- r = .28.

This association appears across:

- face-to-face;
- internet-based therapy.

## 46. Alliance association survives some adjustment

Prior meta-analysis found alliance-outcome correlations remained around r ~.23–.31 after adjusting for studied intake/process variables.

This strengthens robustness.

It does not fully identify causality.

## 47. Alliance can be cause and consequence

Possible loop:

`alliance -> engagement/improvement -> stronger alliance`.

Early improvement can improve perceived alliance.

## 48. Repeated temporal data are preferable

Lagged/within-person studies provide stronger process evidence than one-time correlations.

But residual confounding remains.

## 49. Alliance ruptures matter

2026 meta-analysis of rupture-outcome association found clinically relevant relationship, but the evidence base was small and methods heterogeneous.

## 50. Alliance is not a therapy-free variable

A strong alliance may depend on:

- credible rationale;
- suitable tasks;
- therapist competence;
- treatment fit;
- actual symptom improvement.

Thus common and specific factors can be coupled.

---

# X. Group cohesion

## 51. Group therapy contains additional relational processes

Group cohesion is not identical to patient-therapist alliance.

## 52. 2026 meta-analysis

In group psychotherapy:

- alliance;
- cohesion

each uniquely predicted outcome with similar coefficients in the included evidence.

## 53. Generalization is limited

The meta-analysis included only 14 studies and showed substantial heterogeneity.

Do not turn cohesion into a universal mechanism.

---

# XI. Expectancy and credibility

## 54. Outcome expectancy is associated with psychotherapy outcome

Updated meta-analytic evidence has found a small-to-moderate association.

A prior updated synthesis across 81 samples / 12,722 patients reported approximately:

- r = .18.

## 55. Expectancy is not merely noise

Expectancy can influence:

- engagement;
- persistence;
- attention;
- interpretation;
- agency.

## 56. Expectancy is not a universal placebo mechanism

High expectancy may also reflect:

- early improvement;
- treatment credibility;
- alliance;
- patient optimism;
- prognosis.

Therefore:

`expectancy predicts outcome -> expectancy causes outcome`

is too strong without stronger temporal/experimental evidence.

## 57. Recent process research supports bidirectional complexity

2026 multilevel work found outcome expectations predicted later change partly through agency/alliance-related pathways.

This remains study-specific process evidence, not a universal mechanism proof.

---

# XII. Therapist effects

## 58. Therapists differ in outcomes

A 2026 systematic scoping review of reviews reports therapist effects commonly estimated around:

- 5–8% of outcome variance.

## 59. Therapist effect != fixed therapist quality

Observed therapist variance may reflect:

- therapist skill;
- patient mix;
- case complexity;
- setting;
- treatment model;
- matching;
- contextual factors.

## 60. Mechanisms explaining therapist effects remain uncertain

The 2026 review found:

- methodological weaknesses;
- limited preregistration;
- weak risk-of-bias assessment;
- limited contextual moderation.

## 61. Interpersonal skills have comparatively consistent support

Evidence is stronger for interpersonal qualities/skills than for many therapist demographic/personality characteristics.

But effect explanations remain incomplete.

## 62. Therapist allegiance can bias comparative studies

A therapist/research team may:

- prefer;
- know better;
- supervise more strongly

one treatment.

Comparative efficacy interpretation should consider allegiance and competence.

---

# XIII. Specific therapist competence

## 63. Adherence and competence are not identical

- adherence: did therapist deliver specified procedures?
- competence: how skillfully?

## 64. Technical adherence-outcome correlations are generally small

Umbrella process evidence found technical-process associations smaller than relational/therapist categories on average.

## 65. Small correlation does not prove technical skill unimportant

Reasons include:

- restricted therapist range;
- poor measurement;
- competent treatment required as floor;
- treatment-specific timing;
- client responsiveness.

## 66. Competence should not be reified as one global therapist trait

A therapist can be strong with:

- one treatment;
- one population;
- one clinical problem

and less effective elsewhere.

---

# XIV. Common-factor claims and causal inference

## 67. "Common factors explain X%" decompositions should be treated cautiously

Popular variance pie charts often mix:

- different evidence types;
- non-independent sources;
- conceptual categories.

They should not be interpreted as experimentally identified causal variance components.

## 68. Correlation magnitude is not percent causal contribution

For example:

`alliance r=.28 -> alliance causes 28% of treatment benefit`

is invalid.

## 69. Common factors may be prerequisites rather than active mechanisms

A workable alliance may enable:

- exposure;
- skills practice;
- disclosure;
- behavioral experiments.

This does not mean alliance alone produces the same outcome.

## 70. Specific factors may operate through relational processes

A credible exposure rationale may strengthen alliance/task agreement.

The dichotomy can be artificial.

---

# XV. Harms and deterioration

## 71. Psychotherapy is not risk-free

Negative effects can include:

- symptom deterioration;
- emotional distress;
- relationship conflict;
- dependency;
- stigma/self-concept changes;
- treatment burden;
- adverse events.

## 72. Negative effect != adverse event != treatment-caused harm

Distinguish:

- deterioration in outcome;
- adverse event occurring during treatment;
- side effect attributable to correctly delivered treatment;
- malpractice/incorrect application;
- illness progression unrelated to treatment.

## 73. Harm reporting remains inadequate

A 2025 systematic review of 85 psychotherapy RCTs / 14,420 participants found explicit harm-related reporting in about 60% of studies.

Assessment/reporting methods were highly heterogeneous.

## 74. Adverse events are not rare in trials

The same review concluded AEs were seen in a meaningful minority.

But:

- event definitions differed;
- treatment-relatedness was often unclear.

Therefore precise universal harm rates are not defensible.

## 75. Serious adverse events require causal caution

SAEs during psychotherapy may reflect:

- underlying disorder;
- concurrent treatment;
- life events;
- psychotherapy.

Occurrence during treatment is not proof of treatment causation.

## 76. Absence of AE reporting != absence of harm

This is a core safety rule.

## 77. Deterioration can occur despite positive average effects

A treatment can reduce average symptoms while a minority worsen.

Average benefit does not cancel individual harm monitoring.

## 78. Harm should be part of informed intervention reasoning

Benefit–risk reasoning requires:

- improvement;
- deterioration;
- burden;
- negative experiences;
- opportunity cost.

---

# XVI. Comparator choice and school effects

## 79. Waitlist comparisons tend to produce larger apparent effects

Waitlist may involve:

- no active therapeutic contact;
- expectation differences;
- delayed treatment.

## 80. Psychological placebo/attention controls are more active

They can include:

- support;
- attention;
- monitoring;
- rationale.

## 81. Bona fide therapy comparisons are the strongest school-level test

If two credible, competently delivered therapies are compared directly, the question is narrower:

> Does A outperform another serious treatment?

## 82. Smaller bona fide differences do not imply ineffective therapy

Both treatments can be effective.

## 83. Control condition must be part of every effect-size interpretation

Never compare effect sizes across meta-analyses without checking comparator composition.

---

# XVII. Therapy selection

## 84. Evidence-based practice is broader than "pick the top meta-analysis"

APA defines evidence-based practice in psychology as integrating:

- best available research;
- clinical expertise;
- patient characteristics;
- culture;
- preferences.

## 85. This is not permission for unvalidated intuition

Clinical expertise should operate within evidence and monitoring.

## 86. Treatment indication matters

Evidence strength differs by:

- diagnosis;
- symptom architecture;
- severity;
- comorbidity;
- risk;
- developmental context.

## 87. Availability and feasibility matter

An inaccessible "best" treatment can have zero real-world benefit.

## 88. Preference matters but its outcome effect is usually modest

A 2024 anxiety/depression meta-analysis found:

- no clear clinical-outcome benefit;
- small benefit for satisfaction;
- better adherence when preferences were accommodated.

## 89. Broader 2025 update found small preference effects

Across 60 studies, preference/active choice was associated with small improvements in:

- satisfaction;
- completion;
- clinical outcome.

Therefore:

**preferences matter, but preference matching is not a large universal efficacy modifier.**

---

# XVIII. Personalization

## 90. Personalization is a family of strategies

It can mean tailoring:

- treatment choice;
- modules;
- dose;
- sequence;
- therapist;
- format;
- goals.

These are different interventions.

## 91. Personalization is not automatically superior

A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of 16 RCTs found no convincing pooled advantage of more personalized psychological treatment on symptoms, smoking cessation, or process outcomes.

## 92. Personalized treatment rules need prospective validation

Retrospective moderator/model performance is insufficient.

## 93. Machine learning prediction is not treatment-effect estimation

A model predicting who will improve under therapy does not necessarily estimate:

`who will improve more under therapy A than B`.

Treatment selection needs comparative causal prediction.

## 94. 2025 ML meta-analysis suggests promise but limited maturity

ML treatment-response studies show predictive signal, but:

- heterogeneity;
- validation limits;
- clinical utility uncertainty

remain.

## 95. A 2026 preregistered digital treatment-matching study failed to validate its matching rule

The held-out interaction did not support statistically significant individualized treatment recommendations.

This is a useful falsification example.

## 96. Other matching evidence is promising but design-sensitive

A 2025 JAMA Psychiatry study produced a treatment-decision rule for late-life depression with retrospective/secondary-analysis promise.

A 2026 large real-world provider-matching cohort found modest faster improvement with algorithm-recommended therapists.

These findings require design-specific interpretation.

## 97. Observational provider matching is not equivalent to randomized matching

Self-selection and algorithm uptake can confound outcome.

## 98. Small matching benefit can still be practically useful

"Not transformative" does not mean irrelevant.

But effect size, cost, calibration and external validity matter.

---

# XIX. Patient–therapist matching

## 99. Matching can target different constructs

Examples:

- therapist historical outcomes;
- clinical expertise;
- preference;
- demographics;
- interpersonal fit;
- availability.

## 100. Demographic matching is not universally supported

Therapist-effect reviews find little consistent outcome effect for many demographic matching variables, with limited exceptions.

## 101. Historical therapist performance may be useful

But requires:

- adequate case-mix adjustment;
- enough cases per therapist;
- transport stability;
- fairness evaluation.

## 102. Matching algorithm = decision system, not truth about compatibility

It must be evaluated prospectively.

# XX. School allegiance and branding

## 103. Therapy labels can become identity claims

This creates risk of:

- cherry-picking;
- confirmation bias;
- dismissing equivalent alternatives;
- mechanism overclaim.

## 104. Empirical claims should be school-neutral

Ask:

- What package?
- What target?
- What comparator?
- What population?
- What outcome?
- What follow-up?
- What harms?

## 105. "Evidence-based therapy" is condition-specific

A therapy can be well supported for one problem and weakly studied for another.

## 106. "Integrative" does not automatically mean evidence-integrating

An eclectic package may combine useful components.

But combining evidence-based components does not automatically produce an evidence-based package.

# XXI. Comparative evidence hierarchy

## 107. Level 0 — theoretical school claim

Theory says treatment should work.

No outcome evidence.

## 108. Level 1 — uncontrolled improvement

Symptoms improve during treatment.

Natural history/regression/expectancy uncontrolled.

## 109. Level 2 — inactive-control superiority

Treatment outperforms waitlist/no treatment.

Package efficacy supported.

## 110. Level 3 — active-control superiority

Treatment outperforms credible alternative/control.

Stronger incremental evidence.

## 111. Level 4 — bona fide head-to-head evidence

Direct comparison between serious treatments.

Useful for school/package differences.

## 112. Level 5 — replicated network/component evidence

Comparative and component evidence converges across:

- trials;
- settings;
- follow-up;
- bias restrictions.

## 113. Level 6 — validated treatment-selection rule

A patient-level matching rule prospectively improves outcomes versus reasonable alternative assignment.

Current psychotherapy science has relatively little evidence at this level.

---

# XXII. School-level inference examples

## 114. CBT

Strong evidence across many disorders.

But:

`CBT works broadly -> CBT is always best`

is invalid.

Evidence quality varies by disorder.

## 115. Psychodynamic therapy

Evidence exists for several clinical targets, including depression.

Long-term psychodynamic psychotherapy evidence is smaller and more heterogeneous than broad CBT evidence.

Therefore neither:

`psychodynamic = unsupported`

nor:

`psychodynamic = equally established everywhere`

is defensible.

## 116. IPT

Strong evidence particularly in depression and some related contexts.

It is not a universal transdiagnostic default.

## 117. Behavioral activation

Can be effective for depression and appears in component evidence.

Its success does not prove all depression is maintained by activity deficit.

## 118. EMDR

Strong PTSD evidence.

Its clinical efficacy does not by itself establish every proposed mechanism of eye movements.

## 119. DBT/MBT/family/systemic approaches

Evidence exists for particular clinical populations/tasks.

Do not generalize from indication-specific evidence to all psychopathology.

---

# XXIII. Therapy-brand choice versus component choice

## 120. Brand contains bundles

Treatment brands package:

- techniques;
- rationale;
- therapist stance;
- sequencing;
- homework;
- relationship norms.

## 121. Component science can cut across brands

If multiple brands share effective components, brand-level comparisons can obscure them.

## 122. Brand may still matter

Sequencing/interactions/training/ecology can make the package more than the sum of listed components.

Therefore:

`components known -> brand irrelevant`

is too strong.

---

# XXIV. Alliance and technique interaction

## 123. Alliance can enable technique

Examples:

- trust allows disclosure;
- task agreement enables exposure;
- goal agreement supports adherence.

## 124. Technique can build alliance

A treatment that:

- provides relief;
- offers coherent rationale;
- produces mastery

may strengthen alliance.

## 125. Alliance should be monitored but not worshipped

High alliance with ineffective treatment can still fail.

Low early alliance can sometimes be repaired.

## 126. Rupture repair is a process target

Evidence supports attention to ruptures.

But no single rupture-repair protocol is universally proven.

---

# XXV. Expectancy, preference and credibility

## 127. Expectancy

Belief treatment will help.

## 128. Preference

Desire for one treatment/format/provider.

## 129. Credibility

Belief the treatment rationale makes sense.

They overlap but are distinct.

## 130. Preference accommodation may improve retention more reliably than symptoms

This distinction is useful for shared decision making.

## 131. Expectancy can be ethically supported

Providing:

- accurate rationale;
- realistic optimism;
- transparent evidence

is compatible with evidence-based care.

Manipulative overpromising is not.

---

# XXVI. Harms taxonomy

## 132. Symptom deterioration

Reliable worsening on symptom outcome.

## 133. Adverse event

Undesirable event during treatment.

May or may not be caused by treatment.

## 134. Side effect

Negative consequence attributable to correctly delivered treatment.

## 135. Malpractice/incorrect application

Harm from inappropriate/incompetent delivery is conceptually distinct from intrinsic treatment side effect.

## 136. Burden

Time, cost, emotional demand, opportunity cost.

## 137. Relationship/social effects

Therapy can affect:

- relationships;
- identity;
- role decisions.

These effects can be positive or negative.

## 138. No single harms rate exists

Rates depend on:

- definition;
- instrument;
- population;
- treatment;
- source;
- follow-up.

---

# XXVII. Monitoring and feedback

## 139. Routine outcome monitoring can detect nonresponse/deterioration

Feedback systems may improve outcomes in some contexts, especially for patients not on track.

## 140. Measurement alone does not fix treatment

Feedback must be:

- timely;
- interpretable;
- acted upon.

## 141. Progress monitoring can also create measurement reactivity

This is usually acceptable but should be conceptually recognized.

# XXIX. High-value failure modes

## Failure A — all therapies are equally effective

Too strong.

Many bona fide differences are small, but condition-specific differences and evidence strength matter.

## Failure B — common factors explain all psychotherapy

Unsupported.

Common processes are robustly associated with outcome but causal decomposition remains incomplete.

## Failure C — specific techniques explain all psychotherapy

Unsupported.

Relational/patient/therapist/context processes also matter.

## Failure D — similar outcomes = same mechanism

Incorrect.

Different mechanisms can converge.

## Failure E — alliance correlation = alliance mechanism

Incorrect.

Bidirectionality/confounding remain.

## Failure F — strong alliance = effective therapy

Not necessarily.

Outcome and technical adequacy still matter.

## Failure G — therapist effect = fixed therapist talent

Incorrect.

Patient/context/case mix contribute.

## Failure H — therapist demographic match = better outcome

Not generally established.

## Failure I — expectancy = placebo noise

Incorrect.

Expectancy is a meaningful process correlate.

## Failure J — expectancy predicts outcome = expectancy causes outcome

Too strong.

Causal direction remains complex.

## Failure K — no significant head-to-head difference = equivalence

Incorrect.

Need equivalence/non-inferiority logic.

## Failure L — network rank = universal hierarchy

Incorrect.

Rank depends on network/context/outcome.

## Failure M — CBT broadly effective = CBT always best

Incorrect.

Condition, comparator and alternatives matter.

## Failure N — psychodynamic evidence exists = equally supported for every indication

Incorrect.

Evidence volume/quality varies.

## Failure O — EMDR efficacy proves eye-movement mechanism

Incorrect.

Package efficacy != mechanism.

## Failure P — preference match = large efficacy gain

Generally unsupported.

Effects are small/heterogeneous.

## Failure Q — personalization label = personalized evidence

Incorrect.

Need randomized/comparative validation.

## Failure R — ML predicts response = ML selects best treatment

Incorrect.

Prognostic prediction != treatment-effect prediction.

## Failure S — algorithmic provider matching study = causal matching proof

Incorrect unless design identifies the matching effect.

## Failure T — no harm reported = no harm

Incorrect.

Harms are undermeasured.

## Failure U — adverse event during therapy = therapy caused it

Incorrect.

Attribution requires causal assessment.

## Failure V — average benefit = no deterioration

Incorrect.

Some individuals worsen.

## Failure W — preferred treatment must be chosen regardless of evidence

Incorrect.

Preference is one decision input.

## Failure X — school brand = active ingredients

Incorrect.

Packages contain multiple overlapping components.

# XXXII. Evidence-strength map

## High-confidence practical boundaries

1. psychotherapy is effective across many mental-health conditions;
2. effect magnitude varies by condition;
3. multiple therapy families have evidence;
4. therapy labels do not identify mechanisms;
5. many bona fide head-to-head differences are small;
6. small differences do not prove exact equivalence;
7. disorder-specific treatment evidence can still matter;
8. common and specific processes are not mutually exclusive;
9. alliance is robustly associated with outcome;
10. alliance association does not establish one-way causality;
11. expectancy is associated with outcome;
12. therapist-level outcome variance exists;
13. therapist-effect mechanisms remain incompletely identified;
14. patient preference generally has small effects on outcome and somewhat clearer effects on satisfaction/completion;
15. personalization is not automatically superior;
16. treatment-matching models require comparative external/prospective validation;
17. psychotherapy can have negative effects;
18. harm reporting is currently incomplete/heterogeneous;
19. average benefit does not exclude individual deterioration;
20. evidence-based treatment choice integrates research, expertise, context and patient characteristics/preferences.

## Supported but context-sensitive

1. common-factor processes contribute meaningfully;
2. specific components contribute meaningfully in some interventions;
3. alliance rupture/repair is clinically relevant;
4. provider matching may yield modest improvement;
5. some treatment preferences improve adherence/retention;
6. some conditions show more differentiated comparative treatment evidence;
7. routine outcome monitoring can reduce deterioration in some contexts.

## Not established as universal

1. all psychotherapies are equivalent;
2. common factors are the sole mechanism;
3. specific techniques are the sole mechanism;
4. alliance is the causal mechanism of all psychotherapy;
5. therapist quality is one stable trait;
6. expectancy is a universal causal mechanism;
7. one therapy school is globally superior;
8. therapy preference determines best clinical outcome;
9. machine-learning matching reliably improves treatment selection across settings;
10. absence of reported harms means treatment is safe.

## Source-boundary note

The 2026 World Psychiatry state-of-the-art review is used as the main current synthesis of psychotherapy innovation, process, common/specific-factor and personalization evidence.

The process-outcome umbrella review reports **correlational** associations. Its larger relational/therapist associations must not be converted into causal variance decomposition.

The therapist-effect scoping review reports relatively stable therapist-level outcome variance estimates but also substantial methodological limitations; it does not identify a single stable therapist-quality mechanism.

Comparative network meta-analyses are treated as disorder-, comparator-, outcome-, and time-specific.

The preference meta-analyses support small average effects; they do not justify preference-only treatment selection.

The 2026 provider-matching study is observational/real-world and is not treated as randomized proof that matching algorithms cause improved outcomes.

Psychotherapy adverse-event reviews show underdeveloped and heterogeneous harm monitoring. Event occurrence is not automatically treatment causation.

No source here is used to prescribe an individualized treatment.
