# 2. Canonical principle: quality follows methodology

There is no single universal list of qualitative "validity checks" that every good study must complete.

APA JARS–Qual explicitly states that:

- member checking;
- triangulation;
- consensus/auditing;
- thick description;
- reflexive logs;
- interrater/consistency procedures;

may augment methodological integrity, but a study does **not** need all or any specific one of them.

Different qualitative approaches value different quality practices.

### Critical rule

Do not appraise qualitative quality by asking:

> How many rigor techniques did the authors tick?

Ask:

> Are question, methodology, sampling, data generation, analysis, reflexivity, evidence and claims coherent with one another?

---

# 3. Methodological integrity

A useful cross-family project concept is **methodological integrity**.

It asks whether:

1. data are adequate for the intended question;
2. researcher perspectives are handled transparently;
3. findings are grounded in evidence;
4. contradictions/disconfirming data are handled coherently;
5. analytic procedures fit the methodology;
6. claims do not outrun the data;
7. alternative interpretations are considered;
8. context and transferability limits are explicit.

### Rule

Methodological integrity is broader than any one trustworthiness technique.

---

# 4. Lincoln/Guba trustworthiness architecture

The classic four-domain framework remains widely used:

1. credibility;
2. transferability;
3. dependability;
4. confirmability.

This framework is useful but should not be treated as the only legitimate quality philosophy.

---

# 5. Credibility

Credibility asks:

> Is the interpretation sufficiently well-grounded and plausible relative to the participants/data/context?

Possible supports include:

- prolonged engagement;
- persistent observation;
- triangulation;
- clarification during interviews;
- negative-case analysis;
- reflexive analysis;
- participant feedback;
- peer/critical dialogue;
- rich evidence in reporting.

### Critical rule

No single technique "proves credibility".

Credibility emerges from the whole analytic process.

---

# 6. Transferability

Transferability concerns whether readers can judge whether findings may be useful in another context.

It is supported by:

- thick contextual description;
- clear participant/sample description;
- clear setting description;
- boundary conditions;
- explanation of what appears context-specific.

### Critical rule

Transferability ≠ statistical generalizability.

The researcher supplies context; the reader evaluates contextual fit.

---

# 7. Dependability

Dependability concerns the coherence and traceability of the research process.

Useful evidence:

- clear procedural description;
- documented analytic decisions;
- versioning of codebooks/frameworks where relevant;
- audit/decision trail;
- explanation of methodological changes;
- transparent handling of researcher/team changes.

### Critical rule

Dependability does not mean another researcher must reproduce the identical interpretation.

JARS–Qual explicitly recognizes that qualitative researchers may legitimately reach different but methodologically sound interpretations.

---

# 8. Confirmability

Confirmability concerns whether findings can be traced to evidence and analytic reasoning rather than being unsupported assertions.

Possible supports:

- data excerpts;
- audit trail;
- analytic memos;
- reflexive documentation;
- comparison with disconfirming data;
- transparent explanation of interpretation.

### Critical rule

Confirmability does not mean "researcher neutrality".

In many interpretive traditions, researcher subjectivity is part of the analytic instrument.

---

# 9. Reflexivity: canonical definition

Reflexivity is systematic examination of how the researcher/research team shapes:

- question;
- access;
- relationship;
- data generation;
- interpretation;
- decisions;
- reporting;
- ethics.

It is not merely:

> "The first author was a physiotherapist."

A strong reflexive account asks:

> How did being a physiotherapist change what participants told them, what they noticed, what they normalized, and how they interpreted the data?

---

# 10. Positionality vs reflexivity

## Positionality

Describes relevant researcher locations/identities/experiences:

- athlete;
- coach;
- clinician;
- gender;
- disciplinary background;
- insider/outsider relationship;
- prior relationship with participants.

## Reflexivity

Analyzes what those positions **did** in the research process.

### Rule

Positionality statement alone is not complete reflexivity.

---

# 11. Reflexivity is not bias elimination

In interpretive qualitative research, the researcher is not a contamination source that can simply be removed.

Reflexivity aims to:

- identify assumptions;
- examine influence;
- challenge habitual interpretations;
- make decisions transparent;
- use subjectivity productively where appropriate.

### Critical rule

Avoid wording such as:

> reflexivity eliminated researcher bias.

Better:

> reflexivity made researcher assumptions/influence visible and analytically examinable.

---

# 12. Insider/outsider is not a binary quality ranking

Insider status can improve:

- access;
- rapport;
- technical understanding;
- recognition of tacit meanings.

It can also increase:

- normalization of local practices;
- assumptions of shared meaning;
- participant impression management;
- role conflicts.

Outsider status can improve:

- ability to notice taken-for-granted practices;
- willingness to ask naive questions.

But can reduce:

- access;
- trust;
- contextual fluency.

### Rule

Insider ≠ automatically better.
Outsider ≠ automatically more objective.

Reflexive management matters.

---

# 13. Sport/exercise reflexivity example

Competitive-swimming methodology literature documents a researcher who had been both swimmer and coach.

The methodological issue was not to "remove" that background.

It was to continuously examine:

- embodied familiarity;
- tacit assumptions;
- what seemed normal;
- how prior coaching knowledge shaped interpretation.

This is directly relevant to fitness research, where researchers are often:

- athletes;
- coaches;
- physiotherapists;
- S&C practitioners;
- sport scientists.

---

# 14. Reflexive journaling

A reflexive journal can record:

- assumptions before interviews;
- emotional reactions;
- discomfort;
- role conflict;
- unexpected rapport;
- emerging interpretations;
- why questions changed;
- why codes/categories changed;
- doubts;
- ethical tensions.

### Value

Provides an explicit trace of researcher influence and analytic evolution.

### Critical rule

Keeping a diary does not create reflexivity by itself.

The reflections must influence or interrogate the research process.

---

# 15. Analytic memoing vs reflexive journaling

## Analytic memo

Focus:
- concepts;
- categories;
- relationships;
- theories.

## Reflexive journal

Focus:
- researcher position;
- assumptions;
- relational effects;
- decisions;
- emotional/ethical influence.

They can overlap.

### Rule

Do not treat all memoing as equivalent.

---

# 16. Triangulation: major types

Commonly described forms:

1. data/source triangulation;
2. investigator triangulation;
3. method triangulation;
4. theory triangulation.

## Data/source

Different:
- people;
- sites;
- times;
- documents.

## Investigator

Multiple researchers contribute interpretations.

## Method

Different collection methods:
- interview;
- observation;
- document.

## Theory

Use different conceptual lenses.

---

# 17. Triangulation is not majority voting

If:

- interviews say one thing;
- observations show another;
- documents show a third;

the researcher should not simply choose the majority.

Dissonance can be analytically important.

### Example

Athletes say:

> coaches encourage open injury reporting.

Observation shows athletes hiding symptoms around selection decisions.

The contradiction may reveal:

- impression management;
- institutional risk;
- competing norms.

### Rule

Triangulation can produce:
- convergence;
- complementarity;
- contradiction.

All three may be valuable.

---

# 18. Triangulation does not prove one objective truth

Barbour and later methodology critiques warn against assuming multiple methods validate one hidden truth mechanically.

Different methods may capture different aspects of a phenomenon.

### Rule

Preferred framing:

> triangulation broadens, tests or complicates interpretation.

Avoid:

> three methods agreed, therefore the finding is objectively true.

---

# 19. Investigator triangulation

Multiple analysts can:

- expose assumptions;
- challenge blind spots;
- introduce disciplinary perspectives;
- compare interpretations.

But the function differs by paradigm.

### Coding-reliability paradigm

Goal may be:
- consistency;
- codebook stability;
- agreement.

### Reflexive/interpretive paradigm

Goal may be:
- broaden interpretation;
- deepen reflexivity;
- challenge assumptions;

not achieve one "correct" coding.

---

# 20. Intercoder agreement is not universally required

This is a major project rule.

Intercoder agreement can be coherent in:

- coding-reliability thematic analysis;
- structured codebook analysis;
- content-analysis approaches;
- some framework approaches.

It may be incoherent as a validity requirement in:

- reflexive thematic analysis;
- some constructivist/interpretive designs;
- narrative interpretation;
- phenomenological analysis.

### Braun/Clarke rule

For reflexive TA:

- coding is subjective and situated;
- there is no single correct coding solution;
- independent coding + kappa is not the quality target.

### Critical rule

Never demand kappa simply because multiple coders exist.

---

# 21. Consensus coding boundary

Consensus can serve different functions.

## Legitimate structured use

Team reconciles a codebook to maintain consistent classification.

## Reflexive use

Team discussion surfaces alternative interpretations.

### Critical rule

"Consensus" should not automatically mean:

> disagreement was error.

In interpretive work, disagreement can be analytically productive.

---

# 22. Peer debriefing / critical friend

A peer/critical friend can:

- challenge assumptions;
- ask for evidence;
- test rival interpretations;
- identify gaps;
- question overreach.

### Critical rule

Peer debriefing is not independent verification that one interpretation is objectively correct.

Its value is analytic challenge.

---

# 23. Negative / disconfirming case analysis

Researchers actively seek data that do not fit the current interpretation.

Possible outcomes:

- interpretation survives;
- category boundary changes;
- category splits;
- contextual condition is added;
- explanation is rejected.

### Rule

Negative cases are a strong cross-family quality strategy when methodology supports pattern-building.

They should not be hidden merely because they complicate a clean story.

---

# 24. Rival explanations

Especially relevant for:

- case study;
- process evaluation;
- implementation research;
- explanatory qualitative inquiry.

Example:

Low adherence might be explained by:

- intervention burden;
- scheduling;
- transport;
- pain;
- weak coach relationship.

Quality improves when plausible alternatives are actively examined.

---

# 25. Member checking / respondent validation

Member checking can involve returning:

- transcript;
- summary;
- category;
- interpretation;
- findings;

to participants.

Possible purposes:

- factual correction;
- clarification;
- resonance check;
- additional data;
- co-interpretation;
- ethical respect.

### Critical rule

These purposes are not the same.

A study should state which one it intended.

---

# 26. Member checking is not a universal gold standard

Current methodological literature explicitly rejects mandatory member checking.

Problems include:

- participants may change views over time;
- researchers and participants have different analytic roles;
- participants may disagree for legitimate reasons;
- a participant cannot necessarily validate a cross-case interpretation;
- post-hoc feedback may create new data rather than verify old data;
- sensitive findings may create risk.

### Project rule

Do not penalize a qualitative study merely because member checking was not used.

Ask whether its alternative credibility process fits the methodology.

---

# 27. Transcript checking vs interpretation checking

These are different.

## Transcript checking

Question:
> Is this recording/transcription factually accurate?

## Interpretation checking

Question:
> Does this analysis resonate with how the participant understands the experience?

### Rule

Accurate transcript confirmation does not validate the entire analysis.

---

# 28. Member feedback can be new data

If a participant says:

> I disagree with your interpretation now.

This can indicate:

- researcher error;
- changed participant perspective;
- context change;
- discomfort with the implication;
- legitimate alternative interpretation.

### Rule

Participant disagreement should be analyzed rather than automatically treated as a veto.

---

# 29. Member reflection

Some interpretive traditions prefer thinking of participant re-engagement as **member reflection** rather than simple validity checking.

The emphasis is on:

- dialogue;
- elaboration;
- challenge;
- co-construction;
- further insight.

### Project rule

Where authors use participant feedback in this way, record it as:

> member reflection/co-interpretation

rather than forcing the label "validation".

---

# 30. Audit trail / decision trail

An audit trail can document:

- sampling decisions;
- interview-guide changes;
- codebook versions;
- memo development;
- category changes;
- exclusions;
- negative cases;
- team decisions;
- rationale for stopping collection.

### Value

Supports:
- dependability;
- confirmability;
- transparency.

### Critical rule

An audit trail is only useful if the documented decisions can be traced to actual analytic development.

A folder of files is not automatically an audit trail.

---

# 31. Thick description

Thick description provides enough contextualized detail to understand:

- what happened;
- where;
- under what norms;
- among whom;
- why actions had meaning.

### Quality role

Supports:
- contextual interpretation;
- transferability judgment;
- credibility.

### Rule

Thick description ≠ long quotation dump.

Interpretive context is required.

---

# 32. Prolonged engagement

Prolonged engagement can improve:

- trust;
- contextual familiarity;
- access to ordinary vs exceptional behavior;
- ability to detect misinformation;
- understanding of local norms.

Most relevant to:

- ethnography;
- longitudinal field research;
- some case studies.

### Critical rule

It is not a universal requirement for every interview-based qualitative study.

---

# 33. Persistent observation

Persistent observation means sustained attention to the aspects most relevant to the scientific problem.

It complements prolonged engagement.

### Distinction

Prolonged engagement:
> broad contextual familiarity.

Persistent observation:
> focused analytic depth.

---

# 34. Data excerpts / evidentiary grounding

Qualitative claims should be visibly connected to evidence through:

- quotations;
- field-note excerpts;
- interaction sequences;
- documents;
- case examples;
- analytic tables/models.

### Critical rule

Quotes are evidence demonstrations.

They do not replace analysis.

A page of quotes with minimal interpretation is not automatically rigorous.

---

# 35. Reporting contradictions

JARS–Qual explicitly expects findings to make sense of contradictions/disconfirming evidence.

### Rule

Contradictions should be:

- reported;
- interpreted;
- contextualized;

rather than silently removed to make themes cleaner.

---

# 36. Reporting standards vs quality

COREQ, SRQR and JARS–Qual improve transparency.

They are not:

- methodological designs;
- validity tests;
- proof that the study was well executed.

A perfectly completed checklist can describe a weak study.

A strong study can use a methodology-specific reporting structure not identical to one checklist.

### Project rule

Use reporting standards to ask:

> Was enough methodological information reported to appraise quality?

Do not use them as automatic quality scores.

---

# 37. COREQ scope boundary

COREQ was designed specifically for:

- interviews;
- focus groups.

It covers:

1. research team/reflexivity;
2. study design;
3. analysis/reporting.

### Critical rule

COREQ is not automatically the best reporting framework for:

- CA;
- ethnography;
- discourse analysis;
- visual methods;
- all narrative inquiry.

---

# 38. SRQR scope

SRQR is broader and was designed to preserve flexibility across qualitative paradigms.

It is useful for reporting transparency across a wider range of designs.

### Rule

Reporting completeness still does not settle paradigm-specific quality questions.

---

# 39. JARS–Qual methodological-integrity advantage

JARS–Qual is particularly useful for the Stage 2 classifier because it:

- asks researchers to describe prior understandings;
- asks how those understandings influenced research;
- asks for researcher–participant relationship;
- requires rationales for design/analysis;
- explicitly allows multiple valid interpretations;
- rejects universal mandatory "checks".

### Project implication

JARS–Qual is a better cross-family reporting backbone than a rigid universal checklist of trustworthiness techniques.

# 40. Methodology-specific quality: phenomenology

Key quality questions:

- Was the phenomenological tradition explicit?;
- Was the lived phenomenon appropriate?;
- Did analysis fit descriptive vs hermeneutic vs IPA logic?;
- Was researcher positioning handled according to tradition?;
- Was idiographic depth preserved for IPA?;
- Were phenomenological claims grounded in experiential material?

### Critical rule

Generic "two coders + member check + saturation" does not prove phenomenological quality.

---

# 41. Methodology-specific quality: grounded theory

Key quality questions:

- iterative collection-analysis?;
- constant comparison?;
- theoretical sampling?;
- memoing?;
- category development?;
- negative/contrasting cases?;
- theoretical integration?;
- saturation/sufficiency appropriate to lineage?;

### Rule

Intercoder reliability cannot substitute for theoretical development.

---

# 42. Methodology-specific quality: ethnography

Key quality questions:

- field/context depth?;
- social practices actually observed?;
- reflexive role/position?;
- emic/etic interpretation?;
- thick description?;
- contradiction between talk/action handled?;
- sampling across relevant actors/events/times?;

### Rule

Interview-only credibility checks cannot replace missing ethnographic context.

---

# 43. Methodology-specific quality: case study

Key quality questions:

- case clearly bounded?;
- evidence integrated within case?;
- multiple sources where needed?;
- rival explanations?;
- within/cross-case logic?;
- chain of evidence?;
- analytic/generalization claim calibrated?;

### Rule

More coders do not repair an undefined case.

---

# 44. Methodology-specific quality: QD

Relevant quality features:

- descriptive question/design fit;
- low-inference alignment;
- appropriate variation;
- transparent category generation;
- data-near evidence;
- reflexivity despite descriptive aim;
- claim limits.

### Rule

QD does not become stronger merely by adding abstract theorizing.

---

# 45. Methodology-specific quality: Interpretive Description

Relevant features:

- practice-oriented conceptual purpose;
- epistemological integrity;
- representative credibility;
- analytic logic;
- interpretive authority;
- reflexive use of disciplinary knowledge.

### Rule

Member checking is optional and must fit the interpretive purpose.

---

# 46. Methodology-specific quality: narrative inquiry

Relevant features:

- story retained as analytic object;
- temporality;
- plot/identity;
- audience/co-construction;
- narrative context;
- appropriate individual/collective inference;
- researcher restorying transparent.

### Rule

Intercoder agreement on isolated lines can conflict with whole-story narrative analysis.

---

# 47. Methodology-specific quality: discourse / CA

## Conversation Analysis

Quality depends on:

- recording quality;
- transcription detail;
- sequence evidence;
- participant orientation;
- collections/variants/deviant cases.

## DP/CDP/FDA/CDA

Quality depends on:

- analytic tradition clarity;
- discourse-as-action/construction consistency;
- contextual evidence;
- transparent inferential movement;
- power/ideology claims grounded in text/context.

### Rule

Generic member checking is often not an appropriate "validation" requirement for discourse analysis.

---

# 48. Methodology-specific quality: longitudinal qualitative research

Relevant features:

- temporal question;
- meaningful timing;
- trajectory/continuity analysis;
- attrition transparency;
- missing-wave handling;
- stable vs adaptive interview logic;
- relationship/reflexivity across repeated contact.

### Rule

Repeat interviews do not prove temporal quality.

---

# 49. Methodology-specific quality: participatory/co-design research

Quality includes:

- actual decision rights;
- power sharing;
- transparent roles;
- compensation/resources;
- dissent;
- co-interpretation;
- ownership;
- impact of participation.

### Critical rule

A participant advisory panel listed in Methods does not prove substantive participation.

---

# 50. Reflexivity and team diversity

A multidisciplinary team can improve interpretive challenge.

But diversity does not automatically create reflexivity.

### Rule

The team should show:

- how perspectives differed;
- how disagreement was used;
- what assumptions changed;
- how analytic decisions were made.

"Team had diverse expertise" alone is descriptive, not reflexive evidence.

# 51. Fitness / exercise crosswalk

| Situation | Quality issue |
|---|---|
| S&C coach interviews other S&C coaches | insider positionality, rapport + normalization risk |
| Former elite athlete studies injury identity | embodied familiarity, possible shared assumptions |
| Physiotherapist interviews injured athletes in own clinic | authority/power relationship, disclosure risk |
| Researcher joins gym for ethnography | changing insider status, access, reactivity |
| Two coders independently code reflexive TA and calculate kappa | methodological mismatch |
| Codebook content analysis uses trained coders and agreement checks | potentially coherent |
| Coach-athlete CA uses ordinary transcript without timing/overlap | evidence inadequacy for interaction claims |
| Participants approve a transcript | confirms transcription at most, not full analytic validity |
| Participants challenge a theme | new/contradictory data to analyze, not automatic invalidation |
| Interviews and observations disagree | analytic opportunity, not triangulation failure |
| Athlete dropout cases contradict adherence theme | negative cases should refine explanation |
| Ethnography includes many interviews but little field exposure | quality problem not repaired by participant N |
| QD reports barriers using rich evidence but no member checking | can still be methodologically sound |
| Participatory exercise study lists community advisers but no decision rights | tokenism / governance-quality problem |

# 52. Current sport/exercise examples

## Competitive swimming

Insider researcher with former swimmer/coach identity used sustained reflexivity/bracketing to examine assumptions arising from embodied familiarity.

## Elite sport injury surveillance

A qualitative study explicitly reported the lead researcher as an insider physiotherapist and used team discussion/member checking and multiple researchers as trustworthiness strategies.

Project interpretation:

These techniques are not automatically "best practice"; their value depends on the analysis model.

## Paralympic coaching ethnography, 2026

Researcher documented how dual role, access and relationships shaped interpretation using field-note reflexivity.

This is a strong example of reflexivity as ongoing methodological work rather than a one-line positionality statement.

# 54. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| rigor vs checklist count | techniques do not create quality automatically |
| reporting completeness vs research quality | a well-reported weak study remains weak |
| COREQ vs universal qualitative standard | its scope is interviews/focus groups |
| credibility vs objective truth | interpretive plausibility is not positivist proof |
| transferability vs statistical generalization | contextual applicability differs |
| dependability vs identical replication | qualitative interpretation need not be identical |
| confirmability vs researcher neutrality | traceability ≠ absence of subjectivity |
| positionality statement vs reflexivity | biography alone does not analyze influence |
| reflexivity vs bias elimination | aim is critical examination, not removal |
| insider vs biased | insider position has strengths and risks |
| outsider vs objective | outsider status also shapes data |
| triangulation vs truth by agreement | methods capture partial/different realities |
| disagreement across sources vs failure | contradiction can be analytically valuable |
| multiple analysts vs better analysis | depends on paradigm and use |
| inter-coder agreement vs universal rigor | inappropriate in reflexive TA |
