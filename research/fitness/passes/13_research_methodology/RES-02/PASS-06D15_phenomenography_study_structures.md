# 2. Canonical scientific target

Phenomenography asks:

**What are the qualitatively different ways in which a group experiences, understands, conceptualizes or makes sense of the same phenomenon, and how are those ways structurally related?**

The scientific target is:

> variation in ways of experiencing/understanding one phenomenon.

It is not primarily:

- the essence of lived experience;
- a direct summary of opinions;
- prevalence of beliefs;
- an individual psychological trait;
- a causal mechanism.

### Canonical output

The main result is:

**a parsimonious set of categories of description + the logical relations among them = an outcome space.**

---

# 3. Second-order perspective

Phenomenography is classically described as taking a **second-order perspective**.

It does not primarily ask:

> What is phenomenon X objectively?

It asks:

> In what qualitatively different ways is phenomenon X experienced or understood by people?

### Example

First-order-type question:
> What is physical-activity regulation?

Phenomenographic question:
> What are the qualitatively different ways people with knee osteoarthritis understand the regulation of their physical activity?

### Project rule

For phenomenography, the object of analysis is:

> people–phenomenon relation.

Not the phenomenon detached from experience and not the person detached from the phenomenon.

---

# 4. Phenomenography vs phenomenology

This is the central classification boundary.

## Phenomenology

Primary target:
- lived experience;
- experiential meaning;
- lifeworld;
- structure/essence/interpretation of experience, depending on lineage.

Typical question:
> What is it like to experience fear during return to sport?

## Phenomenography

Primary target:
- qualitative variation across ways of understanding/experiencing one phenomenon;
- collective categories;
- relations between categories.

Typical question:
> What qualitatively different ways do athletes understand fear during return to sport?

### Critical rule

Shared vocabulary such as:

- experience;
- perception;
- lived experience;

does not make the two methodologies interchangeable.

Phenomenography requires a **variation/outcome-space architecture**.

---

# 5. “Conception” in phenomenography

A conception is not merely:

- an opinion;
- a belief item;
- one interview answer.

It describes a qualitatively distinct way of experiencing/understanding the phenomenon.

Phenomenographic conceptions are relational:

> meaning exists in the relation between a person and the phenomenon.

### Implication

The same phenomenon can be understood differently because participants discern/focus on different aspects and organize those aspects differently.

---

# 6. Collective rather than person-typing logic

Phenomenographic categories describe:

> ways of experiencing the phenomenon.

They should not automatically be treated as stable categories of persons.

### Critical rule

Do not code:

> Participant A = Category 1 person.

A participant may:

- express more than one conception;
- shift between ways of understanding;
- change over time/context.

The category is a collective analytic construct.

---

# 7. Variation is the core signal

Phenomenographic analysis actively searches for:

- similarities;
- differences;
- contrasts;
- critical distinctions.

The goal is not to maximize the number of themes.

The goal is to identify a **limited, meaningful set of qualitatively distinct ways of understanding**.

### Example

For exercise as knee-OA treatment, different conceptions can organize exercise as:

- health gain;
- source of motivation;
- need for support;
- resistance/burden.

The analytic task is not simply listing those topics, but examining how the phenomenon is understood differently through them.

---

# 8. Referential aspect

The **referential aspect** concerns the meaning assigned to the phenomenon.

In project shorthand:

> What does the phenomenon mean in this way of experiencing it?

Example:

Physical-activity regulation may mean:

- avoiding harm;
- following expert instructions;
- negotiating symptoms;
- maintaining activity;
- developing capacity.

These are different meanings of the same phenomenon.

---

# 9. Structural aspect

The **structural aspect** concerns:

- what aspects are discerned;
- how those aspects are related;
- what is foregrounded/backgrounded;
- how the phenomenon is bounded from context.

Marton/Booth-derived work often further distinguishes:

## Internal horizon

How the parts/aspects of the phenomenon relate to each other.

## External horizon

How the phenomenon is distinguished from its context/background.

### Project rule

A strong phenomenographic analysis should not stop at labels.

It should explain **what differs structurally between ways of understanding**.

---

# 10. Categories of description

A category of description is a collective description of one qualitatively distinct way of experiencing/understanding the phenomenon.

Good categories should be:

1. meaningfully distinct;
2. related to the phenomenon;
3. supported by data;
4. logically related to other categories;
5. parsimonious as a total set.

### Critical rule

Category of description ≠ ordinary thematic category.

A phenomenographic category must participate in the architecture of variation.

---

# 11. Outcome space

The **outcome space** is the structured representation of:

- categories of description;
- dimensions distinguishing them;
- logical relations among categories.

This is the defining phenomenographic product.

### Quality criteria commonly derived from Marton/Booth

A defensible outcome space should aim for:

1. each category to reveal a distinctive way the phenomenon is experienced;
2. clear logical relationships among categories;
3. the smallest/most parsimonious category set sufficient to represent the variation.

### Rule

Themes without an outcome-space logic do not establish phenomenography.

---

# 12. Hierarchy: common but not an automatic requirement

Many phenomenographic outcome spaces are **hierarchically inclusive**:

- narrower/less complex ways;
- broader/more complex ways;
- later categories may incorporate aspects present in earlier categories.

This is common in educational and rehabilitation applications.

However, logical relations should be derived rather than imposed.

Outcome spaces may be represented as:

- hierarchy;
- continuum;
- branching structure;
- other explicit logical arrangement.

### Critical rule

Do not force every phenomenographic result into:

> low → high / bad → good.

Hierarchy must arise from structural relations in the data.

---

# 13. Hierarchy is not a moral ranking

A recurrent risk is treating less complex categories as:

- deficient people;
- wrong people;
- inferior participants.

Phenomenography aims to describe variation, not morally grade individuals.

### Preferred interpretation

A category may represent:

- narrower awareness;
- fewer discerned aspects;
- different organization of the phenomenon;

without implying lower human value or intelligence.

---

# 14. Critical aspects

Phenomenographic work may identify **critical aspects** that distinguish one conception from another.

These can be aspects a person must discern or organize differently to move toward a more comprehensive way of understanding.

### Rehabilitation example

Participation categories may differ through critical aspects such as:

- listening;
- trust;
- collaboration;
- autonomy.

### Practical value

Critical aspects can guide:

- education;
- communication;
- intervention tailoring;
- rehabilitation support.

### Critical rule

A critical aspect is not automatically a causal mediator.

It is an analytically discriminating aspect of conception.

---

# 15. Sampling logic

Phenomenographic sampling should maximize access to **variation in ways of understanding**.

Participants should generally:

1. have meaningful experience of the phenomenon;
2. differ on characteristics plausibly linked to variation.

Possible variation dimensions:

- age;
- sex;
- training experience;
- pain severity;
- fear avoidance;
- rehabilitation phase;
- role;
- setting;
- previous intervention exposure.

### Current exercise example

The 2026 knee-OA physical-activity study purposively sampled 20 adults to maximize variation in:

- sex;
- pain intensity;
- BMI;
- fear avoidance.

This is directly aligned with phenomenographic variation logic.

---

# 16. Homogeneous sampling boundary

Unlike IPA, phenomenography often benefits from heterogeneity when the aim is to map a broad range of conceptions.

But heterogeneity should remain relevant to the phenomenon.

### Rule

Do not maximize demographic variation mechanically.

The question is:

> Which differences plausibly expand the range of ways this phenomenon may be understood?

---

# 17. Sample size

There is no universal phenomenographic N.

The 2024 methodological primer notes published ranges and cites 20–30 as a practical starting heuristic in some settings.

But real health/rehabilitation phenomenographic studies include substantially different Ns:

- 9 patients;
- 16 patients;
- 20 rehabilitees;
- 20 adults with knee OA.

### Project rule

Do not encode:

> phenomenography requires 20–30 participants.

Use:

- variation captured;
- conceptual depth;
- data quality;
- information power;
- adequacy of outcome-space construction.

---

# 18. Saturation boundary

Phenomenography does not need a generic:

> no new themes

rule as its defining stopping criterion.

A stronger question is:

> Is the range and structure of qualitatively different ways of understanding sufficiently developed to support a coherent outcome space?

### Project rule

If authors use “saturation,” extract:

- what they mean;
- how they assessed it;
- whether it concerns categories/conceptions rather than generic topic repetition.

Do not import GT theoretical saturation automatically.

---

# 19. Data generation

Semi-structured interviews are most common.

But possible sources include:

- written responses;
- drawings;
- focus groups;
- observations;
- think-aloud material;
- video;
- other communicative materials.

### Critical rule

Interview ≠ phenomenography.

Data must be generated and analyzed for:

> variation in ways of experiencing/understanding.

---

# 20. Phenomenographic interviewing

Interviews generally need:

- open-ended prompts;
- concrete examples;
- follow-up probing;
- clarification;
- exploration of why/how participants understand the phenomenon.

A useful sequence can move from:

1. concrete experience/action;
2. reflection;
3. abstract meaning.

### Risk

Direct abstract questions such as:

> What is exercise regulation?

may elicit superficial textbook definitions rather than experiential conceptions.

---

# 21. Pools of meaning / collective analysis

Phenomenographic analysis often moves between:

- individual transcript meaning;
- excerpts/meaning units;
- the collective dataset.

A key step is forming a pool of meanings across participants.

### Critical rule

The final categories are collective.

They are not simply one category per participant or one “personal theme profile” per person.

---

# 22. Common seven-step analysis

Dahlgren/Fallsberg's seven-step procedure is commonly used:

1. familiarization;
2. condensation;
3. comparison;
4. grouping;
5. articulation;
6. labeling;
7. contrasting.

### Important caveat

This is a common practical analysis procedure, not the only legitimate phenomenographic analysis.

Current methodology emphasizes that analysis must also establish:

- structural relations;
- outcome-space coherence;
- distinctiveness/parsimony.

### Rule

Seven procedural steps without outcome-space reasoning are insufficient.

---

# 23. Iterative analysis

Phenomenographic analysis is iterative.

Researchers may repeatedly:

- merge categories;
- split categories;
- relabel categories;
- return to transcripts;
- reconsider critical differences;
- test category boundaries.

### Quality target

The final category set should represent the variation with minimal redundancy.

---

# 24. Themes vs dimensions vs categories

These labels must be separated.

## Theme

A recurrent topic/dimension across the data.

## Dimension of variation

An aspect along which categories differ.

## Category of description

A distinct way of understanding the phenomenon.

## Outcome space

The logical system relating categories.

### Example

Phenomenon:
rehabilitee participation.

Possible dimension:
interaction with professionals.

Categories:
- dependent participation;
- progressive participation;
- committed participation.

Outcome space:
the structured relation among these conceptions across several dimensions.

---

# 25. Structural and referential analysis

A stronger phenomenographic analysis asks both:

## Referential

What meaning does this conception assign?

## Structural

What aspects are discerned and organized in this way?

### Critical rule

A study producing only content themes may be phenomenographically incomplete even if authors use the label.

---

# 26. Variation theory relation

Phenomenography and variation theory are historically related but not identical.

Phenomenography:

> maps qualitative variation in ways of understanding.

Variation theory:

> uses patterns of variation/critical aspects as a theory of learning/instruction.

### Project rule

Do not classify every phenomenographic study as variation-theory intervention research.

Record variation theory separately if explicitly used.

---

# 27. Phenomenography vs Qualitative Description

## QD

Question:
> What barriers/facilitators do participants report?

Output:
- direct descriptive categories/themes.

## Phenomenography

Question:
> In what qualitatively different ways do participants understand the barrier/phenomenon?

Output:
- categories of description;
- dimensions;
- outcome space.

### Rule

A list of views is not automatically phenomenography.

The structural variation is essential.

---

# 28. Phenomenography vs thematic analysis

Thematic analysis may identify patterns of shared meaning.

Phenomenography identifies:

> qualitatively different ways of experiencing the same phenomenon and their logical relationship.

### Critical distinction

TA can ask:
> What themes characterize people's experiences of exercise?

Phenomenography asks:
> What different conceptions of exercise are present, and how are they structurally related?

---

# 29. Phenomenography vs grounded theory

## Phenomenography

Output:
- outcome space of conceptions.

## GT

Output:
- explanatory theory/process categories.

### Rule

A hierarchical outcome space is not a grounded theory.

It maps variation in understanding rather than generating a process theory.

---

# 30. Phenomenography vs narrative inquiry

Narrative inquiry preserves:

- story;
- plot;
- temporality;
- identity.

Phenomenography may fragment stories across the collective dataset to identify variation in conception.

### Rule

If story sequence/identity is the scientific object, narrative inquiry fits better.

If variation in understanding one phenomenon is the object, phenomenography fits better.

---

# 31. Phenomenography vs discourse analysis

Phenomenography generally treats participants' accounts as access to ways of experiencing/understanding the phenomenon.

Discursive approaches may instead analyze:

- what the account accomplishes;
- repertoires;
- subject positions;
- discourse/power.

### Critical rule

A statement can be:

- evidence of a conception in phenomenography;
- action-oriented discourse in DP.

These are different epistemic treatments of language.

---

# 32. Phenomenography vs survey of beliefs

Phenomenography is qualitative.

It does not estimate:

- proportion holding each conception;
- population prevalence of beliefs;
- probability of one conception by subgroup.

Even if authors report counts, the primary inferential product remains qualitative variation/outcome space.

### Rule

Category frequency in a purposive qualitative sample ≠ population prevalence.

---

# 33. Longitudinal phenomenography

Conceptions can change over time.

A repeated phenomenographic design could examine:

- whether categories/outcome space change;
- whether the same person's expressed conception changes;
- what critical aspects become newly discerned.

### Critical rule

A static outcome space does not automatically imply people have fixed conceptions.

Phenomenography should avoid person-essentializing.

---

# 34. Quality architecture

Phenomenographic quality should include:

1. clear phenomenon;
2. sampling capable of exposing relevant variation;
3. open data generation;
4. transparent analysis;
5. iterative comparison;
6. category distinctness;
7. evidence grounding;
8. logical relations among categories;
9. parsimonious outcome space;
10. reflexivity regarding researcher preconceptions;
11. fit between claims and second-order perspective.

### Rule

Generic checks such as kappa/member checking are not universal requirements.

Quality follows the phenomenographic analytic logic.

---

# 35. Reflexivity / bracketing boundary

Some phenomenographic guides use bracketing language to reduce premature imposition of researcher assumptions.

But complete presupposition-free analysis is not realistic.

### Project rule

Use calibrated language:

> researcher actively examines and restrains premature assumptions.

Avoid:

> bracketing eliminated bias.

This remains consistent with PASS-06D12.

---

# 36. Claims phenomenography can support

Phenomenography can strongly support claims about:

- distinct ways a phenomenon is understood;
- variation in conceptions;
- dimensions distinguishing conceptions;
- relationships among conceptions;
- critical aspects;
- possible educational/communication tailoring implications;
- different action possibilities associated with different ways of understanding.

---

# 37. Claims phenomenography cannot establish by itself

Phenomenography alone does not establish:

- population prevalence of a conception;
- incidence;
- causal treatment effect;
- effect-size magnitude;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism;
- causal mediation.

### Additional boundary

If one conception appears associated with better behavior in qualitative data, that does not prove:

> changing conception causes behavior change.

It may generate a testable hypothesis.

---

# 38. Fitness/exercise exemplar — knee OA exercise as treatment

A phenomenographic study interviewed 16 middle-aged people with knee OA after exercise-intervention exposure.

It identified different conceptions of exercise as treatment, including:

- gaining health;
- becoming motivated;
- needing support;
- experiencing resistance.

### Methodological value

The study did not ask:

> Does exercise work?

It asked:

> How is exercise-as-treatment understood differently?

The efficacy question belongs to trial/outcome evidence.

---

# 39. Fitness/exercise exemplar — physical-activity regulation in digital knee-OA rehabilitation

A 2026 study purposively sampled 20 adults with knee osteoarthritis to maximize variation.

It identified five qualitatively different ways of understanding physical-activity regulation:

- avoidance of physical activity;
- need for expert guidance;
- navigating symptoms/daily activity;
- adaptation to remain active;
- development through physical activity.

The categories differed across dimensions such as:

- meaning of physical activity;
- interpretation of pain;
- purpose of regulation;
- perception of the body.

### Why this is a canonical fitness example

It demonstrates:

- variation-focused sampling;
- one shared phenomenon;
- categories of description;
- dimensions of variation;
- action possibilities linked to conception.

---

# 40. Rehabilitation exemplar — participation

A rehabilitation study with 20 participants after six months of rehabilitation identified:

- dependent participation;
- progressive participation;
- committed participation.

Categories differed through dimensions involving:

- rehabilitation process;
- everyday life;
- interaction;
- support network.

### Methodological lesson

Phenomenography can reveal progressively broader understandings without treating participants as fixed “types”.

---

# 41. Physiotherapy exemplar — persistent low-back pain

Nine patients receiving physiotherapy after therapists' brief CFT training were interviewed phenomenographically.

Four descriptive categories included:

- “hung out to dry”;
- “stuck”;
- “making sense and taking control”;
- “holistic approach to care and living”.

### Project lesson

Small N does not automatically invalidate phenomenography when the resulting variation architecture is coherent and well supported.

But N alone cannot establish adequacy either.

---

# 42. Physiotherapy exemplar — movement awareness

Phenomenographic work has examined physiotherapists' conceptions of movement awareness.

### Fitness relevance

This supports use for questions such as:

- what does “good movement” mean to practitioners?;
- how is bodily awareness conceptualized?;
- how do coaches understand technique?;
- how do athletes understand fatigue or readiness?

### Critical caveat

These applications are conceptually plausible, but new sport-specific studies must be individually appraised rather than assumed to use true phenomenography.

---

# 43. Potential future fitness questions suited to phenomenography

Examples:

- What different ways do resistance-training beginners understand “training to failure”?
- How do coaches differently understand autoregulation?
- What different conceptions of recovery exist among strength athletes?
- How do injured athletes understand pain during return to lifting?
- What different conceptions of “good technique” exist among coaches?

### Important distinction

If the question is:

> What is the lived bodily meaning of pain?

→ phenomenology.

If:

> What different ways is pain understood as a training signal?

→ phenomenography candidate.
