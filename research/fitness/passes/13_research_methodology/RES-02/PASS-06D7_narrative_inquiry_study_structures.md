# 2. Canonical narrative-inquiry purpose

Narrative inquiry studies experience through stories and storytelling.

Its core scientific target is not simply:

> what topics did participants mention?

It asks:

> how do people organize experience into stories over time, how do those stories construct identity and meaning, how are they shaped by social/cultural narratives, and what does the telling itself accomplish?

Typical narrative questions:

- How does an athlete story an injury across time?
- How does a lifter construct an identity as disciplined, strong or resilient?
- What plots are available to people after disability or career disruption?
- How do people narrate exercise as recovery, addiction, obligation, freedom or self-care?
- How do collective gym stories shape belonging or resistance?
- How do stories change as a person moves through injury, rehabilitation and return?

### Critical rule

Narrative inquiry is not defined merely by collecting interview data.

The **story itself** must be analytically important.

---

# 3. Narrative inquiry is a family, not one fixed method

Narrative research includes multiple traditions and analytic approaches.

Common orientations include:

1. experience-centered narrative inquiry;
2. life-history / life-story research;
3. thematic narrative analysis;
4. structural narrative analysis;
5. dialogic / performance narrative analysis;
6. biographical narrative approaches;
7. collective/cultural narrative analysis;
8. longitudinal narrative inquiry;
9. autoethnographic / autobiographical narrative work.

These can overlap.

### Project rule

Do not store only:

> design = narrative.

Record:

- narrative methodology/tradition;
- unit of narrative analysis;
- analytic orientation;
- temporal architecture;
- individual vs collective/cultural focus.

# 4. Narrative data vs narrative inquiry

Many qualitative studies contain stories.

For example, a participant may tell the story of:

- an injury;
- a competition;
- a failed training plan;
- a transformation;
- recovery.

But a study using thematic analysis may fragment that account into categories such as:

- pain;
- support;
- confidence;
- motivation.

That can be valid qualitative analysis.

It is not automatically narrative inquiry.

### Narrative inquiry preserves analytically relevant features such as:

- sequence;
- chronology;
- plot;
- turning points;
- characters;
- causality as narrated;
- identity positioning;
- audience;
- tellability;
- cultural storylines;
- how the story is performed.

### Critical rule

> story present in data ≠ narrative study.

---

# 5. Narrative interview vs narrative methodology

A narrative interview is a data-generation method designed to elicit extended stories.

It can be used inside narrative inquiry.

But:

> narrative interview ≠ narrative study by itself.

Likewise, semi-structured interviews can support narrative inquiry if they elicit sufficiently extended story material and the analysis treats stories narratively.

### Project extraction

Record separately:

1. interview/data-generation format;
2. narrative methodology;
3. narrative-analysis approach.

# 6. Clandinin/Connelly three-dimensional narrative space

A foundational narrative-inquiry framework organizes experience through three commonplaces:

## Temporality

Experience has:

- past;
- present;
- future.

People and events are always in transition.

## Sociality

Includes:

- personal conditions;
- feelings;
- hopes;
- desires;
- social relationships;
- cultural/institutional conditions.

## Place

The concrete physical/topographical setting in which experience unfolds.

### Canonical project representation

narrative experience = temporality + sociality + place.

### Fitness example

An athlete's story of ACL rehabilitation can be understood through:

- past: pre-injury athletic identity;
- present: rehabilitation uncertainty;
- future: imagined return or career loss;
- sociality: coaches, clinicians, teammates, family;
- place: clinic, gym, field, competition environment.

---

# 7. Temporality is a defining strength

Narrative inquiry is especially useful when meaning depends on sequence.

Examples:

- before injury → injury → diagnosis → rehab → return;
- novice → committed trainee → burnout → disengagement;
- inactive → exercise adoption → identity change;
- athlete → injured athlete → former athlete → adapted athlete.

### Critical rule

Narrative temporality is more than ordering events by date.

The researcher asks:

- which events become turning points?;
- what is treated as beginning or ending?;
- how is the past reinterpreted from the present?;
- what future is imagined?;
- what events are omitted or compressed?;
- how does the plot make the trajectory intelligible?

---

# 8. Plot and emplotment

People do not recount every event.

They select and connect events into an intelligible story.

This process is often described as **emplotment**.

Possible narrative plots include:

- restitution/recovery;
- decline;
- chaos;
- quest/transformation;
- redemption;
- contamination;
- comeback;
- resilience;
- loss;
- identity reconstruction.

### Critical rule

A plot is not simply a theme.

Theme:

> social support.

Plot:

> I lost everything after injury, struggled through isolation, found support, rebuilt myself and returned stronger.

Narrative analysis studies how events are connected into that trajectory.

---

# 9. Turning points

Narrative studies often examine moments that reorganize a person's story.

Examples:

- first serious injury;
- medical diagnosis;
- deselection;
- first competition after rehab;
- becoming a coach;
- joining a disabled-sport community;
- first successful heavy lift after fear;
- abandoning an elite-sport goal.

### Rule

A turning point is analytically important because of how it reorganizes meaning/identity, not merely because it happened chronologically.

---

# 10. Narrative and identity

Narrative approaches often treat identity as something people construct, maintain, revise or contest through storytelling.

Athletic identity can therefore be studied as:

- continuity;
- disruption;
- reconstruction;
- resistance;
- expansion;
- foreclosure;
- transition.

### Example

After spinal-cord injury, an athlete may move among stories such as:

- "I must return to who I was";
- "sport is over, therefore I am no longer an athlete";
- "I can become a different kind of athlete";
- "my life is broader than sport."

Narrative analysis asks how these identity positions are produced and what cultural stories make them available or unavailable.

---

# 11. Individual narratives

Individual narrative inquiry can focus on:

- one person's life story;
- one episode;
- one illness/injury trajectory;
- one career transition;
- one identity transformation.

Small N can be methodologically coherent because the unit of analysis can be:

> one detailed story or narrative trajectory.

### Critical rule

Single-participant narrative inquiry is not automatically a clinical case report.

The object is the narrated experience/identity/trajectory, analyzed through narrative methodology.

---

# 12. Collective narratives

Narratives can also be shared or collectively produced.

A collective narrative can express:

- group identity;
- shared oppression;
- resistance;
- community norms;
- cultural memory;
- accepted ways of explaining experience.

Exercise exemplar:

Disabled gym users may construct a collective story that:

- validates experiences of disablism;
- creates shared understanding;
- supports affirmative identity;
- enables resistance.

### Critical rule

Collective narrative ≠ statistical majority opinion.

It is a socially available/shared story form.

---

# 13. Cultural/master narratives

Individual stories are often shaped by broader cultural narratives.

Sports examples:

- "no pain, no gain";
- comeback hero;
- warrior athlete;
- mental toughness;
- injury as test of character;
- weight loss as moral success;
- exercise as discipline;
- rehabilitation as restoration;
- elite sport as total identity.

Researchers can examine how people:

- adopt;
- resist;
- modify;
- combine;
- fail to fit;

these larger narratives.

### Claim boundary

A master narrative should not be inferred from one person's story without sufficient contextual/theoretical support.

---

# 14. Counter-narratives

Counter-narratives challenge dominant stories.

Examples:

- rejecting the idea that worth depends on athletic performance;
- reframing disability as a new embodied identity rather than failed recovery;
- rejecting gym masculinity norms;
- treating rest as disciplined training rather than weakness.

Narrative inquiry can examine:

- how counter-stories become possible;
- who can tell them;
- whether others accept them;
- what social consequences follow.

---

# 15. Tellability

Not every experience is equally easy or socially acceptable to narrate.

Tellability concerns:

- what can be told;
- to whom;
- in what setting;
- with what expected consequences.

Sport example:

An athlete may be able to tell a heroic comeback story but find it difficult to tell:

- "I was relieved my career ended";
- "I no longer want to compete";
- "training damaged my life";
- "I am afraid of pain";
- "I reject toughness culture."

### Critical rule

Silence, hesitation or narrative impossibility can itself be analytically meaningful.

---

# 16. Thematic narrative analysis

Thematic narrative analysis asks primarily:

> what is the story about?

It examines recurring content while retaining narrative context more strongly than ordinary cross-case thematic fragmentation.

### Distinction from generic thematic analysis

Generic thematic analysis can extract themes across many transcripts.

Thematic narrative analysis should preserve:

- sequence;
- case/story context;
- narrative coherence;
- who tells the story;
- how the theme functions in the story.

### Rule

"Thematic" does not automatically make the analysis non-narrative.

The distinction is whether the story remains the analytic unit.

---

# 17. Structural narrative analysis

Structural analysis asks:

> how is the story told?

Possible targets:

- beginning;
- orientation;
- complicating action;
- evaluation;
- resolution;
- coda;
- pacing;
- repetition;
- tense;
- sequencing;
- contrast;
- narrative form.

Labov-style analysis is one influential structural tradition.

### Example

Two athletes can describe the same injury events but structure them differently:

- one as a temporary obstacle in an inevitable comeback;
- one as irreversible identity collapse.

The structure itself carries meaning.

---

# 18. Dialogic / performance narrative analysis

Dialogic/performance approaches ask:

- why this story here?;
- why to this listener?;
- what identity is being performed?;
- how does the interviewer shape the account?;
- what social narratives are being invoked?;
- what does telling this story accomplish?

The story is treated as relationally produced rather than as a transparent record stored inside the participant.

### Important implication

The interviewer's questions, identity and reactions are part of the narrative event.

---

# 19. Narrative co-construction

Narrative data are not simply "retrieved" from participants.

Stories are shaped by:

- interviewer;
- audience;
- setting;
- relationship;
- cultural expectations;
- timing;
- research framing.

### Reflexivity therefore includes:

- who asked?;
- how?;
- what did participant assume about researcher?;
- what stories might be easier/harder to tell to this researcher?;
- how did follow-up questions redirect the narrative?;
- how did researcher restory the account in the final paper?

---

# 20. Re-storying

Some narrative inquiry traditions reorganize field texts into a coherent research narrative.

Re-storying may involve:

- chronology;
- journey maps;
- phases;
- turning points;
- narrative synthesis.

### Risk

Researcher can impose an overly neat plot on messy experience.

### Project rule

When re-storying is used, extract:

- original data form;
- transformation procedure;
- chronology rules;
- whether participant review was used;
- whether contradictions/ambiguities were retained.

---

# 21. Narrative unit of analysis

Possible narrative units include:

- whole life story;
- extended story;
- episode;
- injury story;
- career story;
- repeated story over time;
- collective narrative;
- cultural/master narrative.

### Critical rule

The unit must be explicit.

If every sentence is coded independently across participants, the study may no longer be operating narratively even if it began with story elicitation.

---

# 22. Narrative sampling

Narrative studies commonly use purposive sampling for participants who have:

- lived the target trajectory;
- experienced a transition;
- sufficient story access;
- relevant identity/context.

Sampling may seek:

- depth;
- contrast;
- trajectory variation;
- different story types;
- different social positions.

### Small samples

Small samples are often coherent because analysis can be case-intensive.

But adequacy depends on:

- narrative depth;
- analytic ambition;
- diversity needed;
- number of trajectories;
- whether collective/cultural claims are made.

### Rule

Small N does not justify broad collective/master-narrative claims automatically.

---

# 23. Narrative data sources

Narrative inquiry is not limited to interviews.

Possible sources:

- life-history interviews;
- narrative interviews;
- repeated interviews;
- diaries;
- journals;
- letters;
- blogs;
- social media posts;
- photographs with elicitation;
- artwork;
- poetry;
- field notes;
- autobiographies;
- video/audio;
- researcher-participant conversations;
- timelines/annals.

### Rule

A source is useful when it supports reconstruction/analysis of storied experience.

---

# 24. Longitudinal narrative inquiry

Narrative inquiry is naturally suited to temporal change.

A longitudinal narrative design can examine how a person's story changes across repeated time points.

Possible structures:

- prospective repeated interviews;
- diary + interviews;
- interview before/after transition;
- multi-year life-story follow-up;
- repeated story reconstruction.

### Scientific target

Not merely:

> did the participant's opinion change?

But:

> how was the person's story and identity reconfigured over time?

---

# 25. Retrospective narrative vs prospective longitudinal narrative

## Retrospective

Participant reconstructs a past trajectory from the present.

Strength:
- whole-trajectory meaning.

Limitation:
- current identity reshapes memory and plot.

## Prospective longitudinal

Stories are collected across unfolding events.

Strength:
- captures shifts before final outcome is known;
- reduces hindsight smoothing;
- reveals changing future expectations.

Limitation:
- resource intensive;
- research relationship itself evolves.

### Project rule

Record temporal architecture separately.

---

# 26. Narrative change is not objective event change

A person's narrative can change even if the underlying historical events do not.

Example:

At month 2:
> injury ruined my future.

At year 2:
> injury forced me into a healthier career.

Both stories can refer to the same event sequence.

Narrative inquiry studies the changing meaning.

### Critical rule

Do not interpret narrative transformation as proof that objective outcomes changed in the same direction.

---

# 27. Narrative causality vs causal identification

Stories often contain causal language:

- "because";
- "that made me";
- "this is why";
- "everything changed after".

Narrative analysis can study how participants construct causal explanations.

But:

> narrated causality ≠ identified causal effect.

Example:

"I stayed active because my training group supported me"

supports:

> perceived/narrated causal meaning.

It does not estimate:

> causal effect of group support on adherence.

---

# 28. Narrative coherence and truth

A coherent story is not automatically factually accurate.

Narrative inquiry is often interested in:

- meaning;
- identity;
- social function;
- narrative form.

Memory can be:

- selective;
- reconstructed;
- socially shaped.

### Critical rule

Do not treat narrative coherence as factual verification.

If historical factual accuracy matters, triangulate with:

- records;
- timelines;
- documents;
- observations;
- other informants.

---

# 29. Narrative inquiry vs phenomenology

## Narrative inquiry

Primary target:
- story;
- temporality;
- plot;
- identity;
- telling;
- cultural narrative.

## Phenomenology

Primary target:
- lived-experience structure/meaning.

Example:

What is it like to feel fear when returning to sport?
→ phenomenology.

How does an athlete story the journey from injury through fear to return, and how does that story reconstruct identity?
→ narrative inquiry.

They can overlap but should not be collapsed.

---

# 30. Narrative inquiry vs grounded theory

## Narrative inquiry

Preserves trajectories/stories and asks how experience is storied.

## Grounded theory

Abstracts across data to build explanatory categories/theory of process.

Example:

Narrative:
How do athletes narrate the transition from injury to retirement?

GT:
What process explains movement from injury disruption to retirement adaptation?

Both can study transitions, but the analytic unit differs.

---

# 31. Narrative inquiry vs qualitative description

QD asks:

What did participants report?

Narrative inquiry asks:

How did participants organize those experiences into stories, what plots and identities were constructed, and what did those stories accomplish?

A barrier/facilitator list is not narrative analysis.

---

# 32. Narrative inquiry vs case study

A narrative inquiry may center on one person.

That does not automatically make it a case study.

## Narrative

Unit:
story/trajectory.

## Case study

Unit:
bounded case/system in context.

Possible hybrid:

narrative case study of one athlete's career transition.

Project rule:

Record both layers when both genuinely structure the research.

---

# 33. Narrative inquiry vs autoethnography

Autoethnography often uses the researcher's own experience to connect self with culture.

It can be highly narrative.

But:

> narrative inquiry ≠ automatically autoethnography.

Autoethnography has a distinct researcher-as-subject architecture and will be checked further in later qualitative gap audit.

---

# 34. Individual vs collective/cultural narrative claims

## Individual-level claim

This participant constructed injury as a redemption story.

## Cross-case narrative pattern

Several participants used similar comeback plots.

## Collective narrative

A group jointly/sharedly constructs a story that supports group identity.

## Cultural/master narrative

A broader socially available narrative provides a template for individual stories.

### Rule

Each step expands the inferential level.

Do not move from one personal account to "sport culture's master narrative" without supporting evidence.

# 36. Sports/exercise exemplar: disability and sport

Smith and Sparkes studied narratives after sport-related spinal-cord injury.

Methodological relevance:

- story form and tellability mattered;
- identity was analyzed over time;
- dominant cultural recovery narratives shaped what could be told;
- bodily change challenged available plots.

This is not reducible to a list of themes such as:

- injury;
- identity;
- recovery.

The narrative architecture itself is the evidence.

---

# 37. Sports/exercise exemplar: collective gym stories

A 2017 study of disabled people exercising in peer groups used dialogical narrative analysis.

The key result was a collective story that:

- validated oppression experiences;
- created shared understanding;
- supported affirmative identity;
- enabled resistance to disablism.

Methodological lesson:

> collective narrative is a social resource, not merely a frequency count of similar opinions.

# 39. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| story in interview vs narrative inquiry | story must be an analytic object |
| narrative interview vs narrative methodology | interview is data generation |
| theme vs plot | theme is content; plot organizes events |
| chronology vs narrative temporality | narrative asks how time is interpreted/structured |
| turning point vs any major event | turning point reorganizes story/identity |
| personal story vs collective narrative | different inferential levels |
| repeated similar stories vs master narrative | cultural-level claim needs stronger support |
| thematic narrative analysis vs generic TA | narrative analysis preserves story/case context |
| structural analysis vs content analysis | asks how story is formed, not only what it says |
| dialogic analysis vs transcript-as-container | telling is relational/performed |
| narrative coherence vs factual accuracy | coherent story is not verification |
| narrated causality vs causal identification | participant explanation is not effect estimation |
| narrative change vs objective outcome change | meaning can change without event change |
| single-person narrative vs case report | narrative methodology vs clinical reporting |
| narrative inquiry vs phenomenology | story/temporality vs lived-experience structure |
| narrative inquiry vs GT | story trajectory vs abstract process theory |
| narrative inquiry vs QD | storied meaning vs descriptive summary |
| small N vs weak design | depth can justify small samples |
| small N vs broad cultural claim | inferential level still matters |

## HIGH

Well-supported:

- narrative inquiry studies stories/storying rather than merely topics;
- narrative research is a family, not one analysis method;
- temporality is central;
- Clandinin/Connelly framework includes temporality, sociality and place;
- Riessman-style narrative analysis can be thematic, structural and dialogic/performance-oriented;
- narrative inquiry can analyze identity construction and cultural storylines;
- researcher/interviewer participates in story production;
- narrative evidence does not establish prevalence or causal effect by itself.

## MODERATE-HIGH

Project routing choices:

- exact individual → collective → master-narrative inferential ladder;
- threshold distinguishing thematic narrative analysis from ordinary thematic analysis;
- standardized extraction router;
- how strongly "whole story preservation" should be required across narrative traditions.

Reason:

narrative inquiry is highly plural, definitions of story differ, and some traditions intentionally combine narrative and thematic procedures.
