# 2. Canonical scientific / change target

Appreciative Inquiry asks:

**What gives life to this system/practice when it is functioning at its best, what conditions enable those strengths, what preferred future can be imagined from them, and how can people collaboratively design and enact more of those life-giving conditions?**

Its target is not merely description.

Canonical AI combines:

- inquiry;
- collective sense-making;
- future imagination;
- organizational/practice change.

### Canonical project representation

affirmative topic
+
inquiry into strengths / peak experience / life-giving conditions
+
shared stories
+
preferred-future imagination
+
co-construction of design
+
action / destiny / delivery
+
ongoing generative inquiry.

### Critical rule

AI is not simply:

> “ask positive questions.”

A full methodology links affirmative inquiry to generative interpretation and change-oriented design/action.

---

# 3. Genealogy

Appreciative Inquiry was developed by David Cooperrider and Suresh Srivastva in the 1980s.

The 1987 foundational work presented AI as a refiguration/complement to conventional action research and problem-solving organizational inquiry.

Its early conceptual basis is better described through:

- sociorationalist thinking;
- social constructionist orientation;
- organizational development;
- action research;
- generative theory.

### Historical caution

AI **predates modern positive psychology** as a formal field.

Later applied literature, especially sport/organizational practice, often connects AI with:

- positive organizational psychology;
- strengths-based practice;
- flourishing.

### Project rule

Do not rewrite AI's historical origin as:

> “a method derived from positive psychology.”

Positive psychology is a later compatible influence/adjacent framework, not its original source.

---

# 4. Problem-solving critique

Cooperrider/Srivastva challenged the assumption that organizational inquiry should begin by asking:

> What is wrong?

or:

> What problem must be fixed?

They argued that inquiry itself helps construct what becomes visible and actionable.

### AI alternative

Ask about:

- strengths;
- exceptional moments;
- life-giving conditions;
- valued capabilities;
- desired futures.

### Critical rule

AI does not logically require pretending that problems do not exist.

It changes the **starting frame and generative question**.

---

# 5. Affirmative topic choice

An AI project begins by choosing what is worth inquiring into.

Examples:

Deficit frame:
> Why is athlete communication failing?

Affirmative frame:
> When does athlete–staff communication work at its best, and how can those conditions be expanded?

### Scientific importance

The topic choice is not neutral.

It determines:

- stories elicited;
- attention;
- emotional tone;
- possibilities participants imagine.

### Project extraction

Record:

- who defined the topic?;
- whether it was genuinely affirmative?;
- whether participants shaped it?;
- whether deficit/problems were excluded or reframed?;
- whether the topic was meaningful to affected stakeholders.

---

# 6. Positive core

AI often seeks the system's **positive core**:

- strengths;
- resources;
- successful practices;
- values;
- relationships;
- capabilities;
- life-giving forces.

### Critical boundary

Positive core ≠ list of compliments.

It should be empirically grounded in:

- actual examples;
- stories;
- observed practices;
- conditions that enabled success.

---

# 7. Original 4D cycle

The classic widely used AI cycle is:

1. **Discovery**;
2. **Dream**;
3. **Design**;
4. **Destiny**.

Some literature uses:

> Delivery

instead of Destiny.

### Historical rule

The 4D model predates widespread use of the 5D model.

Do not retroactively call every early AI study a 5D study.

---

# 8. Later 5D cycle

A later commonly used version adds:

1. **Define**;
2. Discovery;
3. Dream;
4. Design;
5. Destiny/Delivery.

### Define

Clarifies:

- affirmative topic;
- inquiry focus;
- boundaries;
- project purpose;
- participants/stakeholders.

### Critical rule

Define is a later explicit addition.

Its absence in an older 4D study is not automatically a fidelity failure.

---

# 9. Discovery — “best of what is”

Discovery investigates:

- peak moments;
- successful experiences;
- valued relationships;
- effective practices;
- enabling conditions.

Common data-generation methods:

- appreciative interviews;
- paired conversations;
- group storytelling;
- written accounts;
- observations;
- documents.

### Critical rule

Discovery is not:

> “participants list strengths.”

A stronger Discovery phase elicits concrete stories and conditions behind success.

---

# 10. Discovery stories as data

Peak/success stories can reveal:

- relational processes;
- organizational resources;
- tacit practices;
- leadership behaviors;
- environmental conditions.

### Example in sport

Ask:

> Tell me about a time the athlete-support system worked exceptionally well.

Then probe:

- What happened?
- Who was involved?
- What made it possible?
- What should be preserved?

### Rule

Claims should remain traceable to actual stories/data rather than positivity slogans.

---

# 11. Dream — “what might be”

Dream uses the Discovery material to imagine a preferred future.

Participants may create:

- narratives;
- images;
- metaphors;
- scenarios;
- future statements;
- shared visions.

### Critical rule

Dream should be **generative but grounded**.

It should build from:

> what has been shown possible / valuable

rather than arbitrary wishful thinking.

---

# 12. Design — “what should be”

Design translates the preferred future into:

- structures;
- relationships;
- processes;
- policies;
- practices;
- roles;
- commitments.

A common AI device is the **provocative proposition / possibility statement**:

> a challenging but plausible statement describing the desired organization/practice as if its best potential were realized.

### Critical rule

Design is more than brainstorming.

It should connect:

Discovery evidence
+
Dream vision
→
concrete social/organizational architecture.

---

# 13. Destiny / Delivery — “what will be”

Destiny/Delivery concerns:

- implementation;
- experimentation;
- commitments;
- sustaining change;
- learning;
- adaptation.

Later AI practice often emphasizes:

- self-organized action;
- distributed ownership;
- ongoing learning rather than one top-down implementation plan.

### Critical rule

A study that stops after interviews or Dream does not demonstrate full action/change-cycle fidelity.

It may still be a legitimate **partial AI / AI-informed study**.

---

# 14. 4D/5D phases are not rigid standardized procedures

The 2013 healthcare methodological review found substantial adaptation across studies.

The 2024 integrative review similarly found:

- varied purposes;
- varied adherence to 4D;
- modified models;
- positive effects reported even when all phases were not followed.

### Project rule

Use a **fidelity/profile approach**, not binary purity.

Record:

- which phases were implemented;
- how;
- who participated;
- whether action occurred.

---

# 15. Five original AI principles

The five commonly recognized original principles are:

1. **Constructionist Principle**;
2. **Simultaneity Principle**;
3. **Poetic Principle**;
4. **Anticipatory Principle**;
5. **Positive Principle**.

Later literature has proposed additional/emergent principles.

### Project rule

Store original five separately from later additions.

Do not imply all current principles were part of the earliest 1987 formulation in their later named form.

---

# 16. Constructionist Principle

Core idea:

> language, interaction and shared interpretation participate in constructing organizational/social reality.

### Implication

The way a research question is framed changes:

- what people notice;
- what they discuss;
- what futures become thinkable.

### Critical rule

This is not evidence that material/structural constraints are unreal.

Strong AI must still account for:

- resources;
- policies;
- hierarchy;
- environment;
- institutional constraints.

---

# 17. Simultaneity Principle

Core idea:

> inquiry and change are not sequentially separate.

The moment questions are asked, participants may begin to:

- notice different things;
- reinterpret history;
- alter expectations;
- behave differently.

### Methodological consequence

Data collection itself can be intervention-like.

### Critical rule

This complicates any claim that an AI interview is a neutral measurement of a pre-existing state.

---

# 18. Inquiry–change simultaneity

This is one of AI's major distinctions.

Traditional representation:

research
→
findings
→
intervention/change.

AI representation:

inquiry
↔
meaning construction
↔
change.

### Project implication

A study can legitimately treat:

- conversations;
- storytelling;
- future imagination;

as both:

- data generation;
- early change process.

### Causal boundary

This does not identify the **causal effect size** of the inquiry.

---

# 19. Poetic Principle

Core idea:

> organizations/human systems contain many possible stories and topics; what we choose to study matters.

### Implication

The system is not reducible to:

- its failures;
- one dominant problem narrative.

### Critical rule

Poetic openness should not become selective blindness.

Relevant negative/contradictory experiences may still matter.

---

# 20. Anticipatory Principle

Core idea:

> images of the future influence present action.

Dream/future imagery is therefore not a decorative stage.

It is intended to:

- orient motivation;
- create direction;
- make alternative action conceivable.

### Critical boundary

A compelling future image is not proof that the future outcome will occur.

---

# 21. Positive Principle

Core idea:

> positive questions, affect, relationships and hope can support engagement in change.

### Critical rule

Positive ≠ compulsory optimism.

High-fidelity AI should not use positivity to:

- silence harm;
- erase conflict;
- suppress criticism;
- blame people for “being negative.”

---

# 22. Strengths-based ≠ problem denial

A sophisticated AI process can acknowledge:

- injury;
- conflict;
- organizational failure;
- discrimination;
- burnout;
- unsafe practice.

The methodological shift is to ask generative questions such as:

> What would safe, trusting practice look like?

> Where have we already seen even partial examples of it?

> What conditions would allow more of it?

### Rule

Serious problems can enter AI as:

- context;
- contrast;
- “shadow” material;
- conditions requiring redesign.

---

# 23. Positivity bias / “shadow” problem

Critical literature identifies a central risk:

> participants may feel pressure to tell positive stories while difficult or dissenting experiences become unspeakable.

Potential consequences:

- suppressed conflict;
- marginalized voices;
- false harmony;
- incomplete diagnosis of structural harm.

### Project extraction

Record:

- whether negative material was allowed;
- whether dissent was documented;
- whether harms/power were explicitly explored;
- whether the facilitator redirected criticism too aggressively.

---

# 24. Authentic appreciation

Critical adaptations argue that appreciation can include:

- honest recognition of pain;
- constraints;
- contradiction;
- “what depletes life”;

without abandoning generative orientation.

### Project rule

Do not score a study as “less AI” merely because it permits critical/negative experiences.

The stronger question is:

> Does it use them generatively without suppressing reality?

---

# 25. Participation

AI is often collaborative, but participation depth varies greatly.

Possible roles:

- interview participant only;
- storyteller;
- workshop participant;
- co-interpreter;
- co-designer;
- implementation partner;
- system-wide stakeholder.

### Critical rule

AI label does not automatically imply:

- PAR-level governance;
- CBPR partnership;
- equal decision power.

Use PASS-06D8 decision-right fields.

---

# 26. Whole-system / summit variants

AI can be conducted as large-system inquiry involving broad stakeholder representation.

Possible format:

- leaders;
- employees;
- service users;
- community members;
- partners;

working through 4D/5D phases together.

### Critical rule

Large participation does not automatically mean equitable power.

Formal hierarchy can remain influential.

---

# 27. Power

Current sport-AI guidance explicitly warns about power differentials such as:

- athlete vs selector;
- athlete vs coach;
- staff vs senior management/ownership.

### Risks

Participants may:

- self-censor;
- defer to authority;
- agree publicly while dissenting privately.

### Quality questions

- Who chose the affirmative topic?
- Who could challenge leaders?
- Was psychological safety established?
- Whose Dream shaped Design?
- Who controlled final implementation?

---

# 28. Collaboration ≠ democracy

AI frequently uses collaborative language.

But collaboration can coexist with:

- unequal authority;
- unequal consequences;
- unequal resources.

### Critical rule

Do not infer shared governance merely because people participated in an AI workshop.

Store:

- actual decision rights;
- veto power;
- resource control;
- implementation ownership.

---

# 29. Data-generation methods are flexible

AI can use:

- appreciative interviews;
- paired interviews;
- focus/group conversations;
- written narratives;
- observations;
- workshops;
- visual/future exercises;
- surveys;
- quantitative measures.

### Major taxonomy point

AI is **not intrinsically restricted to qualitative data**.

Its methodology/change architecture may be embedded in:

- qualitative studies;
- mixed methods;
- quasi-experiments;
- organizational interventions.

### Project rule

When classifying a study, preserve:

AI methodology/process
+
actual data/design architecture.

---

# 30. Analysis methods are not fixed

Applied AI studies may use:

- thematic analysis;
- qualitative content analysis;
- narrative synthesis;
- group sense-making;
- frequency summaries;
- mixed-method evaluation.

### Critical rule

Thematic analysis is not the AI methodology itself.

Store separately:

AI inquiry/change architecture
+
downstream analysis method.

---

# 31. Generativity

A key AI quality concept is **generativity**:

> does the inquiry produce new possibilities, interpretations, conversations and actions that the system could not easily see before?

### Strong generative signs

- new language;
- changed relationships;
- novel options;
- self-organized commitments;
- redesigned practices.

### Critical rule

Positive participant satisfaction is not the same as generativity.

---

# 32. Quality architecture

Strong AI should demonstrate:

1. clear affirmative topic;
2. theoretical/methodological AI lineage;
3. stories/data about actual strengths or peak functioning;
4. transparent 4D/5D implementation;
5. grounded Discovery;
6. generative Dream;
7. Design linked to Discovery/Dream;
8. Destiny/Delivery or clear explanation if omitted;
9. meaningful stakeholder participation;
10. attention to dissent/power/negative experience;
11. transparent adaptations;
12. traceability from stories → themes/positive core → design;
13. distinction between inquiry outcome and implemented change;
14. evaluation proportional to claimed impact;
15. reflexivity about facilitator/researcher role.

# 34. AI vs generic strengths-based interviewing

A researcher may ask:

> What worked well?

That is not enough.

Full AI requires a broader generative/change logic:

Discovery
→
preferred future
→
design
→
action.

### Rule

Strengths-focused interview ≠ Appreciative Inquiry automatically.

---

# 35. AI vs positive psychology

## Positive psychology

Studies constructs/interventions such as:

- strengths;
- positive affect;
- meaning;
- flourishing;
- gratitude.

## Appreciative Inquiry

Is an organizational/social inquiry and change process.

### Critical rule

AI can use positive-psychology ideas, but:

positive psychology intervention
≠
AI

unless affirmative/generative inquiry architecture is present.

---

# 36. AI vs generic action research

AI grew from/reframed action-research traditions.

Both can involve:

- inquiry;
- action;
- iteration;
- organizational change.

### AI-specific architecture

- affirmative topic;
- strengths/positive core;
- 4D/5D process;
- generative/future-oriented inquiry;
- simultaneity principle.

### Rule

AI is a distinct action/inquiry methodology, not a synonym for action research.

---

# 37. AI vs Participatory Action Research

PAR emphasizes:

- participation;
- power;
- action;
- often emancipatory/justice aims.

AI may be participatory but does not inherently require:

- marginalized-group governance;
- emancipatory politics;
- PAR-level co-ownership.

### Hybrid representation

PAR
+
AI process.

Preserve both if explicitly used.

---

# 38. AI vs Co-operative Inquiry

## Co-operative Inquiry

Distinctive features:
- all members co-researchers/co-subjects;
- four ways of knowing;
- repeated action/reflection cycles;
- Heron/Reason validity architecture.

## AI

Distinctive features:
- affirmative topic;
- Discovery–Dream–Design–Destiny;
- positive core;
- anticipatory/generative principles.

### Rule

Both can be participatory/action-oriented, but they are not interchangeable.

---

# 39. AI vs co-design

Co-design focuses on jointly creating:

- intervention;
- service;
- product;
- process.

AI's Discovery and Dream may precede/co-produce a Design phase.

### Correct hybrid

AI inquiry
→
co-design process
→
implementation/evaluation.

### Rule

A Design workshop is not enough to classify the whole project as AI if Discovery/Dream/affirmative logic is absent.

---

# 40. AI vs Experience-Based Co-Design

EBCD uses:

- patient/staff experience;
- touchpoints;
- joint redesign.

AI instead foregrounds:

- positive core;
- life-giving stories;
- preferred future.

Both can create service change.

### Rule

Do not merge them under one strengths-based redesign category.

Preserve lineage/process.

---

# 41. AI vs solution-focused practice

Both may avoid prolonged deficit analysis.

But solution-focused practice has its own:

- therapeutic/coaching lineage;
- exception questions;
- preferred-future methods.

AI's canonical unit is typically:

- organization;
- team;
- community/system;

with 4D/5D generative change architecture.

### Rule

Solution-focused questioning ≠ AI automatically.

---

# 42. AI vs program evaluation

AI can be used evaluatively to ask:

- what worked well?;
- what should continue?;
- what future should be built?

But AI evaluation is not automatically a design capable of estimating:

- effect size;
- counterfactual impact.

### Project rule

Store:

evaluation purpose
+
AI inquiry method
+
separate quantitative/causal design if present.

---

# 43. Inquiry itself may influence reported data

Because AI deliberately treats inquiry as intervention, participant responses may change through:

- positive framing;
- group energy;
- future imagining;
- facilitator influence.

### Critical implication

An AI interview cannot be interpreted as if it neutrally sampled an unchanged latent attitude.

### Rule

This is a methodology feature, not necessarily bias.

But it limits claims about pre-inquiry state.

---

# 44. Social desirability / positivity demand

Affirmative framing can produce demand characteristics.

Participants may infer that researchers want:

- success stories;
- optimism;
- consensus.

### Quality safeguards

- explicitly permit criticism;
- invite “what made success difficult?”;
- ask about conditions that block the positive core;
- preserve minority/dissenting voices;
- conduct private as well as group elicitation when hierarchy is strong.

---

# 45. Implementation outcome vs research finding

Possible AI outputs include:

- shared themes;
- positive core;
- preferred-future vision;
- provocative propositions;
- redesigned processes;
- action commitments;
- organizational initiatives.

### Critical rule

These operate at different levels.

A report should not collapse:

> “participants designed a new process”

into:

> “the process improved outcomes.”

Effectiveness requires evaluation.

---

# 46. Claims AI can support

AI can support claims about:

- participant-defined strengths;
- successful practices;
- conditions enabling effective functioning;
- shared aspirations;
- stakeholder priorities;
- preferred futures;
- co-designed organizational/practice changes;
- participant experience of the inquiry;
- locally observed change processes;
- generative shifts in language/relationships.

---

# 47. Claims AI cannot establish by itself

AI alone does not establish:

- population prevalence;
- incidence;
- average treatment effect;
- intervention effectiveness;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism;
- causal mediation.

### Important boundary

AI may itself be an intervention.

But an AI intervention requires:

- controlled/quasi-experimental/other appropriate evaluation

to estimate its causal effect.

---

# 48. Current causal-evidence boundary

The 2022 healthcare systematic review noted that AI evidence was dominated by positive qualitative/observational reports and was vulnerable to:

- positivity bias;
- publication bias;
- implementer–evaluator overlap.

Current 2026 workplace/healthcare literature is beginning to use:

- quasi-experiments;
- experimental comparisons.

### Project implication

Keep two evidence questions separate:

1. How does AI work as methodology/change process?
2. Does AI causally improve outcome X?

D28 answers question 1.

Question 2 belongs to experimental/quasi-experimental appraisal.

---

# 49. Sport exemplar — physical-literacy collaboration

Morgan et al. used AI as a methodological approach among:

- a national sport/physical-activity organization;
- a national governing body for sport;
- a local-authority sport/leisure department;
- university consultants.

Over six months, the study focused on:

- Discovery;
- Dream;
- Design.

Data included:

- AI conversations;
- written accounts;
- observation;
- collaborative meeting/actions.

### Methodological value

This is a direct sport/physical-activity example of **partial-cycle AI**.

### Critical lesson

The study did not require completed Destiny to be methodologically informative, but it should not be represented as a full 4D cycle.

Practitioners included:

- technical/S&C coaches;
- physiologists;
- physiotherapists.

The study used:

- appreciative inquiry;
- social-constructionist framing;
- interviews;
- inductive reflexive thematic analysis.

### Methodological lesson

This is directly relevant to:

- coaching;
- S&C;
- physiology;
- physiotherapy;
- athlete-centered delivery.

### Taxonomy lesson

AI inquiry architecture
+
reflexive thematic analysis

should be stored as separate layers.

# 51. Sport exemplar — organizational practice, 2025

Staten et al. provide a current AI application framework for sport organizations and sport/exercise/performance psychology practitioners.

Key current practice recommendations include:

- ask provocative/open questions;
- anticipate power differentials;
- promote psychological safety;
- support implementation;
- embed AI into organizational routines.

### Evidence caveat

The 2025 paper is primarily a practice/methodological application article drawing on authors' experience.

It reports **no new empirical dataset**.

### Rule

Use it as current sport-methodology guidance, not as evidence of AI effectiveness.

# 52. Sport organizational power

The 2025 sport paper explicitly notes power relations such as:

- athletes vs selectors;
- staff vs management/ownership.

### Project implication

In performance environments, AI positivity can be unsafe if:

- selection consequences;
- contract status;
- hierarchy;

make dissent costly.

### Quality requirement

Sport AI should report how:

- psychological safety;
- confidentiality;
- mixed-status facilitation;
- dissent;

were handled.

# 53. Candidate fitness / strength / rehabilitation questions suited to AI

Examples:

- When does coach–athlete communication work exceptionally well, and how can those conditions be expanded?
- What makes rehabilitation-to-training handoffs work at their best across physio, S&C and coaching staff?
- When do athletes feel most ownership over load-management decisions?
- What conditions allow an S&C department to integrate athlete feedback without sacrificing performance standards?
- What does an ideal recovery culture look like when built from athletes' best existing experiences?
- How can a gym preserve the practices that make novice lifters feel safe, competent and included?

### Critical condition

If the objective is merely:

> describe barriers

without generative/action architecture, ordinary qualitative methods may fit better.

# 54. Potential strength-training application

AI is especially plausible for system-level questions such as:

- team culture;
- coaching quality;
- adherence-support systems;
- rehab collaboration;
- athlete-service organization.

It is less naturally suited as a parent methodology for narrowly mechanistic questions such as:

- hypertrophy signaling;
- force–velocity physiology;
- dose-response estimation.

### Rule

Methodology must match the scientific object.

# 55. Sample size

AI does not have one universal participant N.

Possible designs range from:

- small interview studies;
- team workshops;
- large whole-system summits.

Adequacy depends on:

- system boundaries;
- stakeholder diversity;
- power structure;
- intended design/action scope.

### Critical rule

AI sample adequacy is more about:

> whether relevant voices/system parts were included

than generic thematic-saturation numbers alone.

# 57. Data sufficiency

Useful adequacy questions:

- Were enough peak stories collected to identify enabling conditions?
- Were contradictory contexts explored?
- Were marginalized voices included?
- Did Discovery produce a grounded positive core?
- Was Dream clearly connected to Discovery?
- Did Design specify actionable organizational/practice features?
- Was Destiny/Delivery followed enough to support action claims?

### Rule

Generic “no new themes” is not sufficient as the sole AI adequacy criterion.

# 59. AI-informed interview-only studies

An interview study may explicitly use appreciative framing but stop before Dream/Design/Destiny.

Correct classification may be:

> AI-informed qualitative study

> full Appreciative Inquiry change cycle.

### Critical rule

Preserve the methodological contribution of affirmative/generative questioning without overstating action fidelity.

# 60. Positive psychology boundary in sport

Current sport literature often situates AI alongside positive organizational psychology.

But AI differs because it is not just measuring/intervening on:

- optimism;
- gratitude;
- resilience;
- strengths.

It organizes a collaborative system inquiry.

### Project rule

A gratitude intervention or strengths questionnaire is not AI.

# 64. Confidence

## HIGH

Well-supported:

- AI originates with Cooperrider/Srivastva in organizational development/action-research critique;
- original AI predates modern positive psychology;
- AI uses an affirmative/generative rather than deficit-first inquiry logic;
- the classic cycle is Discovery–Dream–Design–Destiny, with Define later added in common 5D models;

AI literature intentionally permits substantial adaptation, and the same label is used for world view, research methodology, facilitation approach, organizational intervention and evaluation tool.

# 66. Status after PASS-06D28

## Established

1. Appreciative Inquiry is a distinct Cooperrider/Srivastva-derived generative inquiry-and-change methodology.
2. Its historical roots are action research/organizational development and social-constructionist/sociorationalist thinking; positive psychology is a later adjacent influence rather than the original source.
3. AI begins with an affirmative topic and investigates strengths, peak functioning and life-giving conditions.
4. The classic cycle is Discovery → Dream → Design → Destiny; Define is a later common fifth D.
5. Discovery must be grounded in concrete stories/conditions rather than generic praise.
6. Dream constructs a preferred future from Discovery rather than arbitrary optimism.
7. Design converts the positive core/future vision into social/organizational architecture.
8. Destiny/Delivery concerns implementation, ownership, learning and adaptation.
9. The five original named principles are constructionist, simultaneity, poetic, anticipatory and positive.
10. The simultaneity principle means data generation/inquiry may itself function as change.
11. Strengths-based orientation does not require suppressing harms, problems or dissent.
12. Positivity bias, “shadow” suppression, hierarchy and collaboration-without-democracy are major quality risks.
13. AI participation depth must be measured through actual decision rights rather than assumed from collaborative language.
14. AI is not intrinsically qualitative; it can coexist with thematic analysis, mixed methods or quasi-experimental evaluation.
15. AI must be separated from generic strengths interviewing, positive psychology, generic action research, PAR, Co-operative Inquiry, co-design/EBCD and solution-focused work.
16. Full-cycle AI and AI-informed/partial-cycle designs should be stored separately through a fidelity profile.
17. Direct sport evidence includes physical-literacy organizational collaboration and British Sailing Team research involving coaches, S&C, physiologists, physiotherapists and elite athletes.
18. Current 2025 sport-organizational guidance confirms direct relevance and highlights power/psychological-safety requirements, but is methodological/practice guidance rather than new effectiveness evidence.
19. AI supports strengths/generative/organizational-change claims, not prevalence or causal treatment-effect estimates without a separate evaluation design.
