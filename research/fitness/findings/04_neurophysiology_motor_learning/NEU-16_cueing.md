# NEU-16 — Cueing

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-19**  
Область: 04 — Neurophysiology / Motor Learning

## 0. Core decision

Cueing should not be reduced to one universal verbal formula.

A defensible project model is:

`task information need`
+
`learner capacity/interpretation`
+
`cue semantics`
+
`focus direction`
+
`process/outcome target`
+
`current error/goal`
+
`ability to perform without cue`
→
`cue strategy`.

Project rule:

**use the minimum information that is sufficient to specify the task-relevant relation the learner currently needs, then verify that the cue changes the intended variable and that the skill can later be expressed without it.**

---

## 1. One cue vs multiple cues

No universal optimal cue count is established.

Multiple simultaneous explicit rules can increase working-memory demand.

Evidence shows that learners with lower working-memory capacity can be harmed by heavy explicit instruction load.

Project rule:

**“one cue at a time” is a practical low-load default, not a biological law.**

---

## 2. Cue brevity

Shorter is not automatically better.

A brief cue can reduce:

- verbal working-memory demand;
- rehearsal demand;
- execution-phase micromanagement.

But a cue can also be too vague to communicate the needed relation.

Project rule:

**compress wording only while preserving enough information to guide the target action.**

---

## 3. Cue specificity

Specificity means precision of relevant information.

It does not mean:

- more words;
- more joint angles;
- one exact movement geometry.

Project rule:

**be specific about the task relation that matters, not unnecessarily prescriptive about every degree of freedom.**

---

## 4. External vs internal cue

No universal external-cue superiority is retained.

External cues can be useful for many performance goals.

Internal cues can be useful when:

- body position matters;
- proprioceptive information is directly relevant;
- movement form itself is the target;
- muscle recruitment is the explicit goal.

Project rule:

**choose focus direction according to the information needed and outcome being optimized.**

---

## 5. Analogy cues

Analogy cues compress multiple relations into a metaphor or image.

Current evidence supports potential benefits for:

- performance;
- retention;
- some stress conditions;
- novice/child learning.

But:

- heterogeneity is substantial;
- risk of bias is high;
- superiority is not universal.

Project rule:

**use an analogy when it efficiently communicates a useful movement relation; verify its effect rather than assuming it works.**

---

## 6. Analogy ≠ implicit learning

Analogy is an instructional format.

It does not prove:

- absence of explicit knowledge;
- absence of conscious strategy;
- process-pure implicit learning.

Project rule:

**analogy cue and implicit learning are separate constructs.**

---

## 7. Process vs outcome cue

Process and outcome are separate cue targets.

A cue can direct attention to:

- what should happen during the movement;
- what result should occur.

Direct resistance-exercise evidence shows different process/outcome cues can optimize different acute variables.

Project rule:

**match the cue target to the actual task goal; do not assume outcome cues always outperform process cues.**

---

## 8. Cue semantics

Wording can matter beyond broad cue categories.

Action verbs can alter current motor output.

Learners can also reinterpret or simplify the coach's wording.

Project rule:

**inspect both the exact phrase and the learner's represented meaning.**

---

## 9. Individual response

Cue response varies across people and tasks.

But current evidence does not justify permanent learner labels such as:

- “external-focus learner”;
- “analogy learner”;
- “internal learner.”

Preference can be informative but is not proof of long-term optimality.

Project rule:

**individualize by observed task response and interpretation, not fixed typology.**

---

## 10. Cue tied to outcome

Outcome-oriented cueing can be useful when:

- success criteria are clear;
- the learner can organize movement around the task effect;
- exact body geometry is not the primary target.

Project rule:

**outcome cues are tools, not universal defaults.**

---

## 11. Cue tied to error cause

A cue should ideally act on the diagnosed cause of a relevant error.

Direct causal RCT evidence comparing cause-targeted vs symptom-targeted cues is sparse.

Therefore:

**cue-to-cause matching is a PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC.**

Exact diagnosis remains NEU-18.

---

## 12. Instruction shapes task representation

Instructions are not neutral.

Initial explicit organization can influence:

- how a skill is represented;
- temporal structure;
- early performance;
- later execution patterns.

Poor initial instructions can persist even after extensive practice.

Project rule:

**avoid cueing arbitrary movement structure without a task-relevant reason.**

---

## 13. When to remove a cue

No universal removal schedule exists.

No evidence supports one fixed rule such as:

- after N repetitions;
- after N successful attempts;
- after one session;
- after a fixed percentage reduction.

Cue removal should be considered when:

1. the target relation appears reliably;
2. the learner can self-detect the relevant error;
3. the cue no longer improves the target outcome;
4. the cue increases conscious micromanagement/cognitive load;
5. independent execution is now the learning target.

Project rule:

**test cue-free execution directly.**

---

## 14. Cue removal ≠ feedback fading

Cueing and feedback are distinct.

- Cueing directs action before/during an attempt.
- Feedback provides information about an attempt.

Therefore feedback-fading evidence cannot automatically define a verbal cue-fading schedule.

Project rule:

**cue independence must be tested directly, not inferred from feedback literature.**

---

## 15. Cognitive overload

Technical instruction becomes overload when instruction demand competes with resources needed for executing/learning the movement.

Risk rises with:

- multiple simultaneous rules;
- lower working-memory capacity;
- unfamiliar tasks;
- rapid execution;
- fatigue;
- pressure;
- dual-task demand.

No universal cue-count or word-count threshold exists.

Project rule:

**cognitive overload = instruction load × task demand × learner capacity.**

---

## 16. Less information is not always better

Two failure modes exist.

### Too much information
Can exceed working-memory capacity.

### Too little information
Can fail to specify the needed movement relation.

Direct lifting evidence shows a generic short cue may fail where detailed coaching changes the intended coordination.

Project rule:

**minimum sufficient information, not minimum information at any cost.**

---

## 17. Resistance-exercise cueing

Direct cue studies in resistance exercise show acute effects on:

- velocity;
- force;
- RFD;
- EMG;
- posture;
- stiffness;
- displacement.

Examples include:

- hex-bar deadlift;
- bench press;
- isometric lifting;
- squat;
- knee-extension training.

Project rule:

**cue wording can change current resistance-exercise motor output.**

---

## 18. Acute cue effect ≠ motor learning

An acute cue can improve or worsen:

- force;
- velocity;
- movement form;
- muscle activity.

This does not prove:

- delayed retention;
- transfer;
- cue-free execution.

Project rule:

**require delayed cue-free outcomes for motor-learning claims.**

---

## 19. ADHD scope

Area 04 explicitly assigns ADHD-specific cueing to Area 12.

Therefore NEU-16 does not prescribe:

- ADHD-specific cue count;
- ADHD-specific wording;
- ADHD-specific cue timing;
- ADHD-specific removal schedule.

NEU-16 passes only general boundaries:

- instruction load can interact with cognitive capacity;
- there is no universal cue count;
- individual response matters;
- unnecessary information should be minimized.

ADHD-specific implementation remains PSY/COA/IND.

---

## 20. Practical coaching synthesis

For later resistance-exercise technique work:

1. identify the actual task goal or diagnosed error;
2. begin with the minimum sufficient cue;
3. keep simultaneous instructions limited unless additional information is necessary;
4. use wording specific enough to guide the intended relation;
5. choose internal/external/analogy/process/outcome format according to task information needs;
6. verify whether the cue changes the intended variable;
7. change wording when the learner interprets it differently than intended;
8. retain no cue merely because it is popular;
9. remove/test cue-free execution when the learner can express the skill independently;
10. judge learning by retention/transfer, not acute cue-dependent performance.

Status:

**PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not a universal cueing algorithm.

## 21. Canonical non-equivalences

1. One cue ≠ universally optimal.
2. Multiple cues ≠ automatically overload.
3. Short cue ≠ good cue.
4. Detailed cue ≠ bad cue.
5. Specificity ≠ verbosity.
6. Specificity ≠ micromanagement.
7. External cue ≠ universally superior.
8. Internal cue ≠ universally harmful.
9. Analogy cue ≠ implicit learning.
10. Analogy cue ≠ universally superior.
11. Outcome cue ≠ external cue.
12. Process cue ≠ internal cue.
13. Cue category ≠ cue semantics.
14. Preferred cue ≠ objectively optimal cue.
15. Individual response ≠ fixed learner type.
16. Cue removal ≠ feedback fading.
17. Frequent cueing ≠ proven dependency.
18. Acute performance change ≠ durable learning.
19. Visible error ≠ error cause.
20. More information ≠ more learning.
21. Less information ≠ always better learning.

## 22. Measurement boundaries

### Cue count
Number of simultaneously active instructions.

### Cue length
Representational/verbal complexity.

### Specificity
Precision of task-relevant information.

### Direction
Internal vs external.

### Target
Process vs outcome.

### Format
Literal/explicit vs analogy.

### Semantics
Action representation invoked by actual wording.

### Cue effectiveness
Immediate change in intended variable.

### Cue-learning effect
Delayed retention/transfer after cue removal.

### Cue dependence
Failure to express the relation without the cue.

### Cognitive overload
Instruction demand relative to available cognitive resources.

---

## 23. Confidence

### HIGH

- no universal cue-count threshold;
- multiple explicit rules can overload some learners;
- internal/external is not a universal quality hierarchy;
- analogy does not prove implicit learning;
- acute cue response ≠ durable learning;
- ADHD-specific cueing is downstream scope.

### MODERATE-HIGH

- cue semantics can affect performance;
- task-goal alignment matters;
- analogy cueing can be useful;
- individual cue response/interpretation matters;
- detailed instruction can be needed when generic cues fail.

### MODERATE

- one-cue-at-a-time as practical default;
- cue removal after independence emerges;
- cue-to-error-cause matching.

### LOW / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- exact optimal cue count;
- exact word count;
- exact cue-removal schedule;
- exact compound-lift cue format;
- exact ADHD-specific cue architecture.

### NOT ESTABLISHED AS UNIVERSAL

- one cue > multiple cues;
- shorter > longer;
- external > internal;
- analogy > explicit;
- outcome > process;
- preferred cue = best cue;
- fixed cue-removal timing.
