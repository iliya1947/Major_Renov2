# NDEV-02 — Broader neurodevelopmental differential and sufficiency audit: intellectual/adaptive functioning, language/pragmatics, learning, motor coordination, tics/stereotypies, uneven profiles, and comorbidity

Status: **COMPLETE_FOR_CURRENT_SCOPE — 20/20 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Close the remaining neurodevelopmental qualification gap after NDEV-01.

NDEV-01 established:

- developmental origin != age of diagnosis;
- ADHD/autism are developmental but heterogeneous;
- adult diagnosis does not imply adult onset;
- executive-function weakness is not a diagnostic biomarker;
- masking/compensation changes observability without proving a diagnosis;
- ADHD and autism can co-occur without automatic double counting.

NDEV-02 addresses the neurodevelopmental territory still left open:

1. disorders of intellectual development / intellectual disability;
2. adaptive functioning;
3. developmental speech and language disorders;
4. pragmatic/social-communication impairment;
5. developmental learning disorder / specific learning disorder;
6. developmental motor coordination disorder;
7. tics/Tourette versus stereotypies, compulsions and habits;
8. uneven developmental profiles;
9. cross-condition comorbidity and double-counting limits;
10. integrated NDEV sufficiency.

The goal is **boundary competence**, not a diagnostic encyclopedia.

Canonical rule:

`developmental domain -> age/developmental expectation -> standardized/functional evidence -> adaptive/contextual impact -> competing developmental and acquired explanations -> co-occurrence -> narrow conclusion`.

Never:

`one low score -> global developmental diagnosis`

`poor school performance -> learning disorder`

`clumsiness -> DCD`

`repetitive movement -> tic/OCD/autism`.

# I. Question Card

## 1. Concrete dependency

Reliable general psychological reasoning requires distinguishing broad versus specific developmental impairment and separating:

- intellectual ability from adaptive functioning;
- language disorder from autism and social-pragmatic difficulty;
- academic learning disorder from low opportunity, language difficulty, ADHD or generalized intellectual limitation;
- motor coordination disorder from distractibility, neurological disease, sensory impairment or low practice;
- tics from stereotypies, compulsions, habits and functional tic-like behaviors.

Without this layer, the main risks are:

- diagnosing intellectual disability from IQ alone;
- interpreting low adaptive skill as low intelligence;
- treating low academic performance as dyslexia/SLD without adequate instruction and norms;
- using an IQ-achievement discrepancy as if it were the essence of learning disorder;
- converting pragmatic difficulty directly into autism;
- confusing language comprehension problems with defiance/inattention;
- interpreting clumsiness from ADHD distractibility as DCD;
- confusing tics, compulsions and autistic stereotypies;
- ignoring true co-occurrence because one diagnosis already exists;
- double counting the same impairment across multiple diagnoses.

## 2. Question types

- [x] classification;
- [x] differential diagnosis;
- [x] developmental inference;
- [x] measurement;
- [x] functional/adaptive interpretation;
- [x] evidence-to-individual transfer;

## 3. Target constructs

- intellectual functioning;

- developmental language;
- pragmatic language/social communication;
- academic learning;
- motor coordination;
- tics;
- stereotyped movements;
- compulsions;
- uneven developmental profiles;
- developmental comorbidity.

## 4. Population/context

Children, adolescents and adults, with special attention to:

- developmental stage;
- language/cultural background;
- educational opportunity;
- sensory and motor status;
- changing environmental demands;
- late recognition;
- acquired loss of previously established skills.

## 5. Main comparators

Each developmental hypothesis must be compared with:

- normative variation;
- insufficient learning opportunity;
- cultural/linguistic difference;
- sensory impairment;
- intellectual-developmental disorder;
- ADHD/autism;
- psychiatric state;
- neurological/medical condition;
- acquired injury/regression;
- substance/medication effects where relevant.

## 6. Outcome

Allowed:

- pattern compatible with a developmental domain;
- evidence stronger for broad versus specific impairment;
- co-occurrence plausible;
- evidence insufficient;
- acquired explanation takes priority;
- standardized/functional assessment required.

Not allowed:

- formal diagnosis from one score or behavior;
- causal mechanism from category membership;
- individualized conclusion from generic literature alone.

## 7. Time/course

Mandatory distinctions:

- acquisition failure/delay versus loss of previously acquired skill;
- early developmental pattern versus acquired decline;
- late recognition versus late onset;
- persistent domain-specific difficulty versus transient state.

## 8. Eligible evidence

Primary/current anchors:

- WHO ICD-11 CDDR 2024;
- AAIDD current intellectual-disability criteria;
- NIDCD developmental-language guidance;
- ASHA current language/social-communication guidance;
- CDC 2026 tic/Tourette guidance;
- current systematic reviews/guidelines for DCD, tic differential and adult SLD;
- project PSYCHOMETRICS, DEV, EXE and CLN rules.

## 9. Applicability boundary

This block cannot:

- replace specialist speech-language, educational, occupational/physical, neurological or developmental assessment;
- infer intellectual disability from IQ alone;
- infer SLD from one test or one academic weakness;
- infer DCD from general clumsiness;
- infer tic disorder from any repetitive movement;
- infer person-specific etiology.

# II. Neurodevelopmental domain architecture

## 12. Neurodevelopmental disorders concern acquisition/execution during development

WHO ICD-11 CDDR defines neurodevelopmental disorders as conditions arising during the developmental period with significant difficulties acquiring/executing:

- intellectual;
- motor;
- language;
- social

functions.

The developmental timing is central.

## 13. Developmental does not mean congenital phenotype is fully visible at birth

A developmental disorder may become evident only when:

- academic skills are formally taught;
- social demands increase;
- motor tasks become complex;
- independent adaptive demands increase;
- compensatory strategies fail.

This is consistent with NDEV-01.

## 14. Acquisition failure and acquired loss are different

A major generic boundary:

- developmental disorder: skill acquisition/execution is atypical from the developmental period;
- acquired condition: previously established skill is lost or deteriorates due to injury, neurological disease, psychiatric state or other later process.

This distinction is especially important for:

- language;
- learning;
- motor coordination;
- cognition.

## 15. Uneven profiles are normal within neurodevelopmental conditions

A person may have:

- strong verbal reasoning and weak motor coordination;
- strong general intelligence and specific reading impairment;
- low intellectual functioning with relatively stronger social/practical skills;
- strong academic knowledge with weak adaptive independence;
- language weakness with typical nonverbal reasoning.

Unevenness itself is not a diagnosis.

# III. Intellectual functioning versus adaptive functioning

## 16. Intellectual disability/disorder of intellectual development requires more than IQ

WHO ICD-11 requires significant limitations in:

1. intellectual functioning;
2. adaptive behaviour.

AAIDD likewise requires:

1. significant intellectual limitations;
2. significant adaptive-behavior limitations;
3. developmental-period onset.

Therefore:

`low IQ -> intellectual disability`

is invalid.

## 17. Adaptive functioning is a separate construct

AAIDD defines adaptive behavior as learned:

- conceptual;
- social;
- practical

skills used in everyday life.

It is not the same construct as intelligence.

## 18. Conceptual adaptive skills

Examples include:

- literacy;
- numeracy;
- money/time concepts;
- self-direction;
- applied communication/problem solving.

## 19. Social adaptive skills

Examples include:

- interpersonal competence;
- social responsibility;
- practical social judgment;
- avoiding exploitation/victimization;
- following social/legal rules.

## 20. Practical adaptive skills

Examples include:

- self-care;
- health/safety;
- transport;
- occupational routines;
- money use;
- household/technology use.

## 21. Adaptive expectations are context-sensitive

Adaptive functioning must be interpreted relative to:

- age;
- culture;
- community expectations;
- available supports;
- opportunities;
- environmental demands.

Therefore:

`needs help with one culturally/contextually specific task -> global adaptive deficit`

is invalid.

## 22. IQ is an estimate, not the whole condition

WHO explicitly warns:

- IQ is not an isolated diagnostic requirement;
- testing conditions and technical properties affect scores;
- scores can vary over development;
- adaptive behavior must also be evaluated.

This directly inherits PSYCHOMETRICS rules.

## 23. Measurement error matters near thresholds

AAIDD explicitly requires clinical judgment and attention to measurement error.

A cutoff should not be treated as an ontological cliff.

## 24. Low adaptive functioning does not prove low intellectual functioning

Adaptive limitations can also reflect:

- autism-related social difficulty;
- motor impairment;
- language impairment;
- psychiatric symptoms;
- lack of opportunity;
- environmental dependence;
- sensory impairment.

## 25. Low intellectual scores can be invalid or misleading under some conditions

WHO flags interpretation problems when there are:

- communication impairments;
- sensory/motor impairments;
- behavioral disturbance;
- low literacy;
- cultural/language mismatch;
- severe deprivation;
- mental disorders;
- treatment effects.

Assessment must match the person's capacities.

## 26. Strengths and limitations can coexist

AAIDD explicitly emphasizes that limitations often coexist with strengths.

Therefore intellectual disability must not be converted into:

- global incompetence;
- one personality type;
- absence of agency;
- absence of learning potential.

## 27. Support needs can change

WHO notes people can continue gaining skills and competencies, with support needs changing across development/life circumstances.

Thus:

`lifelong condition -> fixed functioning`

is invalid.

# IV. Broad developmental delay versus specific profile

## 28. Broad delay and specific impairment are different

A generalized developmental profile affects multiple domains broadly.

A specific developmental disorder can affect one domain much more than others.

## 29. Developmental level matters for differential diagnosis

A difficulty should be judged against:

- chronological age;
- developmental level;
- general intellectual functioning;
- opportunity to learn.

## 30. Specific impairment may coexist with broader intellectual disability

WHO allows additional developmental speech/language or learning diagnoses when impairment is **substantially beyond** what would be expected from the person's broader intellectual/adaptive level.

Therefore:

`intellectual disability present -> no specific developmental disorder can coexist`

is invalid.

## 31. But ordinary downstream consequences should not be double diagnosed

If academic limitation is fully explained by broad intellectual-developmental impairment, a separate learning disorder should not be assigned merely because school performance is low.

# V. Developmental language disorder

## 32. Language is not one skill

Developmental language disorder can involve relative weakness in:

- phonology;
- morphology;
- syntax;
- semantics;
- receptive language;
- expressive language;
- pragmatic language.

A single global "language ability" score can hide important structure.

## 33. DLD concerns persistent acquisition/use difficulty

WHO requires persistent deficits in acquiring, understanding, producing or using spoken/signed language.

NIDCD similarly describes DLD as a developmental communication disorder interfering with learning, understanding and using language.

## 34. DLD is developmental but presentation changes

NIDCD notes DLD begins in childhood and may continue into adulthood while manifestation changes.

Examples:

- early grammar/vocabulary difficulty;
- later complex language;
- reading/writing;
- narrative organization.

## 35. Late talker != DLD automatically

Some children with delayed early language catch up.

Persistent developmental language difficulty needs longitudinal evidence.

## 36. Language difficulty can look behavioral

NIDCD notes a child may:

- fail to follow instructions because they did not understand;
- avoid interaction because language is hard.

Observers can misread this as:

- defiance;
- inattention;
- shyness.

Therefore behavioral interpretation must not precede language assessment.

## 37. DLD and learning disorder are related but distinct

DLD increases risk for later literacy/academic problems.

But:

`DLD -> learning disorder automatically`

is invalid.

Both may be diagnosed when each has independent evidence.

## 38. DLD can coexist with ADHD/DCD

ASHA current terminology allows DLD to coexist with conditions such as ADHD or DCD when language impairment is not simply secondary to the other condition.

## 39. Culture and multilingualism matter

WHO and ASHA require attention to:

- dialect;
- language exposure;
- multilingual development;
- culturally appropriate norms.

A difference due to language exposure or dialect is not a disorder.

## 40. Dynamic assessment can help distinguish difference from disorder

ASHA notes that ability to learn/change language performance with supported teaching can provide useful differential information.

It is complementary evidence, not a perfect gold standard.

# VI. Pragmatic language / social communication boundary

## 41. Pragmatic difficulty is not synonymous with autism

Pragmatic language involves the use/understanding of language in social context.

Difficulties can occur in:

- developmental language disorder;
- ADHD;
- autism;
- acquired brain injury;
- other conditions.

## 42. ICD-11 and DSM-style organization differ

ICD-11 includes developmental language disorder with mainly pragmatic-language impairment.

DSM-style systems include social (pragmatic) communication disorder as a distinct category.

Therefore diagnostic labels are framework-specific.

## 43. Autism requires more than pragmatic language impairment

WHO differentiates autism from pragmatic-language DLD by broader:

- reciprocal social-interaction deficits;
- restricted/repetitive/inflexible behavior/interests.

Pragmatic language impairment alone should not be used to infer autism.

## 44. Social communication disorder and autism are not simply additive labels

ASHA's DSM-style guidance notes SCD is not diagnosed concurrently with autism because autism already includes social-communication difficulty plus restricted/repetitive behavior.

ICD-11 organizes pragmatic-language impairment differently.

This is a classification-system difference, not proof that one construct is "real" and the other is not.

## 45. ADHD can produce pragmatic difficulty

ASHA notes pragmatic-language problems occur at elevated rates in ADHD.

Possible pathways include:

- interruption;
- inattention;
- poor conversational tracking;
- impulsive timing.

Thus:

`pragmatic difficulty -> autism`

is invalid.

## 46. Acquired social-communication problems require different reasoning

Social communication can be impaired after:

- traumatic brain injury;
- neurological disease;
- aphasia/right-hemisphere injury.

Acquired change should not be retrofitted into a developmental diagnosis.

# VII. Developmental learning disorder / specific learning disorder

## 47. Developmental learning disorder is about acquisition of academic skills

WHO requires significant limitations in learning:

- reading;
- writing;
- mathematics

that are markedly below age expectation and impair functioning.

## 48. Adequate learning opportunity matters

WHO requires that limitations are not simply attributable to:

- economic/environmental disadvantage;
- inadequate educational opportunity.

Therefore:

`poor school performance -> developmental learning disorder`

is invalid.

## 49. Learning disorder is not generalized low intelligence

Specific academic impairment must be distinguished from broad intellectual-developmental limitation.

If intellectual disability is present, a separate learning disorder requires academic impairment beyond what is expected from the person's intellectual level.

## 50. IQ–achievement discrepancy is not the essence of SLD

Modern DSM/ICD frameworks no longer require the classic IQ-achievement discrepancy as the defining rule.

A discrepancy may be descriptive but should not replace:

- norm-referenced academic impairment;
- developmental history;
- opportunity/instruction;
- functional impact;
- alternative-explanation checks.

## 51. One low achievement score is not sufficient

WHO explicitly notes one standardized test score does not by itself distinguish disorder from normality.

Need:

- persistence;
- history;
- task/domain convergence;
- functional impact;
- measurement quality.

## 52. Compensation can hide learning disorder

WHO notes some people maintain apparently adequate academic performance through:

- extraordinary effort/time;
- compensatory strategies;
- high support.

Difficulty may become clearer as demands for speed/volume increase.

This parallels NDEV-01's demand/compensation rule.

## 53. ADHD and learning disorder commonly coexist

Academic difficulty in ADHD can result from poor sustained attention/organization.

But developmental learning disorder is not merely an attention problem.

WHO explicitly allows both diagnoses when requirements for each are met.

## 54. DLD and learning disorder can coexist

Language difficulty can drive literacy problems.

But a distinct developmental learning disorder can also be present when independent requirements are satisfied.

## 55. Sensory impairment must be addressed

Vision/hearing problems can impair academic learning.

Appropriate accommodation and differential assessment are required.

## 56. Acquired loss of academic skills is not developmental learning disorder

Loss after:

- traumatic brain injury;
- neurodegenerative disease;
- other acquired condition

is conceptually different from developmental acquisition difficulty.

## 57. Adult SLD diagnosis is particularly vulnerable to construct drift

A 2026 critical two-part review highlights problems in adult/postsecondary SLD diagnosis including:

- inconsistent definitions;
- base-rate neglect;
- reliance on unsupported discrepancy logic;
- selective high-achievement samples;
- contextual and performance-validity issues.

This is a critical narrative synthesis, not a universal professional consensus.

Its appropriate use here is caution:

**adult first-time SLD diagnosis requires especially strong developmental, norm-referenced and functional evidence.**

## 58. High achievement does not automatically exclude hidden difficulty

Compensation/support can preserve outcomes.

But this should not be used as unfalsifiable logic:

`high achievement + subjective effort -> SLD`

is still insufficient.

Need evidence of real developmental academic impairment.

# VIII. Developmental motor coordination disorder

## 59. DCD is a motor coordination disorder, not generic clumsiness

WHO requires:

- significant delay in acquisition of gross/fine motor skills;
- impaired coordinated execution;
- developmental onset;
- significant persistent functional impact.

## 60. Motor impairment must be below age expectation

Assessment should use appropriately normed motor measures where possible plus real-world functional evidence.

## 61. DCD is not diagnosed purely from "awkwardness"

Clumsiness can reflect:

- distraction/impulsivity;
- visual problems;
- neurological disease;
- musculoskeletal/connective-tissue conditions;
- low practice/opportunity;
- intellectual-developmental limitation.

## 62. ADHD can look clumsy without DCD

WHO specifically notes some people with ADHD appear clumsy because of:

- distractibility;
- impulsivity.

A separate DCD diagnosis requires independent motor-coordination evidence.

## 63. Autism and DCD can coexist

But unwillingness to participate in motor tasks because of low interest/sensory/social factors should not automatically be interpreted as motor-coordination deficit.

## 64. Neurological and musculoskeletal differential has priority

WHO lists conditions such as:

- cerebral palsy;
- muscular dystrophy;
- severe visual impairment;
- connective-tissue/musculoskeletal disease

as alternative explanations requiring appropriate medical examination.

## 65. Early-childhood motor variability limits diagnosis

WHO notes motor-skill acquisition is highly variable in early childhood and DCD is usually not diagnosed before about age five.

This is a measurement/developmental-stability issue, not an arbitrary age rule.

## 66. DCD often persists

Current WHO guidance describes a chronic course into adolescence/adulthood for many cases.

Adult difficulties can appear in:

- driving;
- handwriting/note taking;
- tool use;
- sports;
- learning new motor skills.

## 67. DCD co-occurrence is common

WHO notes frequent co-occurrence with:

- ADHD;
- autism;
- developmental language disorder;
- learning disorder.

Co-occurrence makes attribution harder and requires domain-specific evidence.

## 68. 2026 DCD guideline reinforces functional impact

The 2026 APTA pediatric DCD guideline characterizes DCD as impaired motor coordination/planning not better explained by another condition, with effects on:

- school;
- activities of daily living;
- family/peer participation.

This supports the motor + function + exclusion architecture.

# IX. Tics and Tourette syndrome

## 69. Tic phenomenology

Current CDC/WHO guidance describes tics as sudden, rapid, recurrent motor movements or vocalizations.

WHO emphasizes non-rhythmic character.

## 70. Tourette requires motor and phonic tics over time

Current CDC/WHO criteria require both:

- motor tics;
- vocal/phonic tics;

with persistence for at least one year and developmental onset.

## 71. Tic disorders are differentiated partly by type and duration

Current CDC distinguishes:

- Tourette syndrome;
- persistent/chronic motor or vocal tic disorder;
- provisional tic disorder.

## 72. Tics can wax and wane

WHO notes tics can:

- fluctuate;
- remit temporarily;
- be temporarily suppressed;
- worsen with stress;
- change over development.

Therefore:

`not observed during one interview -> no tic disorder`

is invalid.

## 73. Premonitory urge and suppressibility are useful but not universal

Current reviews describe:

- premonitory urge;
- partial suppressibility

as common tic features.

They are not required to be reported by every child/person.

## 74. Tic != compulsion

Compulsions are typically performed:

- in response to obsession;
- according to a rule;
- to reduce anxiety or obtain completeness.

Tics are more commonly:

- sudden;
- brief;
- non-rhythmic;
- urge-driven/sensory-motor.

Complex cases overlap.

## 75. Tic != stereotypy

Stereotypies are more often:

- patterned;
- coordinated;
- rhythmic/predictable;
- sometimes distractible;
- associated with excitement/self-stimulation in some cases.

Tics more characteristically:

- change over time;
- are rapid/non-rhythmic;
- have urge/suppressibility patterns.

Phenomenology must be assessed rather than inferred from the word "repetitive."

## 76. Tic != habit

Habits are learned/automatic behavioral patterns.

A repeated motor behavior does not become a tic simply because it is automatic.

## 77. Tic versus speech dysfluency

WHO explicitly differentiates speech fluency disorder from complex vocal/motor tics:

- fluency disorder involves marked disruption in speech flow/rhythm;
- tics do not create the same core speech-dysfluency architecture.

## 78. Functional tic-like behavior is an important modern differential

CDC 2026 notes abrupt-onset tic-like behaviors can differ from typical Tourette/tic disorders and may be conceptualized as functional neurological symptoms.

High-value clues include:

- sudden abrupt onset;
- first presentation in adolescence;
- complex symptoms from outset;
- atypical developmental course.

These clues are not individually diagnostic.

## 79. Adult-onset tic-like symptoms need aggressive alternative checking

WHO notes true Tourette onset in adulthood is rare.

Possible alternatives include:

- drug effects;
- neurological insult;
- functional tic-like behavior;
- other movement disorders.

## 80. ADHD/OCD commonly coexist with Tourette

CDC 2026 explicitly notes high co-occurrence.

Therefore:

`tic disorder explains all attention/compulsive symptoms`

is invalid.

# X. Repetitive behavior differential matrix

## 81. Tic

Typical clues:

- sudden;
- brief;
- recurrent;
- non-rhythmic;
- waxing/waning;
- possible premonitory urge;
- partial suppressibility.

## 82. Compulsion

Typical clues:

- linked to obsession/feared consequence/completeness;
- rule-bound;
- anxiety/urge reduction;
- often cognitively elaborated.

## 83. Autistic stereotypy/repetitive behavior

Typical clues:

- patterned/repetitive;
- can be rhythmic;
- may regulate arousal/sensory state;
- embedded in broader autism developmental architecture.

## 84. Stereotyped movement disorder

Requires repetitive patterned movements sufficiently significant/impairing and not better explained by other conditions.

## 85. Habit

Learned repeated behavior that may be automatic but lacks the specific tic/compulsion developmental phenomenology.

## 86. Functional tic-like behavior

Often differs in:

- onset pattern;
- phenomenology;
- social/contextual pattern;
- developmental history.

## 87. No single feature is enough

For example:

- suppressibility occurs in tics but also deliberate behavior;
- urge occurs in tics and compulsions;
- repetition occurs everywhere;
- stress worsens many conditions.

Use the full pattern.

# XI. Language, learning and cognition: avoid construct substitution

## 88. Reading difficulty is not automatically a language disorder

Reading can be affected by:

- phonological processing;
- decoding;
- language comprehension;
- attention;
- vision;
- instruction;
- general cognition.

## 89. Language difficulty is not automatically intellectual disability

A person can have severe language impairment with relatively typical nonverbal reasoning.

## 90. Low verbal test score may reflect language proficiency

In multilingual or language-impaired individuals, verbal IQ/cognitive scores can underestimate some abilities.

Test interpretation must consider language demands.

## 91. Poor instruction can mimic learning disorder

Before developmental learning diagnosis ask:

- Was instruction adequate?
- Was attendance adequate?
- Was the language of instruction accessible?
- Were sensory issues addressed?
- Were relevant supports tried?

## 92. Executive weakness is not the hidden master diagnosis

WHO notes psychological-process weaknesses can accompany learning disorder, but the relationship is not understood well enough to classify learning disorder by these underlying processes.

Therefore:

`working-memory weakness -> dyslexia/SLD mechanism established`

is invalid.

# XII. Adaptive functioning and uneven ability

## 93. High cognitive ability does not guarantee adaptive independence

A person can reason very well yet struggle in:

- daily organization;
- money management;
- self-care;
- social judgment;
- practical independence.

## 94. Low adaptive functioning does not prove intellectual disability

Need significant intellectual limitation too.

## 95. Intellectual disability does not imply uniformly low abilities

Profiles can be uneven.

Use domain-specific strengths and support needs.

## 96. Adaptive functioning can change with environment

Support, technology, structure and opportunity can improve real-world performance.

This does not necessarily mean the developmental condition disappeared.

## 97. Environment can create apparent disability or hide it

High support can conceal difficulty.

Poor accessibility can magnify difficulty.

Person × environment remains central.

---

# XIII. Co-occurrence and diagnostic layering

## 98. Neurodevelopmental conditions commonly co-occur

Examples supported by WHO/current sources:

- ADHD + learning disorder;
- DLD + learning disorder;
- DCD + ADHD;
- DCD + autism;
- Tourette + ADHD/OCD;
- intellectual disability + autism.

## 99. Co-occurrence should be based on independent criteria

Ask:

1. Does each condition have its own core evidence?
2. Is impairment beyond what the first condition explains?
3. Is the same behavior being double counted?
4. Does the second label add real descriptive/clinical information?

## 100. Do not use diagnostic parsimony as an absolute rule

One diagnosis does not have to explain everything.

## 101. Do not use diagnostic proliferation as an absolute rule

Every weakness does not need a new diagnosis.

## 102. Domain-specific assessment often outperforms one global explanation

If the person has:

- language;
- academic;
- motor;
- adaptive

difficulties, characterize each domain before assuming one umbrella mechanism.

# XIV. Regression and acquired-change boundary

## 103. Loss of acquired skill is a red flag

Developmental weakness differs from:

- clear decline;
- regression after a period of established normal function;
- sudden new neurological/language/motor change.

## 104. Acquired language loss is not DLD

Possible causes include:

- stroke;
- TBI;
- infection/encephalitis;
- epilepsy-related syndromes;
- neurodegeneration.

Medical/neurological evaluation takes priority.

## 105. Acquired academic decline is not developmental learning disorder

A person who previously mastered reading/math and later loses the skill requires acquired-neurological/medical differential.

## 106. Acquired motor decline is not DCD

Progressive or sudden motor change requires neurological/musculoskeletal differential.

## 107. Adult-onset tic-like symptoms are not automatically Tourette

The developmental history matters.

---

# XV. Cultural, language and opportunity boundaries

## 108. Norms are population-dependent

Testing must consider:

- culture;
- language;
- educational system;
- socioeconomic opportunity;
- disability access;
- test norming.

## 109. Multilingualism is not language disorder

Language proficiency and exposure history are required.

## 110. Dialect is not disorder

ASHA explicitly requires dialect/cultural differentiation.

## 111. Educational disadvantage is not learning disorder

Low achievement due to inadequate instruction/opportunity must be distinguished from developmental learning impairment.

## 112. But disadvantage does not make true disorder impossible

A person can have both:

- inadequate opportunity;
- developmental disorder.

The task is causal/differential separation, not exclusion by social context alone.

---

# XVI. Adult recognition across broader NDEV

## 113. Late recognition can occur beyond ADHD/autism

WHO notes learning disorder can be recognized later when academic/professional demands exceed compensation.

Intellectual-developmental disorders, especially milder presentations, may also remain undiagnosed until adolescence/adulthood.

## 114. Late recognition still requires developmental evidence

Adult first-time assessment should seek:

- school history;
- developmental milestones;
- old academic performance;
- adaptive-function history;
- family/informant information;
- prior accommodations;
- objective current impairment.

## 115. Adult SLD evidence should be especially conservative

Selection into higher education and accommodation contexts can distort base rates and diagnostic practice.

Use population norms and documented developmental/functional evidence where possible.

## 116. Adult DCD requires persistent motor-function evidence

Current adult measurement evidence remains less mature than pediatric evidence.

Do not infer DCD from generalized self-described clumsiness alone.

# XVII. Measurement architecture

## 117. Intellectual testing

Ask:

- appropriate norm?
- language/cultural fit?
- sensory/motor confounds?
- confidence interval?
- profile interpretation?
- adaptive corroboration?

## 118. Adaptive assessment

Ask:

- conceptual/social/practical domains?
- self-report or informant?
- context?
- opportunity?
- cultural expectations?
- support level?

## 119. Language assessment

Ask:

- receptive/expressive/pragmatic?
- spoken/signed?
- multilingual history?
- hearing?
- dynamic learning?
- functional communication?

## 120. Academic assessment

Ask:

- reading/writing/math domain?
- population norms?
- educational opportunity?
- sustained impairment?
- developmental history?
- support/compensation?

## 121. Motor assessment

Ask:

- standardized motor competence?
- functional impact?
- neurological/sensory exclusion?
- persistence?
- pervasiveness?

## 122. Tic assessment

Ask:

- motor/phonic?
- onset?
- duration?

## Failure A — low IQ = intellectual disability

Incorrect.

Adaptive functioning and developmental onset are also required.

## Failure B — low adaptive functioning = low intelligence

Incorrect.

They are separate constructs.

## Failure C — intellectual disability = global incapacity

Incorrect.

Strengths, learning and support responsiveness remain.

## Failure D — late diagnosis = late developmental onset

Incorrect.

Recognition can be delayed.

## Failure E — late talker = DLD

Incorrect.

Some children catch up; persistence matters.

## Failure F — does not follow instructions = defiant/inattentive

Incorrect.

Language comprehension may be the limiting factor.

## Failure G — pragmatic difficulty = autism

Incorrect.

Pragmatic impairment also occurs in DLD/SCD/ADHD and acquired conditions.

## Failure H — poor grades = SLD

Incorrect.

Instruction, opportunity, attention, language, intelligence and sensory factors matter.

## Failure I — IQ-achievement discrepancy = SLD essence

Incorrect.

Modern classification does not reduce SLD to discrepancy.

## Failure J — one low achievement score = SLD

Incorrect.

Persistence, norming, function and developmental history matter.

## Failure K — ADHD explains all learning difficulty

Incorrect.

SLD can coexist and requires independent evidence.

## Failure L — clumsy = DCD

Incorrect.

Distractibility, neurological, sensory and opportunity explanations matter.

## Failure M — ADHD clumsiness = DCD

Incorrect.

Independent motor evidence is required.

## Failure N — repetitive movement = tic

Incorrect.

Compulsions, stereotypies, habits and functional symptoms must be distinguished.

## Failure O — suppressible = voluntary/fake

Incorrect.

Tics can often be temporarily suppressed.

## Failure P — tic = compulsion

Incorrect.

Phenomenology/function differ.

## Failure Q — no tic observed today = no tic disorder

Incorrect.

Tics wax/wane and can be suppressed.

## Failure R — adult abrupt tic-like onset = Tourette

Unsafe.

Developmental history and functional/neurological/substance alternatives must be checked.

## Failure S — multilingual difference = language disorder

Incorrect.

Language exposure and norms matter.

## Failure T — educational disadvantage = learning disorder

Incorrect.

Opportunity must be assessed.

## Failure U — disadvantage excludes genuine disorder

Incorrect.

Co-occurrence is possible.

## Failure V — acquired loss = developmental disorder

Incorrect.

Regression/decline requires acquired medical/neurological differential.

## Failure W — one neurodevelopmental diagnosis explains every weak domain

Incorrect.

Co-occurring domain-specific disorders can exist.

## Failure X — every weak domain gets a separate diagnosis

Incorrect.

Need independent core evidence and incremental explanatory value.

# XXI. Evidence-strength map

## High-confidence practical boundaries

1. neurodevelopmental conditions are defined by developmental-period architecture;
2. age of recognition is not age of onset;
3. intellectual disability requires both intellectual and adaptive limitations;
4. adaptive functioning is not identical to intelligence;
5. low IQ alone is insufficient for intellectual disability;
6. developmental language disorder is distinct from ordinary language difference and from acquired language loss;
7. pragmatic-language difficulty alone does not establish autism;
8. developmental learning disorder is not simply poor academic performance;
9. adequate opportunity/instruction and alternative explanations matter for SLD;
10. one low academic score is insufficient;
11. DCD is not generic clumsiness;
12. ADHD-related distractibility can mimic clumsiness without DCD;
13. tics, compulsions, stereotypies and habits are not interchangeable;
14. Tourette requires developmental tic architecture rather than any repetitive movement;
15. developmental disorders can co-occur;
16. one diagnosis does not explain every weak developmental domain;
17. acquired loss/regression requires acquired medical/neurological differential;
18. cultural/language/test validity constraints are mandatory.

## Supported but context-sensitive

1. late recognition through compensation/high support;
2. adult SLD when developmental evidence is reconstructed carefully;
3. DCD persistence into adulthood;
4. functional tic-like behavior as a distinct differential;
5. dynamic language assessment as additional difference-versus-disorder evidence;
6. standardized adaptive testing near ID thresholds.

## Not established as universal

1. one IQ cutoff as a natural boundary;
2. one adaptive scale as gold standard;
3. one cognitive-process profile that diagnoses SLD;
4. one motor test that diagnoses DCD;
5. premonitory urge in every tic case;
6. one symptom-level rule that always separates tic, compulsion and stereotypy;
7. one diagnosis that explains a person's complete developmental profile.
