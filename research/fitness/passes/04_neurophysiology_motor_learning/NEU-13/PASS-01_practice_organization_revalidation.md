- assuming more practice is always better;
- assuming more sessions or more spacing is universally better;
- treating massed practice as universally harmful;
- treating random/interleaved practice as universally superior;

## 3. Practice frequency

Practice frequency is the temporal recurrence of practice opportunities.

It can be described at several levels:

- repetitions within a set/block;
- blocks within a session;
- sessions per day/week;
- days between sessions.

These are not interchangeable.

### Key boundary

A “frequency effect” can actually reflect:

- total volume;
- rest/recovery;
- fatigue;
- consolidation interval;
- retrieval/re-familiarization;
- total target-task exposures.

Therefore practice frequency must not be interpreted without the accompanying dose and spacing structure.

### Current evidence

The 2026 tennis-serve RCT (PMID 42640629) compared short-rest/consecutive-day versus longer-rest/nonconsecutive-day schedules with controlled total volume. It found no statistically significant between-group differences in serve acquisition or retention.

The 2026 motor-sequence spacing study (PMID 42177224) found a clear end-of-training performance benefit for spaced practice but no meaningful later learning advantage when task conditions were equated.

Conversely, other motor tasks show benefits from distribution across sessions/days, while some serial-sequence work reports better learning with shorter inter-trial intervals (PMID 40778584).

Therefore:

**frequency has no universal optimum independent of task, fatigue, spacing scale and total practice.**

For resistance-exercise technique, no current direct evidence justifies an exact “X technique sessions per week” rule from motor-learning science alone.

Confidence: **HIGH for context dependence / LOW for exact RT frequency prescription.**

## 4. Practice volume

Practice volume is the amount of task exposure:

- repetitions;
- trials;
- blocks;
- minutes;
- successful target executions.

More practice can increase learning opportunity, but the dose-response is not assumed linear.

Important distinctions:

1. **efficacy** — total improvement;
2. **efficiency** — improvement per trial/time unit;
3. **retention** — durability;
4. **transfer** — expression in altered conditions.

Larger dose can increase total learning while producing progressively smaller gain per additional repetition.

Clinical dose modeling in chronic stroke (PMID 32830617) explicitly showed:

- greater dose → greater overall efficacy/retention;
- additional dose/bouts → lower efficiency per unit.

This cannot be numerically transferred to healthy strength learners, but the diminishing-efficiency principle is relevant.

Real-world high-volume video-game data (PMID 34987368) also showed nonlinear daily practice benefit: additional practice produced diminishing incremental gain rather than an indefinitely linear response.

Canonical rule:

**more practice can provide more learning opportunity, but marginal learning per added trial can decline; total dose and efficiency are separate.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for principle / LOW for RT-specific dose threshold.**

## 5. Distributed vs massed practice

### Operational distinction

**Massed practice** = relatively dense practice with short rest/spacing.

**Distributed practice** = practice separated by longer within-session or between-session intervals.

These labels are incomplete unless the time scale is stated.

Examples:

- 0 vs 10 s between trials;
- 1 vs 5 min between blocks;
- same day vs separate days.

### Evidence audit

The evidence is mixed across motor tasks.

Findings favoring distribution exist, but recent work directly challenges universal spacing claims:

- PMID 42177224: spacing changed training performance strongly but later learning minimally/not detectably;
- PMID 40778584: shorter inter-trial intervals enhanced serial motor learning;
- PMID 42640629: no significant retention advantage from longer-rest/nonconsecutive tennis practice when total volume was controlled.

Older evidence also shows discrete and continuous tasks can respond differently (PMID 2489826).

Therefore:

**distributed practice is not a universal motor-learning superiority law.**

A likely reason is that spacing changes:

- fatigue/reactive inhibition;
- memory/retrieval demands;
- consolidation opportunity;
- context reinstatement;
- number of uninterrupted repetitions.

Canonical rule:

**state the exact spacing scale and task; do not import declarative-learning spacing effects as a universal motor rule.**

Confidence: **HIGH against universal superiority / MODERATE for task-dependent spacing effects.**

## 6. Blocked practice

Blocked practice repeats one task/variant before switching.

Typical effects:

- lower switching cost;
- easier acquisition;
- better within-session performance in many paradigms;
- potentially stronger context dependence.

Blocked practice can be useful when:

- the learner cannot yet execute the task at all;
- task variants are highly dissimilar/complex;
- safety requires stable early repetition;
- early task understanding is the immediate constraint.

But better blocked acquisition does not prove better learning.

The 2026 random-vs-blocked study (PMID 42691817) showed:

- blocked better at end of practice;
- random better at 24-h retention.

However counterexamples exist. Manual-force learning (PMID 30185111) favored blocked practice even at retention.

Canonical rule:

**blocked practice is a legitimate schedule with potential acquisition advantages; it is not synonymous with poor learning.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 7. Random / interleaved practice

Random/interleaved practice switches among task variants frequently, often trial-to-trial.

This increases contextual interference.

Potential mechanisms include:

- repeated reconstruction of action plans;
- comparison/contrast among task variants;
- reduced dependence on one immediate context;
- greater retrieval demand.

### Current evidence

PMID 42691817 provides a contemporary clean example of the classic dissociation:

- random worse during acquisition;
- random better at 24 h retention.

Meta-analytic evidence also supports an average retention benefit from high contextual interference.

But:

- task complexity matters;
- similarity among variants matters;
- learner level can matter;
- applied effects are smaller/less consistent than laboratory effects.

Canonical rule:

**random practice can enhance delayed retention in many tasks, but acquisition impairment is expected and the benefit is not universal.**

Confidence: **HIGH for average CI retention effect / MODERATE for applied-complex tasks.**

## 8. Constant vs variable practice

### Constant practice

Practice repeatedly targets the same criterion condition.

Likely advantages:

- high target specificity;
- stable early task mapping;
- efficient improvement when only one criterion task matters.

### Variable practice

Practice includes multiple parameter values/task variants.

Possible advantages:

- broader solution space;
- transfer/generalization;
- reduced context dependence.

But current evidence rejects “variable is always better.”

The 2026 registered report (PMID 41351896; n=80) found no significant 24-h retention difference between constant and variable practice for the criterion task, with Bayesian support for the null.

The 2024 variability-load study (PMID 38948206) explicitly concluded “more is not better.”

PMID 36132213 likewise found no retention advantage from variable practice even when introduced after stabilization.

Older applied evidence shows a possible specificity-transfer trade-off:

- constant can favor retention of the practiced criterion;
- variable can favor transfer to a novel condition (PMID 16158696; 9760639).

Canonical rule:

**variable practice is most defensible when transfer/generalization is part of the target; it is not inherently superior for retention of one fixed criterion task.**

Confidence: **HIGH against universal superiority / MODERATE for transfer benefit.**

## 9. Whole vs part practice

### Whole practice

Practice the full target skill.

Advantages:

- preserves coordination among components;
- maximizes target-task specificity;
- avoids learning isolated pieces that do not recombine cleanly.

### Part practice

Practice components/subsequences separately.

Potential value when:

- the whole task is too complex to produce informative attempts;
- components have meaningful separability;
- a bottleneck component can be isolated;
- safety/setup demands make whole practice initially impractical.

### Evidence

The main meta-analysis (PMID 20038005) generally supported classic task complexity/organization recommendations but was limited by sparse evidence in several comparisons.

A later systematic review in procedural/medical learning (PMID 26768734) judged evidence insufficient for strong general recommendations.

A 2023 juggling study (PMID 38022928) found different early learning trajectories: whole-task learners improved faster initially, while part-learning participants caught up.

Canonical rule:

**whole-vs-part choice depends on task organization, component separability and whether part practice transfers back to the integrated target skill.**

Confidence: **MODERATE**.

For compound lifts, isolated drills should therefore be treated as temporary scaffolds whose transfer back to the whole lift must be checked.

## 10. Repetition without mindless repetition

Repetition is necessary for skill learning, but skilled repetition does not mean exact kinematic duplication.

The “repetition without repetition” perspective (PMID 32903689) emphasizes behavioral flexibility:

- stable task outcome can coexist with variation in movement solution;
- skilled performers can adapt movement to context;
- variability can be functional.

Therefore practice should distinguish:

1. repetition of **task-relevant goal/control problem**;
2. exact reproduction of every joint trajectory.

For resistance-exercise technique, useful repetition means repeated attempts that allow:

- perception of outcome/error;
- refinement of coordination;
- comparison of solution quality;
- stable execution under target constraints.

It does not require mechanically identical repetitions.

### “Mindless” boundary

There is no validated binary scientific construct “mindless repetition” that yields a universal stopping rule.

Operationally, practice quality declines when added repetitions mainly produce:

- fatigue-related deterioration;
- no new task-relevant information;
- no target-specific exposure;
- uncorrected repeated error;
- redundant easy success with no relevant retention/transfer objective.

Canonical rule:

**repeat the task problem, not an exact kinematic template; additional repetitions need task-relevant information or consolidation value.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for flexibility principle / PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC for “mindless” stopping criterion.**

## 11. Contextual interference

Contextual interference (CI) describes interference created when multiple tasks/variants are practiced in an interleaved/random order rather than in blocks.

### Retention meta-analysis

PMID 38987617:

- 54 studies in meta-analysis;
- high CI showed a statistically significant medium beneficial effect on retention overall;
- laboratory effects were clearer than applied-field effects.

### Transfer meta-analysis

PMID 39205981:

- 42 studies in systematic review;
- 34 in quantitative analysis;
- overall transfer effect favored random practice, SMD ≈ **0.55**;
- laboratory effect was larger/significant;
- applied-setting effect was smaller and statistically non-significant.

This is closure-critical.

Therefore:

**CI is a real average effect, but its applied magnitude is context dependent and smaller/less certain outside laboratory paradigms.**

CI does not mean:

- maximal randomness;
- random exercise selection;
- changing technique every rep;
- introducing irrelevant variation.

Canonical rule:

**use contextual interference to create useful retrieval/reconstruction demands among relevant variants, not chaos.**

Confidence: **HIGH for average laboratory retention effect / MODERATE for applied transfer.**

## 12. Specificity of practice

Specificity means that retained/transfer performance depends on overlap between practice and target conditions.

Relevant dimensions can include:

- movement direction;
- force/load;
- speed;
- timing;
- sensory context;
- equipment;
- task goal;
- target geometry.

PMID 42607302 showed robust practice-dependent gains with substantial transfer, but transfer remained constrained by movement direction.

PMID 41351896 shows why variability does not automatically beat specificity when the target is one fixed criterion condition.

Thus specificity and variability are not opposites to “choose once.”

A practical design can combine:

- sufficient target-condition practice for specificity;
- selected relevant variation for transfer robustness.

Canonical rule:

**practice must include the actual target task/condition; variation is justified by the transfer demands that matter.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 13. Fatigue as a modifier of motor learning

Fatigue affects practice organization because it can change:

- current performance;
- sensory precision;
- force production;
- coordination;
- attention;
- error structure.

The 2026 systematic review (PMID 42480681; 43 experiments) found during acquisition:

- 65.1% detrimental effects;
- 9.3% facilitatory;
- 16.3% neutral;
- 9.3% mixed/inconclusive.

Retention effects depended on:

- fatigue modality;
- task;
- whether retention was tested rested or fatigued.

Local neuromuscular fatigue appeared more consistently disruptive than cardiovascular fatigue.

The 2026 fatigue-practice RCT (PMID 42811472) found cardiovascular-fatigue practice neither reliably impaired learning nor produced a clear fatigue-resilience advantage.

Therefore:

**fatigue is not simply “bad for learning,” but high fatigue can reduce the quality/informativeness of practice and must be separated from durable learning.**

For strength technique this is especially relevant because local neuromuscular fatigue directly changes the movement-control problem.

Canonical rule:

**do not assume technique learned in a fresh state transfers unchanged to fatigue, and do not assume practicing fatigued automatically builds robustness.**

Confidence: **HIGH for state/task dependence / LOW-MODERATE for optimal RT fatigue exposure.**

## 14. When additional practice stops providing useful signal

There is no universal number of repetitions after which motor practice becomes useless.

The relevant issue is marginal value.

Additional practice can still contribute through:

- further refinement;
- consolidation;
- retrieval;
- robustness;
- transfer exposure;
- reacquisition after error.

But marginal value can decline when:

- acquisition has plateaued;
- task is too easy;
- errors become uninformative;
- fatigue dominates;
- practice no longer resembles the target transfer condition;
- added repetitions repeat the same solved condition without a relevant learning objective.

### Evidence boundary

Dose research is heterogeneous.

- stroke dose modeling (PMID 32830617) supports greater efficacy but lower efficiency with more practice;
- older-adult practice-to-plateau/overpractice RCT (PMID 36189036) suggests individualized dose can outperform low dose in some contexts;
- real-world video-game data (PMID 34987368) show nonlinear, diminishing daily gains.

None yields a universal stopping threshold for healthy resistance-exercise technique.

### Operational project rule

Continue practice while additional trials plausibly provide one or more of:

1. target-specific refinement;
2. error information;
3. useful retrieval/reconstruction;
4. relevant variation/transfer challenge;
5. fatigue-state robustness if that state is intentionally targeted.

Reduce/stop/reorganize practice when additional trials primarily add:

- fatigue;
- deteriorating execution;
- repetitive uninformative success;
- repetitive uncorrected failure.

Status: **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not a validated universal threshold.

## 15. Practice frequency × volume × distribution are different variables

These terms must not be collapsed.

Example:

- 60 trials in one session;
- 20 trials on three consecutive days;
- 20 trials on three nonconsecutive days

have equal volume but different frequency/distribution.

Likewise:

- same number of sessions can contain different volume;
- same trial count can use different inter-trial rest;
- same total time can contain different successful repetitions.

Project rule:

**report at least total trials/target executions, session count, session spacing and within-session rest when interpreting practice organization.**

## 16. Blocked/random and constant/variable are not identical contrasts

Another important boundary:

### Blocked vs random
Primarily concerns **order** of multiple task variants.

### Constant vs variable
Primarily concerns whether **one or multiple task conditions/parameter values** are practiced.

Possible designs include:

- constant practice only;
- variable-blocked;
- variable-random;
- progressive/serial schedules.

Therefore contextual interference should not be conflated with variability-of-practice theory.

Canonical rule:

**practice content and practice order are separate dimensions.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 17. Applied resistance-exercise boundary

Direct resistance-exercise evidence for NEU-13 is limited.

### Squat

PMID 37916599 confirms:

- squat technique variables can be acquired;
- some can be retained after feedback.

But it does not compare:

- distributed vs massed;
- blocked vs random;
- constant vs variable;
- whole vs part.

### Kettlebell swing

PMID 42617501 supports a relationship between early solution variability and later altered-implement transfer, but does not randomize practice variability.

### Isometric knee extension

PMID 42836868 compared random/serial/constant **intensity order** acutely.

It found:

- improved force accuracy across conditions;
- no condition-specific immediate motor-accuracy advantage;
- random practice increased enjoyment;
- no long-term retention conclusion from one acute session.

Therefore:

**general motor-learning practice principles can guide RT technique practice, but exact scheduling prescriptions require downstream direct testing/individualization.**

Confidence: **HIGH for evidence-scarcity boundary**.

## 18. Practical synthesis for later strength-technique coaching

Current evidence supports an adaptive schedule rather than one universal template.

A defensible future sequence can be:

1. enough stable/blocked exposure to understand and execute a new task;
2. repeated whole-task practice when safely possible;
3. part/regression drills only when they solve a specific bottleneck;
4. increasing relevant variation/interleaving when retention/transfer to variants matters;
5. sufficient target-load/target-condition exposure to preserve specificity;
6. avoid letting fatigue turn the task into a different coordination problem unless fatigued robustness is intentionally being trained/tested;
7. judge success by delayed retention/target transfer, not only same-session smoothness.

This is a **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not proof of one optimal sequence.

## 19. Important non-equivalences

1. More trials ≠ proportionally more learning.
2. Higher practice frequency ≠ automatically better learning.
3. Distributed practice ≠ universally superior motor learning.
4. Massed practice ≠ universally harmful.
5. Blocked practice ≠ bad learning.
6. Random practice ≠ universally superior.
7. Random practice ≠ random exercise chaos.
8. Contextual interference ≠ variability of practice.
9. Variable practice ≠ always better retention.
10. Constant practice ≠ poor transfer in every task.
11. Part practice ≠ easier recombination into the whole task.
12. Whole practice ≠ always best for high complexity.
13. Repetition ≠ identical kinematics.
14. Variability ≠ automatically useful practice.
15. Fresh-state learning ≠ fatigued-state robustness.
16. Practice plateau ≠ proof that no further consolidation/transfer benefit is possible.
17. Acquisition improvement ≠ durable learning.

These boundaries are closure-critical.

## 20. Measurement boundaries

### Practice frequency
Must state time scale.

### Practice volume
Must state unit: trials, repetitions, blocks, minutes or successful executions.

### Massed/distributed
Must state exact rest/session spacing.

### Blocked/random
Requires multiple task variants; order is the manipulated dimension.

### Constant/variable
Requires definition of what parameter/task dimension varies.

### Whole/part
Must define component decomposition and how recombination is tested.

### Retention
Must be delayed and defined.

### Transfer
Must specify target variation.

### Fatigue
Must verify fatigue manipulation/state rather than assume it from session length.

### Plateau
Depends on outcome window/noise; it is not a biological endpoint.

### HIGH

1. Practice performance and durable learning must remain separate when comparing schedules.
2. Frequency, volume and distribution are different practice dimensions.
3. Blocked/random order and constant/variable content are different manipulations.
4. High contextual interference has an average retention benefit, but it is not universal.
5. Applied transfer effects of CI are smaller/less certain than laboratory effects.
6. Variable practice is not universally superior for retention of one criterion task.
7. Target-task specificity remains necessary.
8. Fatigue effects on learning are task/state dependent.
9. No universal repetition threshold defines when practice becomes useless.

### MODERATE-HIGH

10. Additional practice often shows diminishing marginal efficiency.
11. Random/interleaved practice can support retention by increasing retrieval/reconstruction demands.
12. Relevant variation can support transfer/generalization.
13. Practice distribution effects depend on task and spacing scale.

### MODERATE

14. Whole-vs-part choice depends on task organization and component separability.
15. A hybrid schedule moving from stable exposure toward relevant variability can be practical for technique learning, but no universal sequence is proven.
