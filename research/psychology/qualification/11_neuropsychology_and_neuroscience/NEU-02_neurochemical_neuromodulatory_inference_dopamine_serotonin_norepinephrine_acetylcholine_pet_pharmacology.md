# NEU-02 — Neurochemical and neuromodulatory inference: dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, acetylcholine, PET/pharmacology, release, occupancy, and causal attribution

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public neuroscience evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

NEU-01 established that activation, connectivity, lesions, stimulation, electrophysiology, and decoding provide different kinds of evidence.

NEU-02 addresses the neurochemical layer:

**What can neurotransmitter, receptor, transporter, PET, pharmacological-challenge, and neuromodulatory evidence legitimately establish about psychological function, and what cannot be inferred from a neurotransmitter label, receptor occupancy, radioligand displacement, or drug effect?**

High-risk errors to prevent:
- "dopamine = reward";
- "dopamine = pleasure";
- "dopamine = motivation";
- "serotonin = happiness";
- "norepinephrine = arousal";
- "acetylcholine = memory";
- "more transmitter = more of the psychological function";
- "low transmitter = psychiatric symptom";
- "one transmitter has one psychological role";
- "one receptor family has one behavioral effect";
- "agonist means more function";
- "antagonist means less function";
- "partial agonist is simply a weak agonist";
- "transporter occupancy = transmitter concentration";
- "receptor availability = receptor density";
- "PET binding potential = neurotransmitter level";
- "PET displacement = exact transmitter concentration change";
- "receptor occupancy = downstream functional effect";
- "drug dose = brain exposure";
- "plasma concentration = synaptic concentration";
- "peripheral monoamine level = brain monoamine level";
- "acute pharmacology = chronic physiology";
- "systemic drug effect identifies one brain circuit";
- "pharmacological fMRI localizes receptor mechanism";
- "tonic and phasic are fixed psychological channels";
- "RPE = all dopamine signaling";
- "LC tonic/phasic mode = complete noradrenergic architecture";
- "nicotinic agonist = more acetylcholine-like cognition";
- "occupancy threshold is universal across drugs, regions, and outcomes";
- "neurochemical association = biomarker or diagnosis".

## Core rule

**A neurotransmitter is a signaling molecule embedded in receptor-, cell-, projection-, region-, timescale-, and state-specific circuits. Neurochemical evidence must therefore specify what is measured: synthesis, vesicular release, extracellular concentration, receptor availability, receptor occupancy, transporter availability, enzyme activity, downstream signaling, or behavior. PET and pharmacology can provide strong target-engagement and perturbation evidence, but they do not collapse these quantities into one scalar "neurotransmitter level" or one psychological function.**

Canonical reasoning:

\`define psychological process -> specify transmitter + circuit + receptor/transporter + timescale -> identify what the assay/drug directly measures or perturbs -> separate availability, occupancy, release, concentration, and downstream efficacy -> model regional/receptor heterogeneity, pharmacokinetics and compensatory effects -> compare placebo/control and alternative transmitters/processes -> integrate convergent neural/behavioral evidence -> state only the narrowest neurochemical claim supported\`.

## 1. Neurotransmitter label is not a psychological construct

Dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, and acetylcholine are:
- chemical signals;
- released in multiple circuits;
- acting at multiple receptor subtypes;
- affecting multiple physiological and psychological functions.

They are not interchangeable with:
- reward;
- mood;
- arousal;
- memory.

A psychological construct requires its own valid definition.

## 2. One transmitter can implement multiple computations

The same transmitter can affect:
- learning;
- motivation;
- attention;
- movement;
- perception;
- autonomic state;
- endocrine regulation.

Which effect occurs depends on:
- projection;
- receptor;
- cell type;
- local circuit;
- timing;
- concentration;
- organism state.

## 3. One psychological process can depend on multiple transmitters

Attention can involve:
- norepinephrine;
- acetylcholine;
- dopamine;
- glutamate;
- GABA

at different levels.

Reward learning can involve:
- dopamine;
- glutamate;
- opioids;
- serotonin;
- local circuit mechanisms.

Therefore:
**one function = one transmitter**
is generally invalid.

## 4. Neuromodulation differs from a simple on/off synapse

Neuromodulators can alter:
- excitability;
- synaptic gain;
- plasticity;
- network state;
- receptor trafficking;
- release probability.

Effects can unfold over:
- milliseconds;
- seconds;
- minutes;
- longer adaptive timescales.

"More transmitter" does not imply a fixed linear behavioral direction.

## 5. Volume transmission complicates source→target mapping

Neuromodulators can act beyond a single classical synaptic cleft through:
- extrasynaptic diffusion;
- broad axonal arborization;
- local uptake;
- receptor distribution.

The 2024 review of neuromodulatory volume transmission emphasizes that extracellular geometry, release, degradation, and receptor affinity shape effective signaling.

Chemical presence therefore does not identify one synaptic pathway.

## 6. Co-transmission matters

Neurons classified by one transmitter can also release:
- glutamate;
- GABA;
- peptides;
- other modulators

depending on circuit and timescale.

A behavior after activating a "dopamine neuron" or "LC neuron" is not automatically attributable only to dopamine or norepinephrine.

## 7. Neurotransmitter systems are anatomically heterogeneous

Modern circuit work increasingly shows:
- projection-specific cell groups;
- transcriptional heterogeneity;
- target-specific release;
- different behavioral correlates.

A transmitter system should not be modeled as one homogeneous global broadcast.

## 8. Chemical identity ≠ signal semantics

The same dopamine pulse can have different consequences in:
- dorsal striatum;
- ventral striatum;
- prefrontal cortex.

The same serotonin signal can act differently through:
- 5-HT1A;
- 5-HT2A;
- 5-HT3;
- other receptors.

Meaning emerges from circuit and receptor context.

# Dopamine

## 9. Dopamine is not "the reward chemical"

Dopamine contributes to:
- learning;
- motivation;
- action selection;
- movement;
- salience/context-sensitive processes;
- interoceptive signaling.

Reward-related dopamine evidence is strong.

It does not justify:
**dopamine = reward/pleasure**.

## 10. Dopaminergic pathways are functionally segregated

The 2025 Trends in Neurosciences review emphasizes distinct:
- nigrostriatal;
- mesolimbic;
- mesocortical

pathways and finer circuit-specific organization.

Dopamine should therefore be localized by pathway/target, not treated as a whole-brain scalar.

## 11. Midbrain dopamine neurons are heterogeneous

Different dopamine neurons can differ in:
- projection target;
- inputs;
- molecular phenotype;
- firing response.

Population-average firing should not be assigned to every dopaminergic neuron.

## 12. Phasic dopamine has strong reward-learning evidence

Fast dopamine transients can encode reward-related teaching signals under many paradigms.

In striatal learning:
- unexpected reward;
- reward-predicting cues;
- omitted expected reward

can produce prediction-error-like dynamics.

This supports a major learning role.

It does not mean every phasic event is one scalar RPE.

## 13. Dopamine prediction errors can contain information beyond scalar value

The 2025 Nature Reviews Neuroscience review argues that transient dopaminergic signals can carry associative information beyond integrated value.

Thus:
**dopamine transient = scalar value RPE only**
is too restrictive.

This extends LEA's earlier boundary.

## 14. Dopamine timescales remain theoretically contested

The 2025 review on striatal dopamine timescales distinguishes:
- fast phasic transients;
- seconds-scale ramps;
- slower tonic shifts.

The field does not have full consensus that these are independent psychological channels.

Do not assign one unique mental function to each timescale by convention.

## 15. Tonic/phasic terminology is partly measurement-dependent

Fast-scan methods, sensors, electrophysiology, and PET sample different bandwidths.

A "tonic" estimate in one method may not be equivalent to:
- baseline extracellular concentration;
- firing rate;
- slow receptor occupancy

in another.

Define the measured signal.

## 16. Dopamine release can be locally regulated

The 2024 review of striatal dopamine emphasizes that local:
- cholinergic;
- GABAergic;
- metabolic/hormonal

mechanisms can modulate terminal release.

Terminal dopamine concentration can therefore diverge from midbrain cell-body firing.

## 17. Cell-body activity ≠ local dopamine release

Somatic firing is one determinant of release.

Local:
- presynaptic receptors;
- interneurons;
- uptake;
- terminal modulation

also matter.

A neural firing measure and transmitter-release measure are related but nonidentical.

## 18. Dopamine receptor subtype matters

Dopamine acts through multiple receptor subtypes with different:
- affinities;
- cellular distributions;
- signaling pathways;
- pre/postsynaptic roles.

A D1-targeted result and D2/D3-targeted result are not interchangeable.

## 19. Motivation and reinforcement can dissociate at receptor/circuit level

A 2025 Nature Neuroscience mouse study found dissociable roles of:
- D3 receptor signaling in motivated behavior;
- D1 receptor signaling in reinforcement

within ventral striatal circuitry.

This directly blocks:
**one dopamine signal = one reward construct**.

Translation to humans requires caution.

## 20. Dopamine effect can be nonlinear with baseline state

Receptor occupancy and behavioral effects can depend on:
- baseline dopamine;
- receptor affinity;
- saturation;
- opposing pathway balance.

This can produce:
- nonlinear;
- inverted-U-like;
- state-dependent

effects.

"More dopamine = better/worse" is not a general rule.

## 21. Dopamine is not equivalent to pleasure

Pleasure/hedonic impact depends strongly on:
- opioid;
- endocannabinoid;
- sensory;
- cortical/striatal

mechanisms in addition to dopamine.

Dopamine can strongly affect incentive/motivation without being identical to hedonic experience.

# Serotonin

## 22. Serotonin is not "the happiness chemical"

Serotonin is involved in:
- learning;
- sensory processing;
- behavioral-state regulation;
- patience/inhibition under some paradigms;
- affective functions;
- sleep;
- gastrointestinal/vascular physiology.

No single mood label captures its biology.

## 23. Serotonin has extensive receptor diversity

Humans have multiple 5-HT receptor families/subtypes.

Most are GPCRs.
5-HT3 is ionotropic.

Receptor subtypes differ in:
- signaling;
- distribution;
- cell type;
- affinity;
- timescale.

Therefore:
**serotonin increased**
does not specify the functional consequence.

## 24. Receptor distribution creates functional specificity

Human/animal receptor-network work shows that distinct serotonin receptor distributions can support different brain-wide functional patterns.

A global serotonin signal can be partitioned by receptor geography.

## 25. Raphe systems are embedded in distributed networks

Current 2026 review work frames raphe nuclei as participating in:
- value;
- uncertainty;
- threat;
- reward context;
- behavioral-state persistence/switching.

Serotonin should not be localized to one affective function.

## 26. Endogenous serotonin and serotonergic drugs are not equivalent signals

A drug can differ from endogenous serotonin in:
- receptor profile;
- concentration;
- timing;
- spatial distribution;
- metabolism;
- receptor efficacy.

The 2026 review of psychedelic action explicitly emphasizes that psychedelic receptor selectivity and spatiotemporal action differ from endogenous serotonin.

## 27. 5-HT2A signaling is functionally selective

The 2025 Pharmacological Reviews synthesis describes:
- agonism;
- antagonism;
- inverse agonism;
- biased/functional selectivity

at 5-HT2A receptors.

Therefore:
**same receptor occupancy**
does not guarantee
**same intracellular or behavioral effect**.

## 28. Occupying a receptor does not define efficacy

Two ligands can occupy similar proportions of receptors but differ in:
- intrinsic efficacy;
- signaling bias;
- off-target binding;
- kinetics.

Occupancy measures target engagement, not the entire pharmacodynamic response.

## 29. Serotonergic receptor occupancy can be regionally variable

PET studies can show:
- different occupancy across regions;
- substantial individual variability.

A global occupancy summary can hide spatial heterogeneity.

## 30. SERT occupancy is not synaptic serotonin concentration

A drug occupying the serotonin transporter changes reuptake dynamics.

But SERT occupancy does not directly measure:
- extracellular 5-HT concentration;
- receptor activation;
- psychological effect.

Transporter, transmitter, receptor, and behavior are different layers.

# Norepinephrine

## 31. Norepinephrine is not "arousal"

LC-NE participates in:
- arousal;
- attention;
- sensory prioritization;
- learning;
- stress response;
- pain;
- sleep-wake regulation;
- flexible behavior.

Arousal is one function among several.

## 32. LC is not a homogeneous broadcast nucleus

The 2025 literature emphasizes:
- projection specificity;
- molecular diversity;
- heterogeneous firing;
- target-specific modulation.

The old "uniform global NE" model is inadequate.

## 33. 2026 topographic LC evidence

A 2026 Nature mouse study found:
- individual LC-NE neurons had extensive but regionally constrained projection sets;
- dorsal/ventral LC populations differed in activity correlates;
- some cortical-projecting neurons encoded choice switching and reward-prediction-error-like signals.

This supports topographic functional organization.

It does not imply a one-to-one map between one LC subregion and one psychological function.

## 34. Tonic/phasic LC schema is useful but incomplete

Traditional accounts often contrast:
- tonic LC activity;
- phasic bursts.

A 2026 cross-species pooled study found a broader manifold of LC firing patterns influenced by:
- species;
- sex;
- age;
- preparation/context.

Therefore:
**LC = two fixed firing modes**
is too simple.

## 35. LC-NE effects depend on receptor and target circuit

NE acts through multiple adrenergic receptor classes/subtypes.

These differ in:
- affinity;
- pre/postsynaptic role;
- cellular signaling;
- region.

A single LC firing measure cannot specify downstream effect without target context.

## 36. LC neurons can co-release other transmitters

Recent circuit evidence shows some LC neurons can have fast glutamatergic effects alongside slower noradrenergic modulation in specific pathways.

Therefore:
**LC stimulation effect = NE effect**
is not universally valid.

## 37. Pupillometry is not direct norepinephrine measurement

Pupil diameter can correlate with:
- arousal;
- LC-linked activity;
- luminance;
- autonomic state;
- cognitive effort.

Pupil change is an indirect proxy, not a quantitative NE concentration measure.

## 38. Peripheral norepinephrine ≠ central LC-NE signaling

Plasma catecholamines reflect peripheral sympathetic processes.

They do not directly quantify:
- cortical NE;
- LC firing;
- synaptic NE release.

Central and peripheral compartments must be separated.

# Acetylcholine

## 39. Acetylcholine is not "the memory chemical"

ACh contributes to:
- attention;
- perceptual detection;
- cue updating;
- learning/memory;
- arousal;
- cortical-state modulation.

Memory is one domain.

## 40. Basal forebrain cholinergic system is anatomically structured

The 2025 human review describes multiple basal forebrain cholinergic cell groups with preferential projection patterns.

The cholinergic system is not one homogeneous nucleus.

## 41. Basal forebrain cholinergic subtypes can have distinct connectivity

2025 whole-brain mapping in animals identified distinct projection-defined cholinergic subtypes with different:
- inputs;
- outputs;
- electrophysiological properties.

This supports circuit-specific cholinergic modulation.

## 42. Nicotinic and muscarinic receptors are fundamentally different receptor families

Nicotinic receptors:
- ligand-gated ion channels;
- multiple subunit assemblies.

Muscarinic receptors:
- GPCRs;
- multiple subtypes.

"Cholinergic effect" must specify receptor class when possible.

## 43. Nicotinic receptor stoichiometry can alter function

Recent work shows that even similar nicotinic subunit combinations can form functionally distinguishable receptor assemblies.

Thus receptor subtype labels may still hide functional heterogeneity.

## 44. Nicotinic agonism can cause desensitization

An agonist can:
- activate receptor acutely;
- also promote receptor desensitization.

Therefore:
**agonist = sustained increase in receptor function**
is invalid.

## 45. Human cholinergic pharmacology is not a simple agonist/antagonist story

The 2025 systematic review of human pharmacological studies found:
- ACh manipulation affected attention/perception/updating;
- nicotinic and muscarinic manipulations produced heterogeneous effects;
- some antagonist effects were not the simple inverse of agonist effects;
- pharmacological specificity and inability to mimic endogenous dynamics limited inference.

## 46. Receptor-specific cholinergic effects remain context-dependent

Effect depends on:
- receptor subtype;
- dose;
- baseline state;
- cognitive task;
- brain region;
- acute/chronic exposure.

One cholinergic manipulation cannot define "the ACh function."

# PET / molecular imaging foundations

## 47. PET measures radioligand kinetics/binding, not transmitter directly by default

Neuroreceptor PET measures the behavior of a radiolabeled ligand.

Depending on tracer/model, PET can estimate quantities related to:
- receptor/transporter availability;
- distribution volume;
- binding potential;
- occupancy;
- challenge-induced displacement.

It does not simply "measure dopamine/serotonin levels."

## 48. Binding potential is a model-derived quantity

The consensus PET nomenclature defines binding-potential terms relative to different reference concentrations.

Common \`BP_ND\` is related to:
- available binding sites;
- ligand affinity;
- nondisplaceable free fraction.

Thus:
**BP_ND = receptor density**
is incomplete.

## 49. Receptor availability ≠ total receptor expression

Available receptors can be affected by:
- endogenous neurotransmitter competition;
- internalization;
- affinity state;
- drug occupancy.

PET often estimates **available binding**, not all receptors physically present.

## 50. Reference-region assumptions matter

Reference-tissue models assume a region with:
- negligible specific binding;
- appropriate nondisplaceable kinetics.

Violation can bias BP estimates.

"Reference region" is a modeling assumption, not a guaranteed biological blank.

## 51. Tracer selectivity matters

A radioligand can bind:
- multiple receptor subtypes;
- off-target sites.

Interpretation depends on:
- affinity profile;
- regional receptor composition.

A "D2/3" tracer does not separately quantify D2 and D3 without additional information.

## 52. Tracer agonist/antagonist properties matter

Agonist and antagonist radiotracers can differ in sensitivity to:
- receptor states;
- endogenous transmitter competition.

Therefore two PET tracers targeting nominally the same receptor can yield different physiological sensitivity.

## 53. PET temporal resolution is slow relative to phasic neurotransmission

PET integrates over:
- minutes;
- scan windows.

Fast millisecond/second transients can be invisible or appear only as integrated displacement effects.

PET cannot directly resolve individual phasic dopamine spikes.

## 54. Receptor occupancy

Drug receptor occupancy is typically estimated from the reduction in specific radioligand binding between:
- baseline;
- drug condition.

This is strong evidence of:
**target engagement**.

It is not by itself evidence of:
- therapeutic effect;
- behavioral mechanism;
- direction of downstream signaling.

## 55. Occupancy is nonlinear with drug concentration

Dose/occupancy relationships typically saturate.

A doubling of dose does not imply:
- doubled occupancy;
- doubled effect.

PK/PD models are needed.

## 56. Occupancy and function can have different dose-response curves

A 2026 simultaneous PET/fMRI study of D2 blockade found substantial functional hemodynamic changes emerged nonlinearly at higher receptor occupancy.

Thus:
**target engagement and downstream functional response are distinct dose-response functions**.

## 57. Occupancy threshold is drug/outcome-specific

A receptor-occupancy range useful for one:
- drug class;
- clinical outcome

does not become a universal neurobiological threshold.

Partial agonists can behave differently from antagonists even at similar occupancy.

## 58. Receptor occupancy can coexist with endogenous transmitter

Endogenous transmitter already occupies some receptor sites.

Drug, tracer, and endogenous ligand compete dynamically.

Occupancy should not be interpreted as an isolated two-ligand system when endogenous signaling matters.

## 59. Endogenous transmitter release via displacement PET

Challenge PET can infer endogenous release when:
- a transmitter competes with radioligand;
- BP decreases in a tracer-sensitive way.

This is an indirect inference.

The mapping:
\`ΔBP -> Δtransmitter concentration\`
is not universally linear.

## 60. Dopamine displacement PET has tracer-specific sensitivity

[11C]raclopride is widely used for striatal D2/3 receptor competition.

It can detect:
- strong pharmacological dopamine release;
- some behavioral/cognitive challenges.

Sensitivity is much lower for subtle naturalistic changes.

## 61. 2026 raclopride meta-analysis calibrates challenge magnitude

A meta-analysis of:
- 92 [11C]raclopride PET studies;
- N=1,640

reported approximate dopamine-release-associated BP changes:
- amphetamine: 10.9%;
- methylphenidate: 11.5%;
- ketamine: 9.8%;
- alcohol: 4.8%;
- cognitive challenges: 2.7%.

This is a measurement calibration, not a universal biological hierarchy.

## 62. Reward vs non-reward cognitive PET discrimination was weak

In the 2026 meta-analysis, cognitive tasks with and without reward did not differ significantly in ventral-striatal or overall-striatal dopamine release.

This suggests:
**[11C]raclopride PET has limited sensitivity for distinguishing reward-specific from other cognitive dopamine release**.

It does not imply reward has no dopaminergic component.

## 63. PET displacement can be confounded by receptor regulation

Animal PET work shows agonist-induced:
- receptor desensitization;
- internalization;
- affinity changes

can alter radioligand binding.

Therefore:
**lower BP after challenge = pure extracellular transmitter increase**
can be too simple.

## 64. Serotonin-release PET has improved but remains tracer/challenge-dependent

A 2026 review reports that newer:
- agonist radioligands;
- antagonist tracers;
- optimized pharmacological challenges

have improved in-vivo sensitivity to 5-HT release.

However, optimal strategies and interpretation remain tracer-specific.

## 65. Feasibility of release imaging does not imply direct concentration measurement

A reproducible PET release signal can establish:
- challenge-sensitive serotonergic dynamics.

It still does not yield a universal direct "5-HT concentration" readout across:
- regions;
- tracers;
- receptor states.

## 66. Transporter PET measures transporter availability/occupancy

DAT, SERT, and NET tracers can estimate:
- transporter availability;
- drug occupancy.

These quantities are not equivalent to:
- synthesis;
- vesicular stores;
- synaptic release;
- receptor signaling.

## 67. Transporter blockade changes dynamics, not one scalar level

Blocking a transporter can alter:
- clearance;
- extracellular persistence;
- autoreceptor feedback;
- release.

Net transmitter effect can vary by:
- region;
- baseline firing;
- receptor context.

## 68. Plasma drug concentration is not receptor occupancy

Plasma concentration is an exposure proxy.

Brain occupancy depends on:
- blood-brain penetration;
- free fraction;
- affinity;
- active metabolites;
- kinetics.

PET occupancy studies can directly constrain this relation.

## 69. Brain extracellular concentration is not trivially inferred from plasma

A 2026 mechanistic analysis of dopamine D2 ligands examined the relationship between:
- extracellular brain concentration;
- PET-inferred receptor occupancy.

The need for model-based analysis itself reinforces that:
**plasma/brain concentration/occupancy are distinct quantities**.

## 70. Peripheral transmitter levels are especially weak proxies

Blood/urine/platelet measures of monoamines can reflect:
- peripheral synthesis;
- metabolism;
- autonomic physiology.

They generally cannot be treated as direct measures of:
- brain synaptic neurotransmission.

## 71. Cerebrospinal-fluid measures are also not synaptic readouts

CSF metabolites/transmitters can provide system-level information.

But they integrate:
- multiple sources;
- diffusion;
- metabolism;
- clearance.

CSF concentration does not specify a local neural circuit.

# Pharmacology

## 72. Drug dose ≠ brain target perturbation

Administered dose must pass through:
- absorption;
- distribution;
- metabolism;
- blood-brain barrier;
- target binding.

Behavior should not be attributed to a receptor solely from nominal dose.

## 73. Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics are different

Pharmacokinetics:
**what the body does to the drug**.

Pharmacodynamics:
**what the drug does to targets/system**.

A time-matched behavioral effect needs both.

## 74. Agonist does not simply mean "increase transmitter function"

An agonist activates a receptor.

Consequences depend on:
- receptor location;
- presynaptic vs postsynaptic role;
- feedback;
- desensitization;
- signaling bias.

An agonist at an inhibitory autoreceptor can reduce transmitter release.

## 75. Antagonist does not simply mean "decrease neurotransmission"

Blocking one receptor can:
- reduce one pathway;
- disinhibit another;
- increase transmitter release through feedback;
- shift network balance.

Behavioral direction is circuit-dependent.

## 76. Partial agonist is not simply a weak full agonist

A partial agonist can act as:
- agonist under low endogenous stimulation;
- functional antagonist relative to a full agonist under high stimulation

depending on receptor reserve/context.

Its behavioral effect cannot be inferred from occupancy alone.

## 77. Inverse agonist differs from neutral antagonist

Inverse agonists reduce constitutive receptor activity.

Neutral antagonists block agonist effects without necessarily altering constitutive activity.

Pharmacological labels matter for mechanism.

## 78. Biased agonism/functionally selective signaling

A ligand can favor:
- one intracellular pathway;
- another pathway;
- receptor trafficking state.

Therefore:
**same receptor + same occupancy = same downstream biology**
is invalid.

## 79. Receptor reserve changes occupancy–effect relationships

Some tissues/circuits can show near-maximal effect before near-maximal receptor occupancy.

Other effects may require high occupancy.

Functional response and occupancy can therefore be strongly non-proportional.

## 80. Autoreceptors create feedback effects

Presynaptic autoreceptors can regulate:
- firing;
- synthesis;
- release.

A receptor-active drug can alter the transmitter system indirectly through feedback.

This can reverse simplistic agonist/antagonist predictions.

## 81. Acute and chronic pharmacology differ

Chronic drug exposure can produce:
- receptor up/down-regulation;
- transporter adaptation;
- signaling changes;
- gene-expression changes;
- network compensation.

Acute challenge results should not be assumed to model chronic treatment physiology.

## 82. Withdrawal/discontinuation can alter availability

PET studies after drug discontinuation show receptor availability can change over:
- days;
- weeks.

Post-treatment binding is not necessarily the original baseline.

Homeostatic adaptation matters.

## 83. Polypharmacology is common

Many CNS drugs bind:
- multiple receptor subtypes;
- transporters;
- enzymes.

A systemic drug effect should not be assigned to one target unless:
- selectivity;
- occupancy;
- antagonist/blocking evidence

support that inference.

## 84. Metabolites can be pharmacologically active

Parent drug concentration alone can miss:
- active metabolite exposure.

PK/PD interpretation should include active moieties where relevant.

## 85. Peripheral drug effects can change behavior

A CNS-active drug may also alter:
- heart rate;
- blood pressure;
- nausea;
- sedation;
- autonomic state.

Behavioral change can be secondary to peripheral/interoceptive effects.

## 86. Blinding can fail in psychoactive pharmacology

If participants can identify:
- active drug;
- placebo

through subjective effects, expectancy can influence:
- ratings;
- task behavior.

Active placebos or expectancy controls may be needed.

## 87. Pharmacological fMRI is downstream systems evidence

phMRI can show:
- hemodynamic/network responses to drug challenge.

It does not directly identify:
- receptor occupancy;
- neurotransmitter release

unless combined with molecular imaging/other measures.

## 88. Simultaneous PET/MRI can connect target engagement and systems response

Concurrent PET/MRI can measure:
- occupancy/binding;
- hemodynamic response

in the same experiment.

This strengthens cross-level inference.

It still does not by itself identify the complete causal chain from receptor to behavior.

## 89. Receptor occupancy and subjective effect can dissociate

Different agonists at the same receptor can produce different:
- network;
- physiological;
- subjective

effects at comparable occupancy because efficacy/selectivity/kinetics differ.

Occupancy is necessary evidence for target engagement, not sufficient explanation of experience.

## 90. Dose-response can be nonmonotonic

Neuromodulatory systems often involve:
- saturation;
- opposing receptor subtypes;
- feedback;
- baseline dependence.

Behavioral dose-response can therefore be:
- inverted-U;
- plateauing;
- biphasic.

Linear "more drug -> more psychological function" assumptions are unsafe.

## 91. Species translation requires receptor/circuit comparison

Animal pharmacology can provide:
- invasive release measures;
- cell-specific causality.

Translation to humans must check:
- receptor expression;
- pharmacokinetics;
- circuit homology;
- behavior/task equivalence.

Same drug dose per kg does not guarantee same neural perturbation.

## 94. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- neurotransmitter identity is not psychological-function identity;
- dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, and acetylcholine are receptor/circuit/timescale heterogeneous;
- dopamine RPE evidence is strong but does not exhaust dopamine signaling;
- receptor subtype and projection target materially alter neuromodulator function;
- PET binding potential/availability is not direct transmitter concentration;
- receptor occupancy is target engagement rather than downstream efficacy;
- PET displacement is indirect and tracer/challenge-dependent;
- transporter occupancy is not synonymous with synaptic transmitter level;
- agonist/antagonist/partial-agonist labels do not determine global behavioral direction;
- acute and chronic pharmacology can differ through adaptation;
- systemic drug effects can contain off-target and peripheral contributions.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- specific dopamine transients can support reward-learning computations;
- specific dopamine receptors can dissociate motivation and reinforcement in animal circuitry;
- serotonin receptor-network geography can create functionally distinct modes;
- LC-NE projections are topographically/functionally heterogeneous;
- cholinergic modulation can affect attention, perception and updating in humans;
- challenge PET can quantify relative dopamine/serotonin release under validated paradigms;
- simultaneous PET/MRI can connect occupancy with downstream systems response.

### Not established as universal
- one transmitter/function mapping;
- one fixed tonic/phasic computational code;
- one occupancy threshold for all drugs/outcomes;
- one direct BP→transmitter concentration conversion;
- one PET tracer equally sensitive across regions/challenges;
- one systemic pharmacological manipulation uniquely identifying a receptor mechanism;
- one peripheral/CSF marker that directly indexes local synaptic monoamine signaling.
