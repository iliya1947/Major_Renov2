## 2. Structural hierarchy

Canonical hierarchy:

`whole muscle → fascicles → muscle fibers/myofibers → myofibrils → sarcomeres → myofilaments`

Connective tissue:

- epimysium → whole muscle;
- perimysium → fascicle-level organization;
- endomysium → individual myofiber environment.

Myofibrils contain serial sarcomeres.

Sarcomere:
- bounded by Z-discs;
- contains thin/thick filaments and associated proteins.

Project rule:

**muscle fiber = one multinucleated muscle cell; fascicle = bundle of fibers.**

Do not use these terms interchangeably.

---

## 3. Connective-tissue continuity

Endomysial/perimysial/epimysial networks are structurally continuous with tendon/aponeurotic connective tissues.

Implication:

**force transmission is not only “fiber tip → tendon”.**

Muscle is a composite contractile + connective-tissue organ.

Detailed lateral force transmission / ECM mechanics → MEC/PHY.

---

## 4. Origin / insertion terminology

Traditional anatomical language:

- **origin** = conventionally more proximal / relatively fixed attachment;
- **insertion** = conventionally more distal / relatively mobile attachment.

But in closed-chain or reversed tasks, the “insertion” can be mechanically more fixed.

Project rule:

**origin/insertion are anatomical naming conventions, not guarantees about which attachment moves in a real exercise.**

This prevents later technique reasoning from confusing nomenclature with mechanics.

---

## 5. Monoarticular vs multiarticular muscles

### Monoarticular
Cross one principal joint.

Examples:
- soleus relative to knee–ankle comparison;
- vasti at knee.

### Multiarticular
Cross two or more joints.

Examples:
- rectus femoris;
- gastrocnemius;
- long-head biceps;
- long-head triceps;
- most hip-crossing hamstrings.

Project consequence:

**multiarticular muscle geometry depends on the positions of all crossed joints.**

Detailed active/passive insufficiency → ANA-13/MEC/PHY.

---

## 6. Fascicle vs fiber terminology

### Fiber / myofiber
One skeletal muscle cell.

### Fascicle
Bundle of many fibers.

Ultrasound usually visualizes/tracks a **fascicle path**, not a single myofiber.

Cadaveric literature may use “fiber length” in ways that operationally approximate fascicle length.

Project rule:

For every source distinguish:
- true myofiber length;
- fascicle length;
- extrapolated fascicle length.

---

## 7. Architectural classes

Classical gross classes:

1. parallel/strap;
2. fusiform;
3. convergent/triangular;
4. unipennate;
5. bipennate;
6. multipennate;
7. circular/sphincter-like outside the usual limb-training context.

Project rule:

**architectural class = gross summary, not exact 3D blueprint.**

Real muscles can contain:
- mixed patterns;
- curved fascicles;
- compartmental differences;
- multiple aponeuroses.

---

## 8. Parallel / fusiform architecture

Typical structural tendencies:

- relatively long fascicles;
- relatively lower packing density for a given volume than strongly pennate designs;
- greater potential excursion/shortening distance.

But:

**“parallel/fusiform = fast” is not an anatomy-only conclusion.**

Actual shortening velocity depends on physiology, activation, load and tendon behavior.

---

## 9. Pennate architecture

Pennate fascicles attach obliquely to tendon/aponeurosis.

Primary architectural advantage:

**packing more fibers / contractile cross-section into a given muscle volume.**

2024 geometric modeling reinforces that pennation should not be understood merely as a cosine “force loss”; packing can provide a substantial mechanical advantage for force capacity at fixed muscle volume.

Limit:

That 2024 work is geometric/model-based and does not mean one pennation angle is universally optimal in vivo.

---

## 10. Pennation angle

Pennation angle = angle between fascicle direction and the relevant aponeurosis/tendon/line of action as operationally defined.

It is **state-dependent**, not one immutable personal number.

Changes with:
- muscle length;
- joint position;
- contraction intensity;
- region;
- architecture;
- chronic adaptation.

### Current 2026 direct human 3D evidence

Vosse et al. studied tibialis anterior in 12 participants during graded isometric dorsiflexion.

With increasing contraction:
- pennation angle increased;
- tracked fiber/fascicle length decreased;
- aponeurosis dimensions changed;
- deep vs superficial compartments differed.

Project conclusion:

**record contraction state and region whenever pennation is reported.**

---

## 11. Muscle volume

Whole-muscle volume describes total 3D muscle bulk.

It is not interchangeable with:
- ACSA;
- PCSA;
- one-site thickness;
- fascicle length.

For a simplified architecture:

`volume ≈ effective fiber/fascicle length × effective parallel cross-sectional content`

This motivates the classic force–excursion architectural trade-off, but real human 3D geometry is more complex.

---

## 12. Anatomical cross-sectional area — ACSA

ACSA = geometric cross-section of a muscle at a specified location/orientation.

Useful for:
- local or maximal muscle size;
- imaging comparison.

But:

**ACSA is not the summed fiber cross-section acting in parallel in a complex pennate muscle.**

Site and slice orientation matter.

---

## 13. Physiological cross-sectional area — PCSA

Conceptual target:

**total cross-sectional area of contractile fibers acting in parallel.**

Traditional human estimates commonly use:
- muscle volume or mass;
- muscle density;
- normalized/estimated fascicle length;
- sometimes pennation correction.

PCSA is useful as a structural proxy for maximal isometric force capacity.

It is not a directly measured joint force.

---

## 14. PCSA ≠ force

Actual muscle/joint force also depends on:

- activation;
- force–length state;
- contraction velocity;
- tendon/aponeurosis behavior;
- moment arm;
- force transmission;
- fiber type / contractile properties;
- model assumptions.

2024 systematic review of human specific tension found very wide historical estimates because nearly all in-vivo force estimates are indirect and require multiple difficult inputs.

Project rule:

**do not convert PCSA into personal muscle force using one universal specific-tension constant as if it were directly measured.**

---

## 15. PCSA formula limits

Conventional PCSA equations compress complex 3D architecture into simpler scalar estimates.

### Established caution

The 2024 pennation paper shows that interpretation of PCSA/pennation is more subtle than simple line-of-action cosine reasoning.

### Emerging 2026 PCSS work

Liu et al. proposed a curved **physiological cross-sectional surface (PCSS)** locally perpendicular to reconstructed 3D fiber directions.

Their preprint reported architecture-dependent disagreement between conventional PCSA/GCSA and this 3D surface metric.

Project status:

**PCSS = promising emerging geometric method, not current canonical replacement for PCSA.**

Reason:
- 2026 source is presently a bioRxiv preprint;
- validation/generalizability remain incomplete.

---

## 16. Fascicle length

Fascicle length describes the fascicular path between its attachment/aponeurotic boundaries.

Broad structural relevance:
- serial contractile arrangement;
- excursion potential;
- shortening-velocity potential;
- operating range.

But it is affected by:
- current sarcomere length;
- fascicle curvature;
- 3D path;
- muscle length;
- contraction state.

Project rule:

**fascicle length is not automatically equal to serial sarcomere number.**

---

## 17. Sarcomeres in series

For comparable sarcomere length, a longer fiber/fascicle can contain more sarcomeres in series.

Serial arrangement is structurally relevant to:
- excursion;
- shortening-distance capacity;
- shortening-velocity potential.

However:

`fascicle length = serial sarcomere number × average sarcomere length`

conceptually means fascicle length can change because:
1. sarcomere number changes;
2. sarcomere length changes;
3. both;
4. measured geometry changes.

---

## 18. Important 2025 update — human serial sarcomere evidence

Legacy ANA-11 stated that human training studies had not directly assessed serial sarcomere adaptation.

That statement is now **superseded**.

Andrews et al. studied 12 participants after 9 weeks of Nordic hamstring exercise.

They used:
- 3D ultrasound for BFlh fascicle length;
- second-harmonic-generation microendoscopy for **direct in-vivo sarcomere-length measurement**;
- serial sarcomere number **estimated/calculated** as:

`mean passive fascicle length / mean passive sarcomere length`

They reported:
- region-specific fascicle-length increases;
- calculated increases in serial sarcomere number;
- no statistically significant mean sarcomere-length increase after 9 weeks.

Critical precision:

**sarcomere length was directly imaged; serial sarcomere number was calculated from fascicle length / sarcomere length, not obtained by literally counting every sarcomere in a full human fascicle.**

Sample limitations:
- 12 participants;
- sarcomere imaging available in fewer participants/regions because of technical constraints;
- one muscle/exercise/intervention.

Project conclusion:

Human exercise evidence for serial sarcomerogenesis now exists, but it is still methodologically limited and should not be generalized to all muscles/training modes.

---

## 19. Long-muscle-length review boundary

Wolf et al. systematic review searched through February 2024 and found that included long-vs-short-muscle-length resistance-training studies largely relied on fascicle-length proxies and did not directly estimate serial sarcomere adaptation.

Andrews et al. was published later / outside that review's search window.

Therefore the two sources are not contradictory.

Current project synthesis:

1. **most human hypertrophy studies still use fascicle length as a proxy**;
2. **one modern intervention line now combines fascicle imaging + direct sarcomere-length microendoscopy to estimate serial sarcomere number**;
3. direct sarcomere evidence remains sparse.

---

## 20. Contractile material in parallel

More fibers/sarcomeric elements arranged in parallel increase effective contractile cross-section.

This is the structural basis of force-capacity relations with PCSA.

But muscle volume can be allocated differently:

- longer fascicles / more serial organization;
- greater parallel packing / larger PCSA.

Project rule:

**same volume does not imply same architecture or same mechanical capacity.**

---

## 21. Force–excursion architectural trade-off

Simplified model at equal volume:

- relatively shorter/more pennate fibers → more parallel packing / greater PCSA potential;
- relatively longer fibers → greater serial arrangement / excursion potential.

This is a useful architecture model.

It is **not** a deterministic law for every human muscle because:
- 3D fiber paths differ;
- tendons/aponeuroses deform;
- regional architecture differs;
- neural/physiological factors also matter.

---

## 22. Muscle thickness

Ultrasound muscle thickness = distance between selected tissue boundaries at a specific site.

It is:
- local;
- easy to acquire;
- not whole-muscle volume;
- not PCSA;
- not ACSA;
- not fascicle length.

Project rule:

**one-site thickness is not a complete muscle-size measure.**

Regional hypertrophy makes this especially important.

---

## 23. Regional architecture

A single named muscle can show proximal/distal or superficial/deep differences in:

- thickness;
- fascicle length;
- pennation;
- curvature;
- aponeurosis geometry;
- sarcomere properties.

### 2025 human evidence

Trinchi et al. studied vastus lateralis in 12 healthy young adults.

They found:
- different resting architecture in mid vs distal regions;
- different fascicle/muscle-belly behavior during isokinetic contractions;
- region-dependent architectural gearing.

Project conclusion:

**one ultrasound site cannot automatically represent the whole muscle.**

---

## 24. Compartmental architecture

A named muscle may contain distinct architectural/neuromuscular compartments.

Examples already encountered in current project:
- tibialis anterior;
- anconeus;
- popliteus;
- QL layers;
- multifidus regional bundles.

Project rule:

**named muscle ≠ homogeneous actuator.**

But:
architectural compartment ≠ automatically independently trainable compartment.

That inference requires NEU/MEC/TEC evidence.

---

## 25. Architectural gearing

During contraction:
- fascicles shorten;
- pennation changes;
- muscle belly/aponeuroses deform.

Whole-muscle shortening can therefore differ from fascicle shortening.

This is architectural gearing.

Anatomy establishes:
- deformable geometry;
- 3D arrangement.

Dynamic gearing mechanics → MEC/PHY.

---

## 26. Fiber / fascicle curvature

Real fascicles are not guaranteed straight.

Current 3D imaging shows:
- curvature;
- multiplanar trajectories;
- contraction-dependent changes.

Project rule:

**a straight 2D line is a measurement model, not literal universal fascicle geometry.**

---

## 27. 3D fascicle-measurement validation — 2026

Mantecón-Tagarro et al. compared four 3D-ultrasound approaches with direct dissection in **6 human cadaveric BFlh legs**.

Findings:
- all approaches showed reasonably close agreement with dissection;
- two-plane approaches had lower bias/narrower disagreement than single-plane approaches;
- adding curvature gave only limited additional improvement;
- precision was moderate.

Project conclusion:

**measurement approach matters even in 3D ultrasound, especially for architecturally complex BFlh.**

Limit:
- only six cadaveric legs;
- mid-belly BFlh;
- not an in-vivo intervention validation.

---

## 28. Traditional fascicle measurement caution

Clark et al. 2026 compared in-situ vs chemical-dissection fascicle measurement within animal specimens.

They found systematic method differences that depended partly on architecture.

Project use:

This is **supportive methodological evidence**, not a human quantitative correction factor.

It reinforces:

**“fascicle length” is method-dependent.**

Do not apply the reported animal ratio directly to human ultrasound values.

---

## 29. Ultrasound acquisition reproducibility — 2026

Nacafucasaco et al. studied vastus-lateralis ultrasound architecture in 54 participants.

Compared with re-analyzing one acquired image, repeating image acquisition increased variability for:

- CSA;
- thickness;
- fascicle length;
- pennation angle.

Project rule:

When judging a longitudinal architecture change, record whether:

1. the site was standardized;
2. acquisition was repeated;
3. joint position was fixed;
4. probe orientation was standardized;
5. contraction/rest state was controlled;
6. the observed change exceeded measurement noise/MDC where available.

---

## 30. Fiber type is a separate structural dimension

Type I / IIa / IIx classification describes cellular/contractile phenotype.

It is distinct from:
- fascicle length;
- pennation;
- PCSA;
- tendon length;
- architectural class.

Project boundary:

Basic structural identity belongs here.

Metabolism, recruitment, fatigue, adaptation → PHY/NEU.

## 31. Tendon vs aponeurosis

### Tendon
More cord-like free connective-tissue segment transmitting muscle force to attachment.

### Aponeurosis
Broad sheet-like tendinous structure receiving distributed fascicle insertions.

2026 review by Ross & Herzog emphasizes that aponeuroses:
- link fascicles to tendon;
- shape architecture;
- deform during force production;
- have mechanics that remain incompletely understood in vivo.

Project rule:

**aponeurosis is not a rigid fixed plate.**

Detailed compliance/strain → MEC/PHY.

---

## 32. Tendon length / muscle-belly length

Two people with similar skeletal origin–insertion distance can differ in:

- muscle-belly length;
- free-tendon length;
- aponeurosis length;
- fascicle length;
- pennation.

Consequences:
- visible muscle shape;
- architecture;
- excursion/leverage relationships.

Personal morphology implications → ANA-15.

## 33. Muscle belly shape vs attachment location

Training can change:
- muscle size;
- regional size;
- fascicle architecture.

Training does **not** relocate ordinary anatomical origin/insertion.

Project rule:

**regional hypertrophy can change contour within anatomical attachment constraints; it does not create a new tendon insertion.**

---

## 34. PCSA / ACSA / volume / thickness — final distinction

### Muscle volume
Total 3D amount of muscle tissue.

### ACSA
Geometric cross-section at a chosen location/orientation.

### PCSA
Estimate/concept of total contractile fiber cross-section acting in parallel.

### Thickness
Local linear distance.

### Fascicle length
Serial/path dimension.

### Pennation
Orientation/packing geometry.

Project rule:

**never use these terms as synonyms.**

---

## 35. “Strength score” boundary

No single architecture variable equals whole-joint strength.

Joint torque depends on:

- muscle force;
- neural activation;
- moment arms;
- force–length state;
- contraction velocity;
- tendon/aponeurosis behavior;
- contributions from synergists;
- task constraints.

Architecture constrains capacity but does not uniquely determine performance.

---

## 36. Architecture plasticity boundary

Resistance/eccentric training can alter:
- size;
- fascicle length;
- pennation;
- possibly sarcomere arrangement.

But:

**which adaptation occurs, in what muscle/region, under what training prescription is a physiology/programming question.**

ANA-11 establishes:
- what the variables are;
- how they relate structurally;
- how they are measured/limited.

PHY/PRO determines adaptation evidence.

---

## 37. Regional hypertrophy boundary

Regional architecture means local imaging can detect true region-specific changes.

But “regional hypertrophy” does not automatically mean:
- fully independent compartment training;
- arbitrary muscle reshaping;
- changing insertion sites.

Project rule:

**regional size change is plausible/real; mechanistic and exercise-selective claims require longitudinal evidence.**

---

## 38. Measurement-state minimum

Whenever using architecture measures, record:

1. exact muscle;
2. exact region/site;
3. joint angle;
4. rest vs active contraction;
5. contraction intensity;
6. imaging/dissection method;
7. 2D vs 3D;
8. fascicle extrapolation/trajectory assumptions;
9. probe/acquisition method;
10. repeat acquisition/reliability;
11. whether whole-muscle or local measure.

## 39. What changed versus legacy ANA-11

### Confirmed

Legacy core remains valid:

- structural hierarchy;
- fiber vs fascicle distinction;
- classical architecture categories;
- fascicle length / pennation / PCSA / ACSA distinction;
- tendon vs aponeurosis distinction;
- regional/compartmental architecture;
- dynamic architecture during contraction;
- architecture measurement dependence;
- architecture alone does not prescribe exercise.

### Strengthened / updated

1. **Serial sarcomeres:** legacy “no direct human training evidence” wording is superseded. Andrews et al. directly measured in-vivo sarcomere length and calculated serial sarcomere number from fascicle length/sarcomere length after NHE; evidence is still one small intervention line.
2. **PCSS:** current 2026 PCSS work is a **bioRxiv preprint**, not a peer-reviewed new standard. It is retained as emerging 3D geometry only.
3. **Regional architecture:** 2025 human VL work directly demonstrates region-specific architecture and contraction behavior.
4. **3D ultrasound validation:** 2026 cadaveric BFlh study shows multiplanar trajectory handling can improve agreement with dissection.
5. **Measurement reproducibility:** 2026 VL ultrasound study quantifies extra variability from repeat image acquisition.
6. **Aponeurosis:** 2026 review reinforces dynamic/deformable aponeurotic role and ongoing uncertainty in in-vivo mechanics.

### Tightened

1. Fascicle length is usually a proxy for serial architecture, but direct sarcomere-length-assisted estimation now exists in humans.
2. Serial sarcomere number in Andrews et al. was **calculated**, not literally counted along entire fascicles.
3. Animal fascicle-measurement method differences are not transferred numerically to humans.
4. PCSS remains preprint/emerging.
5. One-site ultrasound does not represent whole muscle by default.
6. Compartment architecture does not prove independent exercise targeting.

## 40. Established findings

### HIGH

1. Structural hierarchy muscle → fascicle → fiber → myofibril → sarcomere is established.
2. Fascicle and myofiber are distinct anatomical entities.
3. ACSA, PCSA, muscle volume, thickness, fascicle length and pennation describe different structural properties.
4. Pennation is a packing/orientation feature and changes dynamically with contraction.
5. Fascicle length relates to serial architecture but is not identical to serial sarcomere number.
6. PCSA is an established structural proxy for parallel contractile capacity, not direct joint force.
7. Tendon and aponeurosis have different geometry and both participate in MTU force transmission.
8. Human muscles show regional and compartmental architectural heterogeneity.
9. Architecture measurement is site-, state- and method-dependent.
10. Multiarticular muscle architecture must be interpreted across all crossed joint positions.

### MODERATE/HIGH

1. Human BFlh NHE data support serial sarcomere addition after 9 weeks when fascicle imaging is paired with direct sarcomere-length measurement, but serial number is calculated and the sample is small.
2. Regional VL architecture can alter local fascicle behavior during contraction.
3. Two-plane 3DUS can improve fascicle-length agreement versus one-plane assumptions in complex BFlh architecture.

### EMERGING / NOT YET STANDARD

1. PCSS as a 3D replacement/refinement of conventional PCSA — current source is a 2026 preprint.
2. Exact independent functional specialization of architectural compartments.
3. Universal quantitative translation from architecture to personal muscle force.
