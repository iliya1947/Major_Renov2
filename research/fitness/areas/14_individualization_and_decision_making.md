# 14. Индивидуализация и принятие решений

Статус: **MAP_READY после отдельного аудита полноты 2026-11-16**.

Цель файла — формализовать переход от групповых научных данных к конкретному человеку. Индивидуализация здесь означает не «у каждого уникальная программа», а последовательное учёты целей, ограничений, предпочтений, фактической реакции и неопределённости.

2. Secondary goal.

4. Non-negotiables.
5. Nice-to-have outcomes.
6. Competing goals.
7. Time horizon.
8. Minimum acceptable result.
9. Criteria for changing priorities.

## 4. Constraint hierarchy

1. Medical/safety constraints first.
2. Anatomical/ROM constraints.
3. Equipment constraints.
4. Time constraints.
5. Recovery constraints.
6. Behavioral/adherence constraints.
7. Preference constraints.
8. Financial constraints.
9. Environment.
10. Some constraints are fixed; others are modifiable.

## 5. Population-level evidence vs personal response

1. Start from best average evidence.
2. Apply only when population/context reasonably match.
3. Do not assume group mean predicts exact individual result.
4. Do not assume visible variation proves true treatment-response heterogeneity.
5. Measurement error and day-to-day variability can mimic “responders/nonresponders”.
6. Personal monitoring becomes increasingly important once a reasonable evidence-based starting point is chosen.
7. Personal data should update, not erase, external evidence.

## 6. Individual response uncertainty

1. True response.
2. Measurement error.
3. Biological variability.
4. Learning effects.
5. Adherence variation.
6. Nutrition/sleep variation.
7. Regression to the mean.
8. Different baseline state.
9. Different implementation quality.
10. Need repeated observations before labeling response.

## 7. Dose titration

1. Start from evidence-informed reasonable dose.
2. Observe performance/recovery/adherence.
3. Increase only if needed and tolerated.
4. Reduce if cost exceeds benefit.
5. Avoid starting at theoretical maximum tolerable dose.
6. Use minimum effective complexity.
7. Reassess after predefined window.
8. Distinguish no response from too-short observation.

## 8. Exercise fit

For each exercise assess:

1. Goal relevance.
2. Technique competence.
3. Anatomical comfort.
4. Pain.
5. Stability.
6. Available load.
7. Progression runway.
8. Measurability.
9. Fatigue cost.
10. Enjoyment/aversion.
11. Setup friction.
12. Substitute if another exercise provides same goal with better overall fit.

## 9. Anthropometry

1. Segment lengths.
2. Joint structure.
3. Range-of-motion differences.
4. Grip width/stance.
5. Exercise geometry.
6. Moment arms.
7. Comfortable trajectory.
8. Avoid forcing textbook geometry onto every body.
9. Do not use anthropometry alone to predict “best exercise” without performance feedback.

## 10. Training status

1. Absolute beginner.
2. Returning/detrained.
3. Novice with prior exposure.
4. Intermediate.
5. Advanced.
6. Skill-specific experience.
7. A person can be trained in one movement/domain and novice in another.
8. Progression rate and complexity should match actual status.

## 11. Health status

1. Healthy general adult.
2. Controlled chronic condition.
3. Active symptoms.
4. Recent injury/illness.
5. Medical clearance when indicated.
6. Program modification vs referral.
7. Do not use “personalization” to work around a contraindication.

## 12. Preference and enjoyment

1. Preferred exercise.
2. Preferred session length.
3. Preferred environment.
4. Alone/social.
5. Routine vs variation.
6. Time of day.
7. Preference is relevant when efficacy differences are small.
8. Preference does not automatically override safety or core specificity.
9. Use preferences to increase adherence without destroying progression.

## 13. Time and complexity budget

1. Minutes per session.
2. Sessions per week.
3. Setup time.
4. Tracking time.
5. Decision burden.
6. Learning burden.
7. Recovery burden.
8. Nutrition burden.
9. Every added intervention must justify its complexity cost.
10. Remove low-value components first.

## 14. Equipment constraints

1. Available loads.
2. Fixed vs adjustable resistance.
3. Bodyweight options.
4. Furniture/support.
5. Space.
6. Safe failure options.
7. Progression ceiling.
8. Trigger for acquiring new equipment.
9. Equipment should serve the program; program should not be invented just to use available objects.

## 15. Autoregulation vs personalization

1. Personalization = design for stable individual characteristics/context.
2. Autoregulation = response to current state.
3. Do not conflate them.
4. Stable program can contain daily autoregulation rules.
5. Daily variation should not erase long-term progression.

## 16. Decision thresholds

Before changing a variable define:

1. What signal triggers change?
2. How large must it be?
3. How many observations?
4. Which variable changes first?
5. Expected result.
6. Evaluation window.
7. Reversal criterion.
8. Safety override.

## 17. When to change load

Possible triggers:
1. Rep target exceeded with stable technique/RIR.
2. Load clearly below target effort.
3. Strength goal requires greater resistance.
4. Current load causes unacceptable technique breakdown.
5. Pain/discomfort requires reduction.
6. Equipment ceiling reached.

## 18. When to change volume

1. Progress absent with adequate adherence/recovery and likely insufficient stimulus.
2. Excess fatigue/recovery cost.
3. Session time burden.
4. Specialization priority.
5. Maintenance phase.
6. Return from layoff.
7. Avoid volume changes from one anomalous session.

## 19. When to change exercise

1. Pain/poor tolerance.
2. Cannot load/progress meaningfully.
3. Technique complexity exceeds benefit.
4. Equipment mismatch.
5. Goal changed.
6. Persistent plateau despite other variables.
7. Excessive fatigue cost.
8. Severe aversion undermines adherence.
9. Planned variation after stable measurement period.
10. Do not rotate merely because an exercise feels “old”.

## 20. When not to change anything

1. Performance trend improving.
2. Technique improving.
3. Adherence good.
4. Recovery acceptable.
5. No meaningful pain.
6. Measurement window too short.
7. One bad day.
8. One unusually good day.
9. Novelty craving alone without strategic reason.
10. External trend/social-media advice without relevance.

## 21. Diagnosing lack of progress

Sequence:

1. Verify measurement.
2. Verify adherence.
3. Verify technique/standardization.
4. Verify enough time.
5. Check load/effort.
6. Check weekly volume.
7. Check frequency.
8. Check recovery.
9. Check nutrition/energy/protein.
10. Check equipment ceiling.
11. Check illness/medical issue.
12. Change smallest plausible limiting variable.

## 22. Personal evidence loop

1. Plan.
2. Execute.
3. Measure.
4. Compare to expected trajectory.
5. Diagnose.
6. Modify one main lever.
7. Re-measure.
8. Keep/revert.
9. Record rationale in `DECISIONS.md`.
10. Preserve history for later learning.

## 23. N-of-1 logic

1. Use external evidence for initial option set.
2. Compare personally relevant options when equipoise exists.
3. Standardize measurements.
4. Repeat exposures where possible.
5. Separate signal from within-person noise.
6. Avoid declaring “I respond better” from one block.
7. Individual experiments can answer practical questions even if not generalizable.

## 24. Prediction vs adaptation

1. Predicting the perfect plan in advance is often impossible.
2. Use baseline factors when they have credible predictive value.
3. Avoid pseudoprecision from genetics/body type/personality labels.
4. Prefer adaptive feedback loops when predictors are weak.
5. Test actual response rather than inventing response from phenotype.

## 25. Personalization hierarchy

When several valid options exist:

1. Safety.
2. Goal specificity.
3. Evidence of efficacy.
4. Actual capability.
5. Progression potential.
6. Recovery cost.
7. Adherence.
8. Preference.
9. Time/cost.
10. Simplicity.

Order can shift by context, but safety/goal relevance remain constraints.

## 26. Minimum viable effective program

1. Smallest structure likely to create target adaptation.
2. Low startup friction.
3. Enough measurable progression.
4. Enough exercise coverage.
5. Acceptable recovery.
6. Easy to expand later.
7. Distinguish minimum viable from minimum theoretically effective dose.
8. Program should earn added complexity.

## 27. Escalation ladder

If progress stalls:
1. Verify data.
2. Improve execution.
3. Adjust reps/load.
4. Adjust volume.
5. Adjust frequency.
6. Adjust exercise.
7. Add equipment.
8. Reassess nutrition/recovery.
9. Seek clinical input if unexplained symptoms.
10. Avoid jumping directly to exotic methods.

## 28. De-escalation ladder

If burden is too high:
1. Remove redundant exercises.
2. Reduce low-value volume.
3. Simplify tracking.
4. Reduce frequency if needed.
5. Use easier setup.
6. Preserve highest-value movements.
7. Maintain continuity rather than abandon entire system.

## 29. Personalization and ADHD

1. ADHD is a constraint profile, not the training goal.
2. Physiological stimulus still has to be sufficient.
3. Reduce friction/decisions where they are real barriers.
4. Preserve feedback/novelty only if useful.
5. Do not assume generic ADHD tricks.
6. Test behavior architecture independently where possible.
7. A program completed consistently can dominate a marginally “optimal” one that is abandoned.

## 30. Personalization and limited equipment

1. Determine whether current resistance is actually limiting.
2. Use mechanics/ROM/unilateral work when valid.
3. Distinguish local hypertrophy stimulus from maximal-strength specificity.
4. Document ceiling.
5. Define objective purchase trigger.
6. Do not lower target indefinitely just to avoid acquiring adequate resistance.

ACSM's 2026 resistance-training position stand explicitly prioritizes individualization by goals, enjoyment and safety, while emphasizing consistency and avoiding needless complexity. Evidence-informed personalization literature similarly argues that complex exercise prescriptions cannot be fully predicted from group studies; testing, monitoring and re-planning are necessary. At the same time, current response-variability research warns that apparent “individual response” can be measurement noise or within-person variability, so personalization must not be based on a single observed change.
