# BIO-15 — Aerobic capacity and economy

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-20**  
Область: 05 — Bioenergetics / Exercise Metabolism

## 0. Core decision

Aerobic capacity, threshold-related sustainable intensity and exercise economy are related but distinct constructs.

Project rules:

- **VO2max ≠ complete endurance performance**;
- **VO2peak ≠ automatically VO2max**;
- **threshold family ≠ one universal threshold**;
- **economy ≠ VO2max ≠ efficiency**;
- **mitochondrial/capillary adaptation ≠ whole-body VO2max by themselves**;
- **oxidative recovery relevance ≠ VO2max predicts resistance-set recovery**.

---

## 1. VO2max

VO2max is the maximal attainable whole-body oxygen uptake under defined test conditions.

It integrates:

- pulmonary gas exchange;
- cardiac output;
- arterial O2 content;
- muscle perfusion/diffusion;
- peripheral extraction;
- mitochondrial utilization.

Project rule:

**VO2max is integrated oxygen-cascade capacity, not one-organ or one-organelle function.**

---

## 2. VO2peak

VO2peak is the highest VO2 observed in a specific test.

Project rule:

**VO2peak ≠ automatically confirmed VO2max.**

Detailed maximality/verification criteria remain TST.

---

## 3. VO2max and endurance performance

VO2max is an important upper-capacity determinant.

Performance also depends on:

- task economy;
- threshold-related sustainable intensity;
- biomechanics/skill;
- durability under fatigue;
- substrate state;
- environment.

Project rule:

**VO2max is important but insufficient as a complete endurance-performance score.**

---

## 4. Fractional utilization

Fractional utilization describes the fraction of VO2max sustained at a physiological landmark or competition intensity.

Current evidence does not support treating it as a universally strong independent predictor.

Project rule:

**fractional utilization is a useful descriptive construct, not a universal standalone performance determinant or training target.**

---

## 5. Threshold family

Relevant constructs include:

- LT1/LT2;
- GET/VT1;
- RCP/VT2-related landmarks;
- CP/CS;
- MLSS.

Project rule:

**always name the threshold construct/method rather than saying “the threshold.”**

---

## 6. LT vs VT

Lactate and ventilatory/gas-exchange thresholds assess related physiological transitions using different signals.

Project rule:

**LT and VT may align in some protocols but are not automatically interchangeable individual values.**

---

## 7. CP vs MLSS

Critical power and MLSS both relate to the heavy–severe boundary.

Current pooled evidence shows:

- CP power tends to be higher on average;
- individual limits of agreement are wide.

Project rule:

**CP ≠ MLSS exactly and no fixed individual correction factor is retained.**

---

## 8. LT vs critical speed

Laboratory lactate threshold and field critical speed can differ systematically and individually.

Project rule:

**LT and CS are not numerically interchangeable unless agreement is established for the method/population.**

---

## 9. Threshold speed/power

Speed/power at a threshold reflects multiple factors:

- VO2max;
- economy/efficiency;
- threshold construct/method;
- task mechanics.

Project rule:

**threshold speed/power is a composite performance-related output, not pure “threshold physiology.”**

---

## 10. Exercise economy

Exercise economy is the oxygen/energy cost of a specified submaximal external task.

It must be tied to:

- mode;
- speed/power;
- grade/surface;
- environment/equipment;
- fatigue state.

Project rule:

**economy is a task-context cost, not a context-free athlete trait.**

---

## 11. Economy vs efficiency

Economy:

- cost to perform a task.

Mechanical efficiency:

- external mechanical work divided by metabolic energy input.

Project rule:

**economy ≠ mechanical efficiency.**

---

## 12. Oxygen cost vs energy cost

VO2-based economy and energy-cost measures are closely related.

They are not always numerically identical because:

- caloric equivalent varies with substrate use;
- RER and steady-state assumptions matter;
- measurement/normalization differ.

Project rule:

**oxygen cost ≠ energy cost exactly.**

---

## 13. Speed/state dependence

Economy can change with:

- speed/workload;
- incline;
- fatigue;
- temperature;
- surface;
- equipment.

Project rule:

**one economy value does not describe economy at every speed or physiological state.**

---

## 14. Treadmill vs overground

A fixed 1% treadmill incline does not universally reproduce overground energetic cost.

Project rule:

**1% treadmill ≠ universal overground correction.**

Running-specific treadmill/overground results must not be generalized to every modality.

---

## 15. Economy determinants

Economy is influenced by integrated:

- biomechanics;
- muscle-tendon behavior;
- neural coordination;
- technique;
- body dimensions;
- footwear/equipment;
- training state.

Project rule:

**economy is an integrated metabolic-mechanical outcome, not a purely mitochondrial trait.**

---

## 16. Strength training and economy

Strength training can improve running economy in some endurance-trained populations.

However:

- effects are heterogeneous;
- some current RCTs report no significant improvement;
- VO2max can remain unchanged.

Project rule:

**strength training can improve economy without VO2max change, but the effect is not universal.**

---

## 17. Economy durability

Economy after prolonged/fatiguing exercise can differ from fresh-state economy.

Project rule:

**fresh economy ≠ economy durability.**

Detailed durability testing/programming remains TST/PRO.

---

## 18. Equipment effects

Equipment can alter measured economy without changing the athlete's intrinsic VO2max or mitochondrial capacity.

Project rule:

**economy is partly athlete × task × equipment × environment.**

No equipment recommendation is made in BIO-15.

---

## 19. Mitochondrial adaptations

ET, HIT and SIT can all increase skeletal-muscle mitochondrial content.

Project rule:

**no one endurance-training intensity format owns mitochondrial adaptation.**

Pooled group percentages are not individual predictions.

---

## 20. Mitochondrial content vs function

Keep separate:

- mitochondrial content/density;
- enzyme activity;
- OXPHOS proteins;
- mass-specific respiration;
- intrinsic function;
- ATP-production capacity.

Project rule:

**one mitochondrial marker ≠ complete mitochondrial function.**

---

## 21. Mitochondria vs VO2max/performance

Mitochondrial characteristics are important and often necessary for endurance performance.

They are not sufficient by themselves because systemic O2 delivery and other factors can constrain performance.

Project rule:

**mitochondrial adaptation ≠ VO2max and ≠ performance guarantee.**

---

## 22. Concurrent training boundary

Current pooled evidence indicates concurrent RT+ET can support VO2max and mitochondrial adaptation similarly to ET alone in many contexts.

But:

- reduced endurance dose can attenuate VO2max gains;
- subgroup context matters;
- linked errata/corrections must be respected.

Project rule:

**no pooled difference ≠ universal equivalence under every concurrent design.**

Exact programming remains PRO/BIO-16.

---

## 23. Capillary adaptations

Training can increase skeletal-muscle capillarization.

Capillarization supports:

- O2 diffusion/extraction;
- perfusion distribution;
- substrate/metabolite exchange.

Project rule:

**capillary adaptation is an important peripheral component, not a direct VO2max meter.**

Detailed angiogenesis remains PHY.

---

## 24. Capillary metrics

Different measures include:

- capillary density;
- capillary-to-fiber ratio;
- capillary contacts;
- exchange-index measures.

Project rule:

**capillary density ≠ total microvascular adaptation.**

Fiber hypertrophy can change density without equivalent loss of capillary supply.

---

## 25. Central adaptations

Central adaptations can include:

- plasma/blood volume;
- stroke volume;
- maximal cardiac output;
- pulmonary/arterial O2 support.

Project rule:

**central adaptation is broader than one cardiac metric.**

---

## 26. Peripheral adaptations

Peripheral adaptations can include:

- perfusion/distribution;
- capillarization;
- diffusion/extraction;
- mitochondrial content/function;
- oxidative enzymes.

Project rule:

**peripheral adaptation is broader than mitochondria alone.**

---

## 27. Central and peripheral coexistence

Training can produce:

- cardiac;
- vascular;
- capillary;
- mitochondrial

adaptations together.

Project rule:

**aerobic adaptation is integrated; central and peripheral changes are not mutually exclusive.**

---

## 28. Adaptation vs limitation

Training adaptation asks what changed.

Rate limitation asks what currently constrains performance.

Project rule:

**adaptation ≠ rate limitation.**

A system can adapt without being the sole current bottleneck.

---

## 29. Population/state dependence

Dominant VO2max limitation can differ by:

- training status;
- mode;
- environment;
- biological sex;
- pulmonary/hematological state.

Project rule:

**elite-endurance O2-delivery limitation ≠ universal limitation for every population.**

---

## 30. Sex-evidence boundary

Sex can affect oxygen-cascade physiology and adaptation.

However evidence strength differs across proposed mechanisms.

Project rule:

**do not apply one universal sex-specific central/peripheral adaptation model.**

---

## 31. Resistance-training recovery relevance

Oxidative metabolism contributes to resistance-set recovery through:

- PCr resynthesis;
- ATP-dependent homeostasis;
- ongoing metabolic restoration.

Project rule:

**between-set resistance recovery has an oxidative component.**

---

## 32. VO2max vs set-to-set recovery

Direct evidence in resistance-trained men and women shows whole-body VO2max is only weakly associated with within-set fatigue and between-set recovery in tested squat/knee-extension protocols.

Project rule:

**VO2max is not a strong universal proxy for resistance-set recovery speed.**

---

## 33. Mechanism vs proxy

These two statements are compatible:

- local oxidative metabolism is mechanistically important for PCr recovery;
- whole-body VO2max weakly predicts a specific set-to-set recovery phenotype.

Why:

- local perfusion matters;
- local oxidative capacity matters;
- muscle/task specificity matters;
- neural/mechanical factors also matter.

Project rule:

**oxidative recovery mechanism ≠ predictive validity of VO2max.**

---

## 34. No rest-interval shortcut

Higher VO2max does not justify:

- automatically shorter RT rest;
- one universal rest interval;
- assumption of full PCr/neuromuscular recovery.

Project rule:

**aerobic capacity is recovery context, not a direct resistance-rest timer.**

---

## 36. Trainer-level implications

1. Use VO2max as one aerobic-capacity variable, not an omnibus endurance score.
2. Keep VO2peak distinct unless maximality is established.
3. Name the threshold method used.
4. Do not substitute LT, VT, CP/CS and MLSS numerically without validation.
5. Define economy by task, speed/workload, mode and environment.
6. Separate economy from efficiency.
7. Recognize that economy can improve without VO2max.
8. Treat mitochondrial/capillary metrics as peripheral components rather than whole-body scores.
9. Interpret VO2max adaptation as integrated central + peripheral physiology.
10. Do not infer an individual's limiting step from VO2max alone.
11. Recognize oxidative support of resistance-set recovery.
12. Do not use VO2max alone to set RT rest.
13. Exact testing remains TST.
14. Exact programming remains PRO.

## 37. Confidence

### HIGH

- VO2max is integrated whole-body oxygen-cascade capacity;
- VO2peak is operationally distinct;
- VO2max is not a complete endurance-performance score;
- threshold constructs are method defined;
- CP/MLSS are not individually interchangeable;
- economy is distinct from VO2max and efficiency;
- economy is context dependent;
- mitochondrial content/function are non-equivalent;
- training can increase mitochondrial content and capillarization;
- central/peripheral adaptations can coexist;
- adaptation and limitation are different questions;
- oxidative metabolism supports RT recovery;
- VO2max is not a universal RT-recovery proxy.

### MODERATE-HIGH

- fractional utilization is weaker as an independent predictor than classical models often imply;
- strength training can improve economy in selected endurance populations;
- short-term higher-intensity VO2max gains can be predominantly central in selected protocols;
- concurrent RT+ET can preserve aerobic/mitochondrial adaptation in many settings;
- whole-body VO2max is weakly associated with set recovery in current direct RT evidence.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT DEPENDENT

- exact agreement among LT/VT/CP/MLSS;
- exact economy response to one intervention;
- exact central/peripheral contribution in one individual;
- exact mitochondrial/capillary contribution to performance;
- exact aerobic contribution to a specific RT recovery phenotype.
