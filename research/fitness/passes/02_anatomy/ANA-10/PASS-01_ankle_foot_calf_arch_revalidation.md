## 2. Leg vs foot

Anatomically:

- **leg** = knee → ankle;
- **foot** = structures distal to talocrural/tibiotalar joint.

This distinction is retained because training language often uses “leg” for the whole lower limb.

---

## 3. Tibia / fibula / talus

### Tibia

Major distal weight-bearing bone.

Relevant distal structures:
- tibial plafond;
- medial malleolus;
- fibular incisura;
- posterior malleolar region.

### Fibula

Contributes:
- lateral malleolus;
- ankle mortise;
- syndesmotic stability;
- muscular/ligament attachment.

It is not a major direct axial weight-bearing bone compared with tibia.

### Talus

Key load-transfer bone between leg and foot.

Important:
- body;
- neck;
- head;
- trochlear dome;
- malleolar facets;
- calcaneal/navicular relationships.

Project rule:

**talus is structurally central but has no ordinary direct muscle attachment; its motion is governed largely by joint geometry, ligaments and transmitted forces.**

---

## 4. Ankle mortise

Mortise formed by:
- distal tibial plafond;
- medial malleolus;
- lateral malleolus.

It surrounds the talar trochlea.

Current anatomy retains the important feature:
- talar trochlea is broader anteriorly than posteriorly.

Project consequence:

**mortise congruence/tension changes with ankle position.**

Exact kinematic/mechanical consequence → MEC.

---

## 5. Distal tibiofibular syndesmosis

Current 2026 reference identifies four key structures:

1. AITFL;
2. PITFL;
3. inferior transverse ligament;
4. interosseous ligament, continuous with distal interosseous membrane.

Roles:
- maintain tibia–fibula relation;
- resist excessive fibular displacement/separation;
- preserve mortise congruence.

Project rule:

**syndesmosis is its own distal tibiofibular joint complex, not simply another ankle collateral ligament.**

---

## 6. Talocrural joint

Talocrural/tibiotalar joint:
- tibia/fibula mortise ↔ talus;
- synovial;
- hinge-dominant.

Primary motions:
- dorsiflexion;
- plantarflexion.

Project rule:

**talocrural joint is not the principal site of all inversion/eversion.**

Those motions are distributed more strongly through subtalar and midtarsal systems.

---

## 7. Lateral ankle ligament complex

Canonical:
1. ATFL;
2. CFL;
3. PTFL.

Broad roles:
- resist excessive inversion/translation/rotation depending joint position.

Project rule:

**lateral ankle stability = position-dependent ligament complex, not one ATFL strap.**

Detailed strain/injury → MEC/MED.

---

## 8. Deltoid ligament complex

Medial ligament complex includes superficial and deep components.

Current reference descriptions include:
- tibionavicular;
- tibiocalcaneal;
- tibiospring;
- superficial posterior tibiotalar;
- deep anterior/posterior tibiotalar components.

Project rule:

**deltoid ligament = complex, not one homogeneous band.**

---

## 9. Hindfoot / subtalar system

Hindfoot:
- talus;
- calcaneus.

Subtalar/talocalcaneal system contributes materially to:
- inversion/eversion;
- 3D foot pronation/supination;
- adaptation between leg and foot.

Project rule:

**subtalar motion is 3D and should not be reduced to a flat one-axis hinge.**

Current 2026 experimental work on talocrural/subtalar/midtarsal kinematics also reinforces that isolated extrinsic-muscle action can produce coupled movement across multiple foot/ankle joints rather than one muscle → one joint-axis motion.

Exact axes/coupling → MEC.

---

## 10. Transverse-tarsal / midfoot system

Talonavicular + calcaneocuboid joints form the transverse-tarsal/Chopart complex.

These interact with subtalar motion in:
- inversion/eversion;
- forefoot/hindfoot coupling;
- foot adaptation/stiffening.

Midfoot includes:
- navicular;
- cuboid;
- three cuneiforms.

Project rule:

**“ankle motion” in training language often spans talocrural + subtalar + midtarsal structures.**

---

## 11. Forefoot / hallux

Forefoot:
- five metatarsals;
- 14 phalanges.

Hallux:
- two phalanges;
- first MTP;
- IP.

Digits 2–5:
- MCP/MTP-level and IP articulations.

First MTP/hallux dorsiflexion is particularly relevant to windlass and push-off.

## 12. Four leg compartments

Current anatomy recognizes:

1. anterior;
2. lateral;
3. superficial posterior;
4. deep posterior.

Boundaries include:
- tibia/fibula;
- interosseous membrane;
- intermuscular septa;
- crural fascia.

Project rule:

**compartments organize anatomy; they do not imply one motion per compartment.**

## 13. Anterior compartment

Core muscles:
1. tibialis anterior;
2. EHL;
3. EDL;
4. fibularis tertius.

Innervation:
- deep fibular nerve.

Broad functions:
- dorsiflexion;
- toe extension;
- inversion/eversion contributions depending muscle.

### Tibialis anterior

- dorsiflexion;
- inversion;
- first-ray/medial-foot connection via medial cuneiform + first metatarsal insertion.

### EHL

- hallux extension;
- dorsiflexion contribution.

### EDL

- digits 2–5 extension;
- dorsiflexion;
- eversion contribution.

### Fibularis tertius

- dorsiflexion;
- eversion;
- anatomically variable / may be absent.

---

## 14. Lateral compartment

Main:
1. fibularis longus;
2. fibularis brevis.

Innervation:
- superficial fibular nerve.

Broad:
- eversion;
- secondary plantarflexion.

### Fibularis longus

Long tendon passes behind lateral malleolus and across plantar foot toward:
- medial cuneiform;
- base of first metatarsal.

This gives it relevance to:
- eversion;
- plantarflexion;
- transverse/medial-lateral arch coupling.

### Fibularis brevis

Insertion:
- base/tuberosity of fifth metatarsal.

Longus and brevis have overlapping but nonidentical mechanical roles.

---

## 15. Superficial posterior compartment

Contains:
1. gastrocnemius;
2. soleus;
3. plantaris.

Gastrocnemius + soleus constitute the principal triceps-surae plantarflexor complex.

---

## 16. Gastrocnemius

Medial and lateral gastrocnemius both:
- arise from distal femoral regions;
- cross knee + ankle;
- contribute ankle plantarflexion;
- assist knee flexion.

Innervation:
- tibial nerve.

Knee angle therefore changes gastrocnemius muscle-tendon geometry.

### 2026 refinement

Current review argues that medial and lateral gastrocnemius should not be assumed functionally identical:
- architecture differs;
- volume differs;
- partial distal tendon independence is reported;
- neural control/secondary functions can differ;
- magnitude of those differences varies substantially among individuals.

Project conclusion:

**medial and lateral gastrocnemius are synergists but not guaranteed interchangeable “two identical heads”.**

Detailed task-specific functional separation → MEC/NEU.

---

## 17. Soleus

Origin broadly:
- posterior proximal fibula;
- soleal line/posterior tibia;
- tendinous arch.

Does not cross knee.

Innervation:
- tibial nerve.

Broad:
- plantarflexion;
- postural/stance control.

Project rule:

**knee position changes gastrocnemius length much more directly than soleus length because soleus does not cross knee.**

Training consequences → MEC/PHY/TEC.

---

## 18. Plantaris

Small, variable biarticular muscle.

Broad:
- weak plantarflexion/knee-flexion contribution.

Can be absent.

Project rule:

**plantaris is not a major ordinary training target.**

---

## 19. Achilles / calcaneal tendon — established core

Achilles/calcaneal tendon transmits major plantarflexor force from triceps surae to posterior calcaneus.

High-confidence:
- gastrocnemius and soleus contribute to the tendon;
- tendon fibers/fascicles exhibit nonuniform/twisted organization;
- structure is not mechanically equivalent to one uniform homogeneous rope.

The free tendon is surrounded by paratenon-type connective tissue rather than a typical synovial tendon sheath.

---

## 20. Achilles subtendon controversy — 2026 update

Legacy wording treated the tendon as a clearly established three-subtendon complex.

That confidence is now reduced.

Arakelian et al. 2026 examined 27 human Achilles tendons histologically:
- some sections showed apparent 2- or 3-subunit patterns;
- frequency analysis was dominated by inter-fascicular distances;
- visual agreement for discrete subtendons was poor;
- standard histology in this elderly cadaver sample could not reliably identify distinct subtendons.

Project rule:

**do not treat “three morphologically discrete Achilles subtendons” as universally established anatomy.**

Current canonical wording:

**Achilles is structurally heterogeneous and twisted, with evidence/models of muscle-specific tendon subregions/subtendon behavior, but the existence and boundaries of discrete histological subtendons remain contested.**

This distinction matters later for calf modeling.

## 21. Deep posterior compartment

Core:
1. popliteus;
2. tibialis posterior;
3. FDL;
4. FHL.

Popliteus is owned primarily by ANA-09.

The remaining three cross posterior to medial malleolus toward the foot.

Innervation:
- tibial nerve.

## 22. Tibialis posterior

Origin:
- posterior tibia/fibula;
- interosseous membrane.

Tendon:
- behind medial malleolus;
- divides into multiple distal attachments.

Broad functions:
- inversion/supination contribution;
- plantarflexion contribution;
- dynamic support of medial/midfoot structures.

Project rule:

**tibialis posterior ≠ simple one-insertion inverter.**

---

## 23. Tibialis-posterior tendon variability — 2026 update

Current 2026 review emphasizes substantial variability in:
- number of branches;
- attachment distribution;
- attachment surface area;
- patterns beyond classic classifications.

Navicular insertion remains prominent, but the tendon frequently has broad multi-bone expansions.

Project rule:

**TPT classifications are descriptive frameworks, not exhaustive personal anatomy.**

Functional significance of individual attachment variants remains incompletely established.

No posture/pathology inference is made from a variant attachment pattern alone.

## 24. FDL / FHL

### FDL

- posterior tibial origin;
- distal tendons to digits 2–5;
- toe flexion;
- plantarflexion/arch-support contribution.

### FHL

- posterior fibular/IOM origin;
- tendon to distal phalanx of hallux;
- hallux flexion;
- plantarflexion/push-off contribution.

Project rule:

**toe flexor systems are part of whole-foot force transmission, not just isolated toe movement.**

## 25. Retinacula / tarsal tunnel

### Extensor retinacula
Constrain anterior tendons and limit bowstringing.

### Fibular retinacula
Constrain fibularis tendons behind lateral malleolus.

### Flexor retinaculum
Forms roof of posteromedial tarsal-tunnel region.

Relevant sequence includes:
- tibialis posterior tendon;
- FDL;
- posterior tibial neurovascular bundle;
- tibial nerve;
- FHL.

Detailed nerve/pathology → ANA-12/MED.

## 26. Extrinsic vs intrinsic foot muscles

Current NCBI reference:
- 10 muscles originate proximally and cross the ankle to act on foot;
- 19 lie intrinsically within foot.

Project rule:

### Extrinsic
Provide major ankle/toe movement and long-tendon force transmission.

### Intrinsic
Contribute local toe control, MTP/IP mechanics, arch support/stiffness and foot-shape control.

“Foot core” is shorthand only.

---

## 27. Dorsal intrinsic muscles

Main:
1. extensor digitorum brevis;
2. extensor hallucis brevis.

Innervation:
- deep fibular nerve.

Broad:
- assist toe extension;
- local forefoot/MTP control.

## 29. Hallux / sesamoids

Two sesamoids usually lie within flexor-hallucis-brevis tendon complex beneath first MTP.

They:
- modify tendon leverage;
- participate in first-ray/forefoot load distribution.

Detailed pressure/lever mechanics → MEC.

## 30. Plantar aponeurosis

Dense plantar connective-tissue structure with:
- central;
- medial;
- lateral bands.

Central band arises from calcaneal region and divides distally toward forefoot/digits.

Broad structural roles:
- links hindfoot to forefoot;
- supports longitudinal arch;
- transmits tension under loading/toe dorsiflexion.

Project preference:
- use **plantar aponeurosis** when precise;
- “plantar fascia” acceptable as common umbrella term.

---

## 31. Plantar-aponeurosis normal variation

Mizuno et al. 2024 dissected 100 feet and documented variation in central-band origin and its relation to abductor hallucis.

Project rule:

**plantar-aponeurosis origin geometry is variable rather than identical across individuals.**

No pathology follows from a variant origin alone.

## 32. Foot arches

Canonical:
1. medial longitudinal;
2. lateral longitudinal;

Current NCBI anatomy emphasizes that these structures function cohesively.

- calcaneus;

## 34. Lateral longitudinal arch

## 35. Transverse arch

Spans:

## 36. Spring / plantar ligament systems

Spring-ligament complex supports the talar head and medial arch as part of a larger talocalcaneonavicular soft-tissue system.

Long and short plantar ligaments reinforce plantar midfoot/lateral-arch relationships.

Project rule:

**no ligament acts as an isolated literal “spring” supporting the foot by itself.**

---

## 37. Windlass mechanism

When MTP joints, especially hallux, dorsiflex:
- plantar aponeurosis wraps/tensions around metatarsal-head region;
- arch can shorten/elevate/stiffen.

Matsumoto & Ogihara directly visualized plantar-aponeurosis deformation with CT in six healthy men.

Important limitation:
- simulated windlass posture was not identical to gait push-off;
- meaningful interindividual morphology/stretching variability was observed.

Project rule:

**windlass is a real structural mechanism, but not a rigid identical response in every foot.**

---

## 38. Foot pronation / supination

Foot pronation/supination are composite 3D motions.

Broadly involve combinations of:
- eversion/inversion;
- abduction/adduction;
- dorsiflexion/plantarflexion.

They are not the same anatomical mechanism as forearm pronation/supination.

Project rule:

**always specify foot vs forearm pronation/supination.**

---

## 42. Calf position boundary

Gastrocnemius crosses knee + ankle.

Soleus crosses ankle only.

Therefore:
- knee flexion shortens gastrocnemius proximally;
- soleus does not undergo that same knee-driven length change.

This is sufficient anatomical basis for later straight-knee vs bent-knee calf work.

It does not establish hypertrophy superiority.

---

## 43. Gastrocnemius heads / triceps-surae boundary

Current 2026 evidence indicates:
- medial/lateral gastrocnemius can differ anatomically and functionally;
- soleus is anatomically distinct and monoarticular.

Project rule:

**“calf = one plantarflexor” is false.**

However anatomy alone cannot rank:
- straight-knee vs bent-knee exercise effectiveness;
- medial vs lateral gastrocnemius targeting.

Those require MEC/PHY/TEC.

---

## 44. Achilles modeling boundary

Current evidence supports tendon heterogeneity/twist.

But 2026 histology questions universally discrete subtendons.

Therefore later models should explicitly state whether they assume:
- fascicle/subregion structure;
- discrete subtendons;
- continuum tendon representation.

Project rule:

**do not smuggle model assumptions into anatomy as settled fact.**

## 47. What changed versus legacy ANA-10

### Confirmed

Legacy core remains valid:
- mortise/talus/talocrural model;
- distal syndesmosis;
- lateral + deltoid ligament complexes;
- subtalar/midtarsal contribution to 3D foot motion;
- four leg compartments;
- gastrocnemius vs soleus biarticular distinction;
- deep posterior muscle map;
- intrinsic/extrinsic foot distinction;
- plantar aponeurosis;
- three arch systems;
- windlass;
- no anatomy-only barefoot/arch-correction prescription.

### Strengthened / updated

1. **Achilles tendon:** current 2026 histology reduces confidence in a universal three-discrete-subtendon model. Structural heterogeneity remains, discrete subtendon boundaries are now explicitly **contested**.
2. **Tibialis posterior tendon:** 2026 review documents extensive branching/insertion variability beyond a navicular-only cartoon; classifications are descriptive, not exhaustive.
3. **Gastrocnemius:** 2026 review argues medial/lateral gastrocnemius have meaningful anatomical/neural/biomechanical differences and should not automatically be treated as identical heads.
4. **Windlass:** 2025 CT evidence directly visualizes plantar-aponeurosis deformation but also demonstrates substantial interindividual variation and a simulated rather than real gait push-off.
5. **Subtalar/midtarsal coupling:** 2026 experimental work reinforces coupled multi-joint effects of extrinsic foot muscles rather than one-muscle/one-joint simplification.

### Tightened

1. No universal “three Achilles subtendons” claim.
2. No navicular-only tibialis-posterior tendon model.
3. No identical-medial/lateral-gastrocnemius assumption.
4. No rigid windlass response.
5. No “foot core” organ.
6. No static arch appearance → pathology/training prescription inference.

## 48. Established findings

### HIGH

1. Talocrural joint is the principal dorsiflexion/plantarflexion articulation within tibia/fibula mortise.
2. Distal syndesmosis stabilizes the mortise.
3. Subtalar + transverse-tarsal systems contribute materially to inversion/eversion and composite foot pronation/supination.
4. Leg contains anterior, lateral, superficial-posterior and deep-posterior compartments.
5. Gastrocnemius is biarticular; soleus is not.
6. Achilles transmits major triceps-surae force to calcaneus and is structurally heterogeneous rather than a homogeneous rope.
7. Tibialis posterior has broad variable distal attachments and contributes to inversion/plantarflexion/arch support.
8. Foot contains extrinsic and intrinsic muscle systems.
9. Foot arches integrate bony, ligamentous/aponeurotic and muscular supports.
10. Plantar aponeurosis contributes to arch support and windlass behavior.
11. Normal foot/ankle anatomy varies materially.

### MODERATE / CONTESTED

1. **Discrete Achilles subtendon anatomy:** muscle-specific subregions/subtendon models exist, but 2026 histology did not reliably identify universal discrete subtendons.
2. Exact functional significance of tibialis-posterior attachment variants remains uncertain.
3. Exact secondary functional differences between gastrocnemius medialis/lateralis are task- and individual-dependent.
4. Exact subtalar/midtarsal coupling and arch stiffness require biomechanics.
5. Windlass deformation magnitude/pattern varies between individuals.
