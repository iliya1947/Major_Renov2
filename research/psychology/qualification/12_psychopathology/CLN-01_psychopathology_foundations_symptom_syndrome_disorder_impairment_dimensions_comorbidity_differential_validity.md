# CLN-01 — Psychopathology foundations: symptom, syndrome, disorder, impairment, dimensional/categorical models, comorbidity, differential inference, and diagnostic validity limits

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Establish the foundational clinical-psychopathology reasoning layer required before studying specific disorders, neurodevelopmental conditions, interventions, safety, or individualized clinical-style formulations.

This block is not a diagnostic manual and is not intended to train Kira to assign formal diagnoses.

Its purpose is to prevent a more basic class of errors:

- treating a symptom as a diagnosis;
- treating a diagnosis as a mechanism;
- treating a syndrome as a natural disease entity;
- treating distress and impairment as interchangeable;
- treating a categorical threshold as a sharp biological boundary;
- treating comorbidity as proof that several independent diseases are present;
- treating one salient diagnosis as sufficient explanation for all symptoms;
- treating a transdiagnostic research construct as a clinical diagnosis;
- treating absence of a diagnosis as absence of clinically meaningful difficulty;
- treating diagnostic reliability, validity, and clinical utility as the same property.

The central clinical inference rule is:

`presentation -> symptom/sign description -> time/course/context -> impairment/distress/risk -> alternative explanations -> differential structure -> classification if warranted -> uncertainty -> mechanism hypotheses remain separate`.

Never:

`familiar symptom -> label -> mechanism -> certainty`.

---

# I. Current classification anchor

## 1. DSM-5-TR remains the current DSM edition

As of the evidence cutoff:

- the American Psychiatric Association identifies **DSM-5-TR** as the latest DSM edition;
- DSM-5-TR was published in 2022;
- APA continues to publish approved criteria/text supplements and coding updates;
- APA's current DSM page states that no ICD-10-CM code changes are planned for DSM-5-TR in 2026 and that criteria/text changes are expected to be posted in early 2027.

Therefore:

**DSM is a living maintained classification system, not a frozen scientific ontology.**

## 2. ICD-11 is the current WHO global classification framework

ICD-11:

- was adopted by the World Health Assembly in 2019;
- came into effect for health reporting in January 2022;
- has a dedicated 2024 WHO Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements manual for mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders (CDDR).

The CDDR is designed to support clinical diagnosis across diverse global settings and includes:

- essential features;
- boundary with normality;
- developmental presentations;
- culture-related features;
- differential diagnosis;
- co-occurring conditions.

## 3. DSM and ICD are classifications, not complete causal theories

A diagnostic classification can be useful for:

- clinical communication;
- case identification;
- treatment planning;
- prognosis;
- service access;
- epidemiology;
- research sampling;
- coding.

This does not mean that each category:

- corresponds to one unique biological mechanism;
- has one necessary cause;
- has one biomarker;
- has perfectly sharp boundaries;
- is homogeneous within the category.

Operational utility and causal ontology are different questions.

---

# II. Core descriptive units

## 4. Symptom

A **symptom** is a reported or elicited experience/behavioral difficulty relevant to a clinical presentation.

Examples of symptom domains include:

- low mood;
- fear;
- intrusive thoughts;
- sleep disturbance;
- loss of interest;
- concentration difficulty;
- unusual perceptual experiences.

A symptom is not automatically:

- a disorder;
- a mechanism;
- a stable trait;
- pathological outside context.

## 5. Sign / observed feature

A **sign** or observed clinical feature is something detected through observation, examination, testing, or collateral information.

Psychiatric terminology does not always enforce a strict symptom/sign distinction, but the inference distinction remains useful:

`self-report != observer report != task performance != physiological measurement`.

Convergence strengthens inference.

Disagreement can be informative.

## 6. Symptom occurrence must be contextualized

The same descriptive symptom can differ in meaning depending on:

- intensity;
- frequency;
- duration;
- onset;
- developmental stage;
- cultural context;
- precipitating event;
- substance/medication exposure;
- medical condition;
- functional consequences;
- associated symptoms.

Therefore:

`same symptom label != same clinical process`.

## 7. Syndrome

A **syndrome** is a recognizable cluster/pattern of symptoms and signs that co-occur in a clinically meaningful way.

A syndrome can be useful even when:

- etiology is unknown;
- mechanisms are heterogeneous;
- boundaries are probabilistic.

Therefore:

`syndrome != identified cause`.

## 8. Disorder

A **mental disorder** is a clinically meaningful classification involving significant disturbance in psychological/behavioral functioning under a defined diagnostic framework.

WHO's current public description emphasizes clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotional regulation, or behavior, usually associated with distress or impairment.

The exact operational requirements vary across disorders.

Therefore:

- distress is not one universal diagnostic rule;
- impairment is not one universal diagnostic rule;
- exact symptom count/duration rules differ by classification and diagnosis.

## 9. Diagnosis

A **diagnosis** is an inference that a presentation satisfies a recognized diagnostic formulation under a classification system and professional clinical judgment.

Diagnosis is not identical to:

- symptom description;
- case formulation;
- causal explanation;
- prognosis;
- treatment prescription;
- personality description;
- biological mechanism.

## 10. Clinical formulation

A formulation attempts to organize:

- presenting problems;
- vulnerabilities;
- triggers;
- maintaining processes;
- protective factors;
- context;
- history;
- mechanisms/hypotheses.

A diagnosis can be one input to formulation.

It is not the formulation itself.

---

# III. Disorder does not equal mechanism

## 11. Diagnostic labels are descriptive/inferential compression

A diagnosis can summarize a recurring pattern efficiently.

But compression loses detail.

Two people with the same diagnosis can differ in:

- symptom profile;
- severity;
- onset;
- course;
- impairment;
- comorbidity;
- environmental context;
- treatment response;
- risk factors.

Therefore:

`same diagnosis -> same mechanism`

is invalid.

## 12. Mechanistic explanation requires separate evidence

To infer a mechanism, ask:

- what process is proposed?
- what evidence distinguishes it from alternatives?
- what timescale?
- what level: cognitive, behavioral, social, developmental, neural, biological?
- does manipulating the mechanism change the target outcome?
- is the mechanism specific or transdiagnostic?

Diagnostic membership alone cannot answer these questions.

## 13. A mechanism can cross diagnoses

Processes such as:

- avoidance;
- rumination;
- threat sensitivity;
- sleep disruption;
- impulsive responding;
- reward-learning abnormalities;
- cognitive control difficulty;

may appear across several diagnostic categories.

This motivates transdiagnostic research.

It does not imply that one mechanism explains every instance of a symptom.

## 14. One diagnosis can contain multiple mechanisms

Within one category, different individuals may arrive at similar symptom patterns through different pathways.

This is consistent with:

- equifinality;
- multifinality;
- developmental heterogeneity;
- environmental heterogeneity.

Therefore diagnostic categories should not be reverse-engineered into a single causal pathway without evidence.

---

# IV. Distress, impairment, dysfunction, and risk

## 15. Distress

Distress refers to subjective suffering or discomfort.

It can be:

- intense with modest observable impairment;
- modest despite severe impairment;
- minimized by low insight;
- culturally shaped in expression.

Therefore distress should not be used as the sole universal index of disorder severity.

## 16. Functional impairment

Impairment refers to reduced functioning in important domains such as:

- work/education;
- relationships;
- self-care;
- daily responsibilities;
- independent functioning.

Impairment is not identical to symptom severity.

## 17. Distress and impairment can dissociate

Possible patterns:

- high distress + preserved functioning;
- low subjective distress + substantial impairment;
- severe symptoms + contextual accommodation;
- mild symptoms + major impairment because of occupational/environmental demands.

Therefore both require explicit assessment.

## 18. Functional impairment is context-dependent

A behavior can be more or less impairing depending on:

- environmental demands;
- support/accommodation;
- resources;
- role requirements;
- cultural expectations.

Thus:

`impairment = person × environment outcome`

rather than a pure trait of the person.

## 19. Dysfunction is stronger than difference

Statistical rarity, unusual behavior, or deviation from cultural norms does not by itself establish psychopathology.

A strong inference requires attention to:

- dysfunction;
- distress;
- impairment;
- risk;
- developmental context;
- cultural context.

## 20. Harm/risk and current distress are distinct

Some presentations can carry major future risk even when current subjective distress is low.

Therefore clinical significance cannot always be reduced to immediate discomfort.

---

# V. Boundary with normality

## 21. Many psychopathology dimensions are continuous

WHO's ICD-11 CDDR explicitly treats the boundary between disorder and normal variation as a threshold problem for many disorders.

The core principle:

**there is often no sharp natural boundary between mild psychopathology and normal-range variation.**

## 22. Categorical decisions can be imposed on continuous variation

A clinical system may need a yes/no decision for:

- communication;
- treatment eligibility;
- reimbursement;
- service allocation;
- epidemiology.

This does not prove that the underlying construct is categorical.

## 23. Thresholds have purposes

A diagnostic threshold can be optimized for:

- reliability;
- clinical utility;
- risk;
- treatment benefit;
- service allocation;
- specificity;
- sensitivity.

Different purposes can support different thresholds.

## 24. Threshold uncertainty is not diagnostic nihilism

The absence of a sharp biological boundary does not mean diagnoses are useless.

It means:

- borderline cases require more uncertainty;
- thresholds are partly decision rules;
- dimensional severity should often be retained alongside categories.

---

# VI. DSM and ICD use categorical and dimensional information

## 25. Modern classifications are not purely categorical

DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 include dimensional elements such as:

- severity;
- specifiers;
- symptom dimensions;
- functional impact;
- dimensional personality assessment in ICD-11.

Therefore the simple contrast:

`DSM/ICD = categorical`
versus
`new frameworks = dimensional`

is inaccurate.

## 26. Categories remain useful when decisions are categorical

Even if symptoms vary dimensionally, care systems often require categorical actions.

Examples:

- emergency intervention;
- admission;
- treatment eligibility;
- legal/administrative decisions.

The decision can be categorical while the underlying phenotype is dimensional.

## 27. Severity should not be inferred from diagnosis count alone

Two diagnoses do not necessarily imply twice the illness burden of one diagnosis.

Severity can depend on:

- symptom intensity;
- functional impairment;
- chronicity;
- risk;
- breadth of affected domains.

Diagnosis count is a crude burden indicator.

---

# VII. Diagnostic reliability, validity, and clinical utility

## 28. Reliability

Diagnostic reliability asks whether comparable evaluators/procedures reach sufficiently consistent classifications.

Reliability can be improved by:

- explicit criteria;
- structured interviews;
- clear thresholds;
- training.

Reliability does not establish validity.

## 29. Validity

Diagnostic validity asks whether a classification corresponds to meaningful and reproducible distinctions relevant to:

- course;
- etiology;
- mechanisms;
- treatment response;
- external criteria;
- functional outcomes.

Validity is multidimensional.

## 30. Clinical utility

Clinical utility asks whether the classification helps clinicians:

- communicate;
- make decisions;
- plan care;
- estimate prognosis;
- identify risks;
- select interventions.

A category can have utility even if its causal validity is incomplete.

## 31. Reliability, validity, and utility can dissociate

Possible:

- reliable but biologically heterogeneous diagnosis;
- valid dimension that is awkward for service coding;
- clinically useful category with fuzzy boundaries.

Therefore:

`reliable != valid != useful`.

---

# VIII. Diagnostic heterogeneity

## 32. Polythetic criteria create multiple presentations

Many diagnoses allow different combinations of qualifying symptoms.

This can produce substantial within-diagnosis heterogeneity.

## 33. Theoretical combination count can exaggerate practical heterogeneity

A 2024 JAMA Psychiatry study across four large samples showed:

- many theoretically possible symptom combinations were extremely uncommon;
- a relatively small number of common combinations accounted for a substantial fraction of cases.

Therefore:

**diagnostic heterogeneity is real, but not all mathematically possible presentations are equally probable.**

## 34. Heterogeneity still constrains mechanism inference

Even when common prototypes exist, category membership alone can hide:

- distinct symptom clusters;
- differing severity;
- different mechanisms;
- different risk factors.

Therefore mechanism research should often retain symptom-level or dimensional information.

---

# IX. Comorbidity

## 35. Comorbidity is common and diagnostically important

Comorbidity means that more than one diagnostic condition is present under the rules of the classification system.

It can be:

- concurrent;
- sequential/lifetime;
- symptom-overlap driven;
- due to shared liability;
- causally linked;
- partly artifactual.

## 36. Comorbidity is not one phenomenon

Possible explanations include:

1. shared risk factors;
2. causal influence of one condition on another;
3. overlapping diagnostic criteria;
4. common transdiagnostic mechanisms;
5. broad latent liability;
6. ascertainment/sampling effects;
7. symptom-level causal connections;
8. classification boundaries splitting a broader syndrome.

No single explanation should be assumed.

## 37. Shared symptoms can create artificial overlap

If two diagnoses share symptoms or closely related criteria, observed comorbidity can partly arise from classification design.

Therefore inspect criterion overlap before inferring independent disorders.

## 38. Shared liability can create genuine covariance

Internalizing, externalizing, thought-disorder and other broad dimensions capture substantial covariance among narrower forms of psychopathology.

This supports hierarchical/transdiagnostic models.

It does not prove one universal cause.

## 39. Sequential comorbidity requires time-order analysis

If one diagnosis tends to precede another, possibilities include:

- causal influence;
- shared developmental liability;
- common environmental exposure;
- measurement artifacts;
- diagnostic switching.

Temporal sequence alone does not establish causation.

---

# X. HiTOP — hierarchical dimensional psychopathology

## 40. What HiTOP is

The Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) is a dimensional hierarchical framework developed by a consortium of psychopathology researchers.

It organizes psychopathology from:

- symptoms/maladaptive behaviors;
- homogeneous components/traits;
- syndromes/subfactors;
- spectra;
- broader superspectra.

## 41. Why HiTOP was developed

HiTOP aims to address limitations of traditional categorical diagnosis including:

- heterogeneity;
- high comorbidity;
- arbitrary thresholds;
- instability;
- excessive lumping/splitting.

## 42. HiTOP is strongly evidence-informed but not a final ontology

The 2024 State of the Science review presents substantial evidence and expanding clinical applications.

But HiTOP remains:

- a developing framework;
- incomplete in coverage;
- dependent on measurement/model choices;
- not equivalent to DSM/ICD clinical diagnosis.

## 43. HiTOP dimensions are statistical/descriptive structures

A HiTOP spectrum summarizes covariation.

It does not automatically identify:

- one cause;
- one neural system;
- one treatment target.

This follows the same anti-reification rule established in PSYCHOMETRICS.

## 44. HiTOP and diagnosis can coexist

A practical assessment can retain:

- DSM/ICD category for clinical communication;
- dimensional symptom/severity profile;
- transdiagnostic dimensions.

These answer different questions.

---

# XI. General psychopathology / p factor

## 45. The p factor summarizes broad covariance

The p-factor literature models the positive correlations across many forms of psychopathology.

It can be associated with:

- broad symptom burden;
- impairment;
- persistence;
- comorbidity;
- adverse outcomes.

## 46. p is not automatically a single causal mechanism

The substantive meaning of p remains debated.

Candidate interpretations have included:

- general liability;
- impairment;
- negative emotionality;
- dysregulation;
- broad severity;
- statistical positive manifold.

Therefore:

`p factor -> one brain mechanism`

is invalid.

## 47. Statistical model and substantive construct must be separated

Different bifactor/higher-order models can produce general factors with different meanings.

A general factor should not be reified merely because model fit is good.

## 48. Broad factors can be clinically useful without causal uniqueness

A broad psychopathology score can summarize severity/burden and predict outcomes.

That utility does not imply it is the single underlying disease.

---

# XII. RDoC — research framework, not diagnosis

## 49. What RDoC is

NIMH's Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) is a research framework for studying dimensions of neurobehavioral functioning across the full range from normal to abnormal.

It integrates units of analysis including:

- self-report;
- behavior;
- physiology;
- circuits;
- molecules/genes where relevant.

## 50. RDoC is explicitly not a diagnostic guide

NIMH states that RDoC:

- is not intended as a diagnostic guide;
- is not intended to replace current diagnostic systems.

Therefore:

`RDoC construct -> diagnosis`

is invalid.

## 51. RDoC reverses the usual research starting point

Traditional diagnosis-first research:

`diagnostic group -> seek mechanism`.

RDoC-style research:

`functional system/dimension -> examine dysfunction across diagnoses and normal variation`.

Both can be informative for different purposes.

## 52. RDoC constructs are not automatically mechanisms either

A construct such as threat, reward, or cognitive control is still a scientific construct requiring:

- operationalization;
- measurement validity;
- causal evidence.

The word “domain” does not make the construct mechanistic.

---

# XIII. Diagnostic classification versus research nosology

## 53. Three frameworks answer different questions

### DSM/ICD

Primary question:

> Does this presentation satisfy a clinically recognized diagnostic classification?

### HiTOP

Primary question:

> Where does this presentation fall within an empirically organized hierarchy of psychopathology dimensions?

### RDoC

Primary question:

> Which neurobehavioral functional systems/dimensions may be involved across normal and abnormal functioning?

## 54. Do not force equivalence

Do not assume:

- one DSM disorder = one HiTOP dimension;
- one HiTOP spectrum = one RDoC system;
- one RDoC construct = one diagnosis.

Mappings are many-to-many.

## 55. Framework disagreement can be informative

If DSM category, HiTOP profile, and RDoC process point to different structures, this may indicate:

- category heterogeneity;
- transdiagnostic mechanism;
- measurement mismatch;
- incomplete theory.

Do not resolve disagreement by choosing the most neuroscientific-sounding framework.

---

# XIV. Differential diagnosis as structured inference

## 56. Differential diagnosis is not a list of labels

It is a process of comparing competing explanations for a clinical presentation.

Generic structure:

`presenting features -> candidate explanations -> discriminating evidence -> exclusions/alternatives -> co-occurrence -> uncertainty`.

## 57. Start with the presentation, not the favored label

Premature diagnostic anchoring can create confirmation bias.

First specify:

- symptoms/features;
- onset;
- duration;
- course;
- context;
- impairment;
- risk.

Then compare candidates.

## 58. “Rule out” means test an alternative explanation

A condition should not be ruled out merely because another diagnosis seems plausible.

Use discriminating evidence.

## 59. Medical/neurological explanations matter

Some psychiatric-like symptoms can arise from:

- neurological disease;
- endocrine/metabolic conditions;
- sleep disorders;
- infection/inflammation;
- medication effects;
- other medical conditions.

The exact differential is symptom-specific and belongs in later disorder-focused blocks.

## 60. Substance/medication effects matter

Symptoms can be:

- induced;
- amplified;
- masked;
- temporally associated

with substances or medications.

Temporal relation, dose/exposure, withdrawal, and persistence matter.

## 61. Sleep can be cause, consequence, or correlate

Sleep disturbance can:

- be a symptom;
- worsen psychopathology;
- mimic cognitive/affective symptoms;
- reflect another sleep disorder.

Do not automatically classify sleep symptoms as secondary to a psychiatric diagnosis.

## 62. Developmental context matters

A behavior can have different meaning at different developmental stages.

Developmental appropriateness, onset pattern, and age-related presentation must be evaluated.

## 63. Cultural context matters

Cultural context influences:

- symptom expression;
- idioms of distress;
- help-seeking;
- interpretation;
- normative thresholds.

Difference from local norms alone does not prove disorder.

## 64. Contextual reaction versus disorder requires care

Grief, stress, fear, anger, and sadness can be expectable responses.

But contextual intelligibility does not automatically mean “not a disorder.”

The question is whether the presentation crosses the relevant clinical threshold considering:

- severity;
- persistence;
- impairment/distress;
- risk;
- syndrome features;
- cultural/developmental context.

---

# XV. Co-occurrence versus differential exclusion

## 65. Competing diagnoses are not always mutually exclusive

Some conditions can genuinely co-occur.

Differential diagnosis asks whether one condition better explains the presentation.

It does not mean only one diagnosis may ever be assigned.

## 66. Avoid diagnostic parsimony as an absolute rule

“Choose the fewest diagnoses” can underrepresent genuine co-occurrence.

Prefer:

- explanatory adequacy;
- classification rules;
- independent symptom evidence;
- temporal/course evidence.

## 67. Avoid diagnostic proliferation

Conversely, overlapping symptoms should not automatically generate multiple diagnoses.

Ask whether each diagnosis has:

- independent required features;
- distinct course/context;
- incremental explanatory/clinical value.

---

# XVI. Screening versus diagnosis

## 68. Screening is not diagnosis

A screening instrument estimates likelihood/risk or detects possible cases.

A positive screen:

`-> further assessment`

not:

`-> diagnosis established`.

## 69. Base rates matter

Positive predictive value depends strongly on prevalence/base rate.

A screening tool can have good sensitivity/specificity and still produce many false positives in a low-prevalence setting.

## 70. Structured interviews improve standardization, not infallibility

Structured/semi-structured interviews can increase reliability.

But validity still depends on:

- criteria;
- respondent information;
- interviewer skill;
- context;
- recall;
- differential diagnosis;
- classification limits.

---

# XVII. Symptom measurement itself can be heterogeneous

## 71. “The same symptom” can be operationalized differently

Different instruments can define or sample a symptom differently.

Examples:

- anhedonia as reduced pleasure versus reduced motivation;
- anxiety as cognitive worry versus physiological arousal;
- impulsivity as trait self-report versus task behavior.

Therefore:

`same symptom name != same measurement construct`.

## 72. Transdiagnostic research does not automatically escape measurement problems

Replacing DSM diagnoses with symptom dimensions can reduce some category problems.

It does not remove:

- measurement heterogeneity;
- construct ambiguity;
- method variance.

Symptom-level research still requires psychometric validation.

---

# XVIII. Diagnostic validity limits

## 73. No diagnosis should be treated as a natural kind by default

Evidence required for a strong natural-kind claim would include unusually coherent:

- boundaries;
- mechanisms;
- course;
- biomarkers;
- treatment response.

Most current psychiatric classifications are better treated as clinically useful constructs with varying degrees of validity.

## 74. Biomarker absence does not make a disorder unreal

A condition can be:

- clinically reproducible;
- impairing;
- treatment-responsive;

without one specific biomarker.

Lack of biomarker specificity limits mechanistic certainty, not necessarily clinical reality.

## 75. Biomarker presence does not automatically validate a category

A biological correlate can be:

- nonspecific;
- downstream;
- state-dependent;
- treatment-related;
- confounded.

Therefore:

`biological difference -> diagnostic essence`

is invalid.

## 76. Etiological heterogeneity limits reverse inference

If one diagnosis has multiple causal pathways, observing the diagnosis cannot identify which pathway applies to a particular person.

## 77. Treatment response does not prove diagnosis

Improvement after a treatment can occur because:

- treatment targets a transdiagnostic process;
- placebo/context effects;
- nonspecific therapeutic factors;
- multiple conditions respond to same intervention.

Therefore:

`responded to X -> must have disorder Y`

is invalid.

---

# XIX. Severity and impairment architecture

## 78. Symptom count is not severity

Severity can vary through:

- intensity;
- frequency;
- duration;
- functional impact;
- risk;
- persistence;
- breadth.

A higher count of mild symptoms can differ clinically from fewer severe symptoms.

## 79. Functional impairment should be measured directly when important

Do not infer impairment only from diagnosis.

Ask about:

- performance;
- relationships;
- self-care;
- role functioning;
- participation.

## 80. Global impairment can create spurious cross-diagnostic correlations

Broad impairment can correlate with many symptom domains.

Thus a general psychopathology factor or comorbidity pattern may partly index global burden rather than one causal liability.

This is a hypothesis requiring testing, not a settled universal interpretation.

---

# XX. Longitudinal psychopathology

## 81. Diagnosis can change over time

A person can:

- remit;
- relapse;
- shift symptom profile;
- cross thresholds;
- receive a different diagnosis later.

Diagnostic instability can reflect:

- true change;
- measurement error;
- developmental change;
- threshold effects;
- category overlap.

## 82. Heterotypic continuity

The form of psychopathology can change while broader liability/function remains related.

Therefore:

`different diagnosis later -> unrelated process`

is not justified.

## 83. Homotypic continuity

The same diagnostic/symptom pattern can persist across time.

Persistence increases evidence for stability but still does not identify mechanism.

## 84. Episode, state, and trait must be separated

A current episode does not automatically imply a lifelong trait.

A longstanding vulnerability does not imply continuous symptoms.

---

# XXI. Diagnostic inference hierarchy

## 85. Level 0 — isolated feature

One symptom/sign is present.

No disorder inference.

## 86. Level 1 — coherent symptom pattern

Several related features co-occur.

Possible syndrome hypothesis.

## 87. Level 2 — threshold/context support

Duration/severity/distress/impairment/course support clinically significant syndrome.

## 88. Level 3 — differential support

Major alternatives are assessed:

- medical;
- substance/medication;
- developmental;
- contextual;
- other psychiatric syndromes.

## 89. Level 4 — classification support

Presentation satisfies a DSM/ICD-style diagnostic formulation.

This supports a diagnosis under that system.

It still does not identify etiology.

## 90. Level 5 — formulation support

Additional evidence supports:

- maintaining processes;
- vulnerabilities;
- triggers;
- functional relations.

## 91. Level 6 — mechanism support

Independent mechanistic evidence distinguishes the proposed process from alternatives.

Mechanism inference requires more than diagnosis.

---

# XXII. Confidence rules

## 92. High confidence can apply to description without high confidence in cause

Example structure:

- high confidence: recurrent panic episodes are reported;
- moderate confidence: pattern resembles a panic syndrome;
- lower confidence: one hypothesized learning/interoceptive mechanism causes it.

Keep confidence claim-specific.

## 93. Diagnostic uncertainty should not be hidden

Appropriate outputs include:

- provisional;
- differential remains open;
- insufficient evidence;
- mixed features;
- subthreshold;
- unspecified/other-specified categories in formal systems where applicable.

Uncertainty is information.

## 94. “Not enough evidence” is preferable to forced classification

Do not complete a category merely because a familiar pattern is present.

---

# XXIII. Clinical boundary for Kira

## 95. Repository knowledge can support reasoning, not professional diagnosis

Kira may:

- explain diagnostic frameworks;
- compare hypotheses;
- discuss symptom patterns;
- identify uncertainty;
- help prepare questions for professionals;
- analyze scientific evidence.

Kira must not represent project analysis as:

- formal diagnosis;
- licensed clinical assessment;
- medico-legal opinion;
- treatment order.

## 96. Labels should not become identity statements

Prefer:

`evidence is compatible with X`

over:

`the person is X`.

Diagnosis is a clinical classification, not an exhaustive identity description.

---

# XXIV. Cross-domain routing

## 97. COG / EXE / MET

Cognitive difficulty:

- process/task interpretation -> COG/EXE/MET;
- measurement -> PSYCHOMETRICS;
- clinical disorder inference -> CLN/NDEV.

## 98. LEA / MOT / AFF / STR

Transdiagnostic mechanisms such as avoidance, reward sensitivity, rumination, regulation, stress response:

- mechanism -> substantive domain;
- syndrome/diagnosis -> CLN.

## 99. PER

Personality trait:

- dimensional trait science -> PER;
- maladaptive clinical personality classification -> CLN / later clinical block.

## 100. DEV

Developmental appropriateness/onset/course:

- developmental framework -> DEV;
- neurodevelopmental diagnosis -> NDEV.

## 101. NEU

Neural correlate:

- neural evidence -> NEU;
- cannot establish diagnosis alone;
- person-level score validity -> PSYCHOMETRICS.

## 102. SAF

Acute self-harm, suicide, violence, severe inability to care for self, or other crisis thresholds route to SAF.

Safety takes priority over fine diagnostic classification.

# XXV. 78-question Psychopathology Foundations Inference Gate

## A. Description

1. What exactly is observed/reported?
2. Which source reported it?
3. Is it a symptom, sign, behavior, score, or interpretation?
4. What is its intensity?
5. Frequency?
6. Duration?
7. Onset?
8. Episodic or persistent?
9. What contexts increase/decrease it?
10. Is the wording itself importing a diagnosis?

## B. Clinical significance

11. What distress is present?
12. What functional impairment is present?
13. Which domains are impaired?
14. Is impairment caused by the symptom or merely correlated?
15. Is there risk despite low distress?
16. Could environmental demands explain apparent impairment?
17. Is the pattern developmentally atypical?
18. Is cultural context considered?

## C. Syndrome

19. Do symptoms form a coherent cluster?
20. Which features are required versus optional?
21. Is the cluster temporally coherent?
22. Is one symptom driving the impression of the whole syndrome?
23. Is syndrome language being mistaken for etiology?
24. Are subthreshold features clinically relevant?

## D. Classification

25. Which system is being used: DSM, ICD, or another?
26. Is the current version being used?
27. Are duration requirements met?
28. Are severity/impairment requirements met where applicable?
29. Are specifiers relevant?
30. Is the category being treated as a natural kind?
31. Would a dimensional description preserve important information?

## E. Differential inference

32. What alternative psychiatric syndromes fit?
33. What medical/neurological alternatives fit?
34. What sleep-related alternatives fit?
35. What substance/medication effects fit?
36. What developmental alternatives fit?
37. What contextual/expectable reactions fit?
38. What discriminating evidence favors each?
39. Are alternatives actually tested or merely listed?
40. Is diagnostic anchoring occurring?

## F. Comorbidity

# XXVI. High-value failure modes

## Failure A — symptom = disorder

Incorrect.

A symptom can occur in multiple disorders and normal/contextual states.

## Failure B — diagnosis = cause

Incorrect.

Diagnosis is classification/inference, not mechanism.

## Failure C — syndrome = disease entity

Incorrect by default.

A syndrome can be clinically useful without one unique cause.

## Failure D — unusual = pathological

Incorrect.

Rarity/deviation alone does not establish disorder.

## Failure E — distress = impairment

Incorrect.

They can dissociate.

## Failure F — impairment = pure person trait

Incorrect.

Function depends on person × environment.

## Failure G — categorical threshold = biological boundary

Incorrect.

Many clinical dimensions are continuous.

## Failure H — dimensional = automatically better

Incorrect.

Categories can have clinical/administrative utility.

## Failure I — DSM/ICD = purely categorical

Incorrect.

Both incorporate dimensional/severity information.

## Failure J — diagnostic reliability = validity

Incorrect.

Consistent classification can still be etiologically heterogeneous.

## Failure K — validity = clinical utility

Incorrect.

A clinically useful category can have incomplete causal validity.

## Failure L — comorbidity = several independent diseases

Incorrect.

Shared liability, overlapping criteria, causal relations and classification structure are alternatives.

## Failure M — one diagnosis explains everything

Incorrect.

Co-occurrence and differential alternatives remain possible.

## Failure N — choose only one diagnosis

Incorrect as a universal rule.

Some conditions genuinely co-occur.

## Failure O — every overlapping symptom gets another diagnosis

Incorrect.

Independent required features and incremental clinical value matter.

## Failure P — positive screen = diagnosis

Incorrect.

Screening estimates possible case/risk.

## Failure Q — HiTOP = replacement diagnosis

Incorrect.

It is a dimensional nosological framework, not current official diagnostic classification.

## Failure R — RDoC = diagnostic system

Incorrect.

NIMH explicitly states it is not.

## Failure S — p factor = single underlying disease

Unsupported.

The substantive meaning of p remains debated.

## Failure T — good treatment response proves diagnosis

Incorrect.

Treatments can operate transdiagnostically.

## Failure U — biomarker = diagnosis/mechanism

Incorrect without specificity, calibration, and causal evidence.

## Failure V — culturally unusual = disordered

Incorrect.

Cultural context is part of threshold interpretation.

## Failure W — expected reaction = never disorder

Incorrect.

Contextual intelligibility does not eliminate clinically significant syndromes.

## Failure X — same symptom name = same construct

Incorrect.

Measurement/operationalization can differ.

# XXVII. Evidence-strength map

## High-confidence working rules

1. symptom, syndrome, diagnosis, formulation, and mechanism are distinct;
2. diagnosis does not itself explain cause;
3. distress and impairment are distinct;
4. many psychopathology dimensions show continuity with normal variation;
5. categorical thresholds can remain clinically useful;
6. reliability, validity, and clinical utility are distinct;
7. diagnostic categories can be heterogeneous;
8. comorbidity has multiple possible sources;
9. differential diagnosis requires competing explanations;
10. medical/substance/developmental/contextual alternatives matter;
11. screening is not diagnosis;
12. DSM/ICD are clinical classifications with dimensional components;
13. HiTOP is dimensional/hierarchical and not a formal replacement diagnostic system;
14. RDoC is a research framework and not a diagnostic guide;
15. p-factor meaning is not settled enough to treat it as one causal mechanism;
16. diagnosis does not establish biological mechanism;
17. treatment response does not prove diagnosis;
18. individual inference must remain uncertainty-calibrated.

## Supported but context-sensitive

1. dimensional profiles can preserve information lost by categories;
2. HiTOP can improve organization of heterogeneity/comorbidity for some research/clinical purposes;
3. broad psychopathology factors can index general burden/severity;
4. structured assessment can improve diagnostic reliability;
5. dimensional severity can improve monitoring;
6. transdiagnostic process models can improve mechanism research.

## Not universal

1. one threshold that cleanly separates health from disorder;
2. one mechanism per diagnosis;
3. one diagnosis per person;
4. one p-factor interpretation;
5. one framework that replaces DSM/ICD for every purpose;
6. one biomarker that validates most current psychiatric categories;
7. symptom count as universal severity metric.

# Canonical working rule

**Diagnosis is a clinically useful classification under a defined system; it is not, by itself, a mechanism, cause, identity, prognosis, or treatment prescription.**

Use:

`presentation -> descriptive features -> severity/time/context -> distress/impairment/risk -> differential alternatives -> dimensional structure -> classification if warranted -> formulation/mechanism hypotheses -> uncertainty`.

Never:

`symptom -> diagnosis`

`diagnosis -> mechanism`

`comorbidity -> independent diseases`

`threshold -> natural boundary`

`HiTOP dimension -> diagnosis`

`RDoC construct -> diagnosis`

`p factor -> one cause`

`screen positive -> diagnosis`

`treatment response -> diagnostic proof`.

## Source-boundary note

DSM-5-TR and ICD-11/CDDR are used as current clinical-classification anchors, not as proof that every diagnostic category is a natural biological entity.

HiTOP is used as a developing dimensional nosology with substantial structural evidence, not as a formal replacement diagnosis or causal theory.
