# AFF-02 — Emotion regulation architecture: process, reappraisal, acceptance, suppression, avoidance, flexibility, and measurement validity

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: generic public affective-science evidence and reusable inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

AFF-01 established that emotion is multicomponent and cannot be inferred uniquely from facial expression, physiology, arousal, or a label. AFF-02 addresses the next inference problem:

**What does it mean to regulate an emotion, which mechanisms can change an emotional episode, and when is a regulation strategy actually adaptive?**

High-value failures to prevent:
- "reappraisal is always good";
- "suppression is always bad";
- "acceptance means doing nothing";
- "avoidance and situation selection are the same";
- "distraction means denial";
- "rumination is simply thinking about an emotion";
- "strategy use frequency = strategy ability";
- "strategy ability = strategy effectiveness";
- "a strategy that reduces negative affect is globally adaptive";
- "more flexible strategy switching is automatically healthier";
- "one questionnaire measures emotion regulation itself";
- "emotion regulation difficulty uniquely identifies a diagnosis or executive-control deficit".

## Core rule

**Emotion regulation is a goal-directed attempt to influence which emotions occur, when they occur, how they are experienced or expressed, and what consequences they have. Strategy value depends on target, timing, goals, context, intensity, implementation quality, costs, and outcome channel.**

No strategy has an invariant adaptive or maladaptive sign across all settings.

## 1. Regulation is not the same as emotion generation

Emotion generation and emotion regulation are conceptually separable but interact recursively.

A regulation attempt can:
- alter the situation;
- alter attention;
- alter interpretation/appraisal;
- alter expression or physiological responding;
- alter later exposure or action;
- fail, overshoot, or create secondary emotions.

A change in emotional intensity can occur without deliberate regulation, for example through habituation, learning, fatigue, changing context, or spontaneous recovery.

Do not infer regulation merely because affect changed.

## 2. Gross process model: five strategy families

The process model organizes strategies by the point at which they influence emotion generation:

1. **Situation selection** — approach or avoid situations likely to evoke certain emotions.
2. **Situation modification** — change external features of the current situation.
3. **Attentional deployment** — redirect or focus attention.
4. **Cognitive change** — alter appraisal or meaning, including reappraisal.
5. **Response modulation** — influence experiential, behavioral, expressive, or physiological responses after emotion is underway.

This taxonomy is organizational, not a proof that regulation always unfolds in a fixed linear order.

## 3. Extended process model: regulation is a cycle

Modern process accounts separate at least:
- **identification**: whether the emotional state should be regulated;
- **selection**: which strategy/goal to use;
- **implementation**: executing the strategy;
- **monitoring**: evaluating progress and changing course.

Thus failure can arise even when a person knows an effective strategy:
- they may not identify the need;
- choose a poor strategy for the context;
- implement it weakly;
- stop too early or too late;
- misread feedback.

A questionnaire asking "how often do you reappraise?" cannot capture all four stages.

## 4. Regulation goal comes before strategy value

Possible goals include:
- reduce unpleasant affect;
- maintain or increase pleasant affect;
- increase an unpleasant emotion for instrumental reasons;
- reduce visible expression without changing experience;
- preserve social harmony;
- communicate boundaries;
- maintain task performance;
- learn from an event rather than feel better immediately.

Therefore **successful regulation ≠ feeling better immediately**.

Effectiveness must be judged relative to the regulation goal and downstream consequences.

## 5. Hedonic and instrumental regulation

A person may intentionally maintain or increase unpleasant affect if it supports another goal—for example, urgency, confrontation, vigilance, or social communication.

Conversely, reducing negative affect can undermine learning or action if the emotion carries useful information.

Hedonic outcome and instrumental success are separate criteria.

## 6. Strategy labels are families, not single operations

"Reappraisal", "suppression", "acceptance", "avoidance", and "distraction" each cover heterogeneous implementations.

For example:
- reappraisal can reinterpret the situation, perspective, cause, consequence, or self-relevance;
- suppression can target expression, thought, urge, or subjective experience;
- distraction can be external, internal, neutral, positive, or task-focused;
- acceptance can mean nonjudgmental allowance, willingness, or reduced control attempts;
- avoidance can involve situation selection, attentional avoidance, experiential avoidance, or behavior escape.

Do not compare broad labels without coding what participants actually did.

## 7. Cognitive reappraisal

Reappraisal changes the meaning assigned to a situation in order to alter emotion.

Common forms include:
- reinterpretation;
- distancing/perspective taking;
- positive reappraisal;
- changing perceived consequences;
- changing self-relevance.

It is not equivalent to positive thinking, factual denial, or forcing an optimistic conclusion.

A reappraisal can be accurate, uncertain, self-protective, socially strategic, or unrealistic; these differ in downstream value.

## 8. Reappraisal changes appraisal, not necessarily the world

If the external situation is controllable, changing the situation may be more useful than repeatedly changing interpretation.

Reappraisal may be useful when:
- the meaning is uncertain;
- the event cannot be changed;
- an initial appraisal is biased or incomplete;
- emotional intensity leaves enough cognitive capacity.

But it can be poorly matched when urgent external action is needed or when reinterpretation distorts important information.

**Reappraisal success is context-dependent rather than a universal virtue.**

## 9. Reappraisal effectiveness versus habitual reappraisal

Three different quantities:
1. habitual self-reported tendency to use reappraisal;
2. ability to implement reappraisal when instructed;
3. actual change in target outcome after reappraisal.

They can correlate imperfectly.

The 2026 meta-analysis on cognitive control found small associations between executive-function measures and reappraisal use/effectiveness (all notable |r| < .15), with substantial heterogeneity.

Therefore low reappraisal frequency does not directly imply executive dysfunction.

## 10. Reappraisal and executive control

Reappraisal often recruits working memory, updating, inhibition of dominant interpretations, and semantic generation.

Yet executive control explains only a small fraction of between-person differences in available meta-analytic data.

Do not reduce reappraisal to "prefrontal inhibition" or infer specific neural control deficits from self-report.

EXE covers cognitive-control mechanisms; AFF covers the affective regulation target.

## 11. Acceptance

Acceptance typically involves allowing internal experiences to occur without excessive judgment, struggle, or immediate attempts to eliminate them.

Acceptance is **not**:
- approving an external harmful situation;
- agreeing that an appraisal is true;
- passivity;
- liking the emotion;
- surrendering goals;
- refusing problem solving.

A person can accept present internal distress while changing the external situation.

## 12. Acceptance versus reappraisal

Reappraisal changes meaning; acceptance changes the stance toward the current internal experience.

Experimental evidence shows both can reduce negative emotional experience relative to passive reaction under some conditions, with different effort/neural/physiological profiles.

Do not assume one is globally superior.

The regulation goal matters:
- if distorted meaning is central, reappraisal may be useful;
- if repeated control attempts amplify struggle, acceptance may be useful;
- if the external situation itself is dangerous, neither substitutes for appropriate action.

## 13. Acceptance measurement

Acceptance scales can measure:
- nonjudgment;
- willingness;
- reduced experiential avoidance;
- mindfulness-related acceptance;
- distress tolerance.

These are not interchangeable.

Clinical and psychological-flexibility constructs overlap with but are broader than emotion-regulation acceptance. Detailed clinical intervention inference belongs to CLN/BHC.

## 14. Expressive suppression

Expressive suppression attempts to inhibit visible or audible signs of an emotion after emotional responding has begun.

It targets **expression**, not necessarily appraisal or subjective experience.

Classic studies often found that suppression reduces expression more reliably than experience and may carry physiological, memory, or social costs. However, these are average patterns with design dependence.

## 15. Suppression is not one global failure mode

Recent evidence prevents a categorical "suppression is bad" rule.

A 2025 paper using two high-powered within-subject experiments found expressive suppression could reduce reported negative emotion relative to comparison conditions.

A 2026 four-experiment program found a small but consistent impairment in verbal memory after suppressing negative emotion, while finding no consistent reappraisal-memory effect.

Thus a strategy can help one outcome and harm another.

## 16. Social consequences of suppression

In interpersonal contexts, visible emotional expression communicates needs, intentions, responsiveness, and trust.

Suppression may:
- prevent escalation;
- meet professional/display norms;
- protect privacy;
- reduce perceived responsiveness or intimacy;
- hide useful information;
- conserve or increase social cost depending on context.

A 2025 study of relationship discussions examined conditions under which suppression related to closeness/responsiveness costs.

The meaning of suppression cannot be inferred outside its social goal.

## 17. Cultural moderation

A 2025 meta-analysis included:
- 249 articles;
- 150,474 participants;
- 861 effect sizes;
- 37 countries/regions.

Associations of reappraisal and suppression with mental-health outcomes varied across cultural dimensions and sample characteristics.

This blocks both extremes:
- "suppression is equally maladaptive everywhere";
- "culture makes strategy effects arbitrary."

Culture is a moderator to test, not a post-hoc excuse.

## 18. Expression regulation versus emotion regulation

A person can successfully regulate expression while leaving experience unchanged.

Example operational outcomes:
- reduced facial action;
- reduced vocal intensity;
- unchanged subjective feeling;
- unchanged or altered physiology.

Therefore visible calm does not establish internal calm, and expression suppression can succeed at its intended target without downregulating all components.

Cross-reference AFF-01: experience, expression, perception, and physiology are separate channels.

## 19. Thought suppression is not expressive suppression

Trying not to display an emotion differs from trying not to think about a topic.

Thought suppression, experiential suppression, motor/expressive suppression, and urge suppression use different tasks and can yield different effects.

Never generalize findings from "suppression" without identifying the suppressed target.

## 20. Distraction

Distraction directs attention away from emotion-generating information toward other content.

It can rapidly reduce contact with high-intensity emotional material and may require less elaborative reinterpretation than reappraisal.

Potential tradeoffs:
- reduced short-term distress;
- reduced processing or learning about the event;
- loss of important information;
- delayed return of unresolved content;
- adaptive interruption when immediate functioning is required.

Distraction is not by definition denial.

## 21. Distraction versus reappraisal under intensity

A common hypothesis proposes greater preference for distraction at high emotional intensity and reappraisal at lower intensity.

Some controlled studies support this pattern, and 2025/2026 work links sensitivity to emotion intensity with flexible strategy selection.

However, naturalistic evidence is mixed: a 2025 dynamic-multimodal study failed to reproduce a simple intensity → distraction rule across a haunted-house/horror context, and a 2026 coordinated daily-life analysis found only a very small shift toward distraction relative to reappraisal as negative affect increased.

Therefore intensity is relevant but not a universal strategy-choice switch.

## 22. Attentional deployment is broader than distraction

Attentional deployment includes:
- distraction;
- concentration;
- attentional narrowing;
- mindful observation;
- selective attention to positive/neutral/goal-relevant features.

Older experimental meta-analysis showed that different attentional tactics within the same family can have opposite effects.

The family name is too broad for mechanism inference.

## 23. Situation selection

Situation selection changes which emotional context is entered.

Examples:
- approaching a supportive environment;
- declining an unnecessary provoking interaction;
- choosing a challenging but meaningful context;
- entering a feared situation for learning purposes.

The same behavior can be adaptive planning or avoidance depending on goals, future consequences, flexibility, and opportunity.

## 24. Situation selection versus avoidance

Avoiding a situation can reduce distress immediately but may:
- protect from genuine danger;
- preserve resources;
- prevent unnecessary escalation;
- block corrective learning;
- narrow behavior over time;
- maintain threat expectations.

Thus avoidance does not have an invariant sign.

Distinguish:
- threat avoidance with genuine external risk;
- experiential avoidance motivated primarily by not feeling;
- strategic situation selection;
- temporary disengagement;
- clinical safety behaviors.

Clinical exposure/avoidance inference is deferred to BHC/CLN.

## 25. Situation modification

Situation modification changes features of the environment after entering it.

Examples:
- asking for clarification;
- changing task structure;
- reducing sensory stimulation;
- seeking help;
- setting a boundary.

It can be more causally direct than changing interpretation when the source of emotion is externally controllable.

Do not interpret every regulatory act as internal cognitive control.

## 26. Rumination

Rumination is repetitive, often self-focused thought about distress, its causes, meanings, or consequences, with limited effective problem resolution.

It differs from:
- deliberate reflection;
- problem solving;
- planning;
- memory retrieval;
- constructive meaning making.

Frequency, duration, repetitiveness, controllability, and function matter.

## 27. Worry

Worry is typically repetitive future-oriented verbal thought about possible negative events and uncertainty.

It overlaps with but differs from rumination, which is often past/present and loss/failure focused.

Both may function as attempts to manage uncertainty or emotion while sustaining negative affect under some conditions.

Do not infer clinical disorder from use of either strategy alone.

## 28. Avoidance, rumination, and suppression in clinical comparisons

A 2026 Psychological Bulletin meta-analysis included 619 studies, 41,590 clinical participants, and 36,787 controls.

Clinical groups reported, on average:
- less acceptance, problem solving, and reappraisal;
- more avoidance, rumination, and suppression;
- broader reported emotion-regulation difficulties.

These are group-level, largely self-report differences.

They do **not** establish:
- a strategy causes a disorder;
- the same profile characterizes every individual;
- changing one questionnaire score treats the disorder;
- a strategy is categorically pathological in nonclinical contexts.

## 29. Clinical implementation efficacy is smaller and channel-dependent

A 2026 preregistered meta-analysis of instructed emotion regulation in mental disorders included 109 studies; 71 studies contributed 566 effects.

Average effects were:
- small for experiential outcomes (d ≈ -0.37);
- very small for physiology (d ≈ -0.09);
- not significant for behavioral outcomes (d ≈ -0.06).

Cognitive-change strategies showed larger experiential effects than response modulation/attentional deployment in that literature.

This reinforces AFF-01's principle: regulation outcomes differ by measurement channel.

## 30. Regulation frequency is not effectiveness

A person may frequently use a strategy because:
- it works well;
- emotional challenges are frequent/intense;
- other strategies are unavailable;
- it is habitual despite poor outcomes.

Therefore high frequency can reflect high regulation demand rather than high skill.

EMA designs should model emotional intensity and prior affect, not simply correlate strategy frequency with later mood.

## 31. 2026 daily-life strategy effectiveness test

A coordinated analysis of:
- 12 ambulatory datasets;
- 1,511 participants;
- 161,513 measurement occasions

found average associations in which acceptance, reappraisal, and social sharing predicted more favorable subsequent affect, while rumination, suppression, and to a lesser extent distraction did not.

However:
- every strategy showed substantial between-person heterogeneity;
- about 75–80% of the variation in apparent effectiveness was explained by the intensity of the state being regulated.

Interpretation:
**strategy labels alone explain far less than person–situation–state context.**

## 32. Reverse causality in daily-life strategy studies

If high-intensity negative states trigger rumination or suppression and are also harder to regulate, naive correlations can make these strategies look harmful even when some association reflects situation difficulty.

Conversely, people may choose reappraisal in easier situations, inflating apparent effectiveness.

Adjust for:
- pre-strategy affect;
- event intensity;
- time course;
- strategy selection propensity;
- person-level differences;
- concurrent strategies.

Do not read causal efficacy directly from ordinary EMA correlations.

## 33. Polyregulation

People often use multiple strategies in the same episode:
- situation change plus reappraisal;
- acceptance plus problem solving;
- distraction followed by reappraisal;
- suppression in public followed by social sharing in private.

A 2026 EMA study found higher event intensity predicted greater use of multiple strategies in emotional episodes.

Calling a person "a suppressor" or "a reappraiser" can hide sequential and combined regulation.

## 34. Strategy sequencing

The order of strategies can matter.

A fast disengagement strategy may lower intensity enough to permit later reinterpretation. Conversely, early avoidance can prevent later learning.

Measure:
- onset timing;
- strategy transitions;
- duration;
- target;
- outcome after each stage.

Single retrospective checklists are poor measures of regulation sequence.

## 35. Regulatory flexibility

Regulatory flexibility usually refers to adapting strategy use to changing:
- emotional intensity;
- controllability;
- goals;
- social demands;
- available resources;
- expected costs/benefits.

Flexibility is not simply "using many strategies" or "switching often."

A switch that mismatches the context can be maladaptive.

## 36. Strategy–situation fit

A strategy is better characterized by fit than by fixed valence.

Potential moderators:
- emotion intensity;
- controllability of the situation;
- regulation goal;
- social context;
- interpersonal consequences;
- cognitive resources;
- culture;
- time horizon;
- need for learning or memory.

A 2025 preregistered EMA reanalysis found context-sensitive use of situation selection associated with better overall affective well-being.

This supports fit in that operationalization, not a universal flexibility law.

## 37. Current challenge to simple flexibility claims

A 2026 coordinated analysis of 12 ambulatory datasets found only a very small increase in relative distraction use as negative affect intensified and no association between that context-sensitive pattern and aggregated well-being.

Therefore:
**context sensitivity exists in some measures, but its measurement and adaptive value remain unresolved.**

Do not convert "flexibility" into a universally good trait without construct-valid measures.

## 38. Expressive flexibility

Expressive flexibility refers to ability to enhance or suppress visible emotional expression according to situational demands.

A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of 29 studies found small associations with lower depressive/anxiety symptoms and preliminary association with life satisfaction, but heterogeneity was substantial/very high, prediction intervals included zero, and evidence was predominantly cross-sectional.

Thus expressive flexibility is promising but not a validated global resilience biomarker.

## 39. Regulation effort and cognitive cost

Emotion regulation itself can be effortful.

Cognitive effort can influence:
- identifying a need to regulate;
- evaluating alternatives;
- implementing reappraisal;
- sustaining attentional control;
- monitoring success.

A 2025/2026 review emphasizes interindividual variability and that high effort requirements can contribute to regulatory failure.

But "automatic regulation" is not automatically better: low effort may come with inflexibility under novel conditions.

## 40. Emotion regulation and executive function: current quantitative boundary

The 2026 meta-analysis linked updating/inhibition measures with reappraisal, rumination, and worry in theoretically expected directions, but effects were small and heterogeneous; suppression had no consistent association.

Hence:
- executive control contributes to some ER processes;
- ER is not reducible to generic executive ability;
- poor regulation cannot be inferred directly from one inhibition/shifting score.

## 41. Strategy implementation versus strategy selection

A good selection can be implemented badly.

For example:
- selecting reappraisal but generating an implausible reinterpretation;
- selecting acceptance but covertly continuing to resist sensations;
- selecting suppression but visibly leaking behavior;
- selecting distraction but repeatedly reorienting to the trigger.

Measure implementation fidelity separately from selection.

## 42. Monitoring and stopping rules

Regulation includes deciding whether to:
- continue;
- intensify;
- switch strategy;
- stop regulating;
- change the emotional goal.

Repeated monitoring itself can sometimes increase self-focus or effort.

An outcome at one time point does not identify whether monitoring was adaptive.

## 43. Emotion regulation can target positive emotions

Regulation is not only downregulation of negative emotion.

People may:
- savor or amplify positive affect;
- dampen excitement;
- suppress pride for social goals;
- increase enthusiasm;
- maintain calm positive states.

A framework built only around reducing negative affect is incomplete.

## 44. Interpersonal emotion regulation

People regulate emotions through and for other people:
- seeking support;
- sharing emotion;
- reassurance;
- perspective input;
- changing another person's state;
- co-regulation.

This creates outcomes for both people and social relationships.

Detailed interpersonal regulation belongs to SOC/REL, but AFF must not treat all regulation as isolated intrapersonal cognition.

## 45. Culture and display rules

Cultural norms affect:
- whether expression is valued;
- which emotions are socially appropriate;
- suppression costs/benefits;
- help seeking;
- social sharing;
- interpretation of control.

Cross-cultural meta-analysis demonstrates meaningful moderation of reappraisal/suppression associations.

Therefore importing a strategy ranking from one cultural setting to another requires evidence.

## 46. Regulation versus coping

Coping and emotion-regulation frameworks overlap substantially.

A 2024 scoping review found common strategies including:
- reappraisal/reinterpretation;
- problem solving;
- acceptance;
- avoidance;
- suppression.

But coping can target the external stressor, resources, or adaptation more broadly; ER specifically targets emotion generation/trajectory.

STR will handle stress/coping architecture in depth.

## 47. Measurement instruments

Common measures include:
- Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ): reappraisal and expressive suppression;
- Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS): broader perceived dysregulation dimensions;
- Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (CERQ): cognitive strategies;
- Acceptance/experiential-avoidance scales;
- laboratory instructed-regulation tasks;
- strategy choice tasks;
- ecological momentary assessment;
- observer/partner ratings;
- physiology and behavior.

They do **not** measure the same latent object.

## 48. Frequency, ability, efficacy, effort, flexibility, and repertoire

These six variables must be separated:

1. **frequency** — how often a strategy is used;
2. **ability** — capacity to execute it under suitable conditions;
3. **efficacy/effectiveness** — resulting change relative to target;
4. **effort/cost** — resources required;
5. **flexibility** — context-sensitive adaptation of use;
6. **repertoire** — number/diversity of available strategies.

A high value on one can coexist with a low value on another.

## 49. Reliability versus construct validity

A reliable self-report may repeatedly measure a person's belief about usual regulation without validly predicting:
- laboratory ability;
- momentary strategy selection;
- physiological change;
- actual social behavior.

Likewise a lab task can reliably measure instructed downregulation while having poor ecological validity.

The measurement question is always:
**reliable for what target, under what context, across what time scale?**

## 50. Regulation success has multiple endpoints

Possible endpoints:
- subjective affect;
- physiology;
- expression;
- memory;
- task performance;
- social connection;
- learning;
- goal attainment;
- recovery time;
- long-term functioning.

A strategy can improve one and worsen another.

Thus "effective regulation" must name the outcome and horizon.

## 51. Avoid outcome substitution

Examples:
- lower facial expression ≠ lower subjective emotion;
- lower reported distress ≠ better memory;
- lower arousal ≠ better social functioning;
- higher reappraisal frequency ≠ successful regulation;
- reduced immediate negative affect ≠ better long-term learning.

This is a direct extension of AFF-01's multichannel measurement rule.

## 52. Regulation goal can itself be maladaptive or mistaken

Successful control toward an unhelpful target is still technically successful regulation.

For instance, eliminating all visible emotion may meet a short-term social goal but undermine another goal such as accurate communication.

Science should distinguish:
- strategy implementation success;
- goal attainment;
- broader adaptiveness.

Do not define "adaptive" solely by immediate affect reduction.

## 53. Emotion-regulation beliefs

Beliefs that emotions are changeable are associated with regulation strategy patterns in a 2024 meta-analysis.

However beliefs can influence:
- strategy selection;
- persistence;
- expectations;
- self-report interpretation.

Association does not prove that changing a malleability belief will cause better regulation in every person/context.

## 54. Clinical associations are not causal prescriptions

The 2026 clinical meta-analysis shows large reported group differences in broad ER difficulties and strategy use across many disorders.

This supports transdiagnostic scientific relevance.

It does not justify:
- diagnosing from DERS/ERQ;
- assuming one strategy caused symptoms;
- prescribing one strategy based solely on group averages;
- inferring a universal emotion-regulation deficit mechanism.

Clinical interpretation belongs to later CLN/BHC evidence.

## 55. Neural boundary

Reappraisal, suppression, distraction, and acceptance engage distributed networks involved in attention, language, control, interoception, and valuation.

No single brain region is an emotion-regulation center.

Neural activity during instructed regulation does not uniquely establish everyday strategy use or effectiveness.

Detailed neural causal inference is deferred to NEU.

## 56. Experimental minimum controls

A strong regulation study should specify:
1. eliciting stimulus/event;
2. target emotion/affective dimension;
3. regulation goal;
4. strategy definition and training;
5. implementation fidelity;
6. baseline/no-regulation control;
7. timing of regulation;
8. subjective outcome;
9. behavioral/expression outcome if relevant;
10. physiological outcome if relevant;
11. memory/task/social outcome if claimed;
12. emotional intensity;
13. controllability/context;
14. prior strategy experience;
15. compliance;
16. individual/cultural moderators;
17. delayed consequences where claimed;
18. strategy alternatives.

No study needs all measures, but conclusions cannot exceed measured outcomes.

## 57. Daily-life/EMA minimum controls

For naturalistic data, model:
- prior affect;
- event intensity;
- strategy selection;
- concurrent strategies;
- person mean versus momentary deviation;
- time lag;
- event controllability;
- social context;
- feedback/goal;
- missingness/reactivity.

Cross-sectional momentary association is not causal effectiveness.

## 58. Emotion Regulation Inference Gate — 54 questions

### A. Target and goal
1. What emotion/affective state is targeted?
2. Is the target experience, expression, physiology, cognition, behavior, or social consequence?
3. Is the goal hedonic or instrumental?
4. Is downregulation actually desired?
5. Is the situation controllable?
6. What time horizon defines success?

### B. Regulation cycle
7. Was the need for regulation identified?
8. Was a strategy deliberately selected or habitual?
9. Was implementation fidelity measured?
10. Was success monitored?
11. Did the person switch or combine strategies?
12. Could affect change have occurred without regulation?

### C. Strategy specification
13. Is situation selection separated from avoidance?
14. Is situation modification measured?
15. Is attentional deployment specified beyond "distraction"?
16. Which type of reappraisal was used?
17. Is acceptance operationalized explicitly?
18. Is expressive suppression separated from thought/experience suppression?
19. Are rumination and worry distinguished?
20. Is social sharing/interpersonal regulation relevant?

### D. Context and fit
21. What is the starting emotion intensity?
22. Is event controllability measured?
23. Are cognitive resources and effort relevant?
24. Are social/display goals present?
25. Is culture/context considered?
26. Are short-term and long-term goals aligned?
27. Could strategy choice reflect task difficulty rather than preference?
28. Is strategy–situation fit directly tested?

### E. Outcomes
29. Is subjective experience measured?
30. Is visible expression measured separately?
31. Is physiology measured?
32. Is behavior/task performance measured?
33. Is memory measured if claimed?
34. Is social functioning measured if claimed?
35. Are delayed consequences assessed?
36. Does success on one channel conflict with another?

### F. Measurement
37. Is strategy frequency separated from ability?
38. Is ability separated from effectiveness?
39. Is effectiveness separated from effort/cost?
40. Is repertoire separated from flexibility?
41. Is questionnaire use separated from laboratory ability?
42. Is EMA intensity confounding controlled?
43. Are within-person and between-person effects separated?
44. Is instrument reliability known?
45. Is construct/ecological validity known?
46. Does strategy labeling match actual implementation?

### G. Causal and transfer limits
47. Is the evidence experimental, longitudinal, or correlational?
48. Could reverse causality explain strategy-outcome association?
49. Is a population average being converted into a universal strategy rule?
50. Is clinical group evidence being converted into diagnosis?
51. Is executive ability being treated as the whole ER mechanism?
52. Is neural activity being treated as a unique causal regulator?
53. What viable alternative strategy/process explains the outcome?
54. What is the narrowest conclusion supported by the measured target?

## 59. Failure modes

A. Reappraisal → always adaptive: **invalid**.

B. Suppression → always maladaptive: **invalid**.

C. Acceptance → passivity: **invalid construct substitution**.

D. Avoidance → always pathological: **invalid without risk/goal/time-horizon analysis**.

E. Distraction → denial: **invalid categorical inference**.

F. Lower negative affect → regulation globally successful: **outcome substitution**.

G. Expression suppressed → emotion gone: **invalid**.

H. More reappraisal use → greater reappraisal skill: **frequency/ability conflation**.

I. Strategy use → strategy caused later mood: **reverse-causality risk**.

J. More strategies → more flexibility: **invalid**.

K. Strategy switching → good fit: **invalid without contextual success evidence**.

L. High DERS → specific clinical diagnosis: **invalid diagnostic leap**.

M. Low reappraisal → weak executive control: **unsupported unique mechanism**.

N. EMA association → causal effectiveness: **invalid without temporal/confound controls**.

O. Cultural moderation → no general principles: **overcorrection**.

P. Experimental affect reduction → real-world effectiveness: **transportability assumption**.

## Canonical operating rule

**Define regulation target and goal → identify process stage → specify actual strategy/tactic → check context, intensity, controllability, culture and resources → measure implementation fidelity → evaluate multiple relevant outcomes and time horizons → distinguish frequency/ability/effectiveness/flexibility → test causal alternatives → infer only task- and goal-bounded regulation success.**

Never:
**strategy label = adaptive value; expression = experience; use frequency = skill; immediate relief = long-term adaptation; questionnaire = diagnosis; correlation = regulation mechanism.**
