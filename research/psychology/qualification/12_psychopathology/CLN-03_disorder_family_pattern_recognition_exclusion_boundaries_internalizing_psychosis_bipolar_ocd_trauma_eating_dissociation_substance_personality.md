# CLN-03 — Disorder-family pattern recognition and exclusion boundaries

Status: **COMPLETE_FOR_CURRENT_SCOPE — 20/20 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Close the remaining CLN qualification gap after CLN-01 and CLN-02:

> distinguish the high-value architecture of major psychopathology families and their exclusion boundaries without turning qualification into criterion memorization or pretending that pattern recognition is formal diagnosis.

CLN-01 established:

- symptom != syndrome != diagnosis != formulation != mechanism;
- diagnosis is a classification, not a causal explanation;
- differential diagnosis requires alternatives;
- categorical and dimensional descriptions can coexist.

CLN-02 established:

- onset, maintenance, remission and recurrence are distinct;
- risk marker != causal factor != mechanism;
- transdiagnostic != universal != causal;
- between-person and within-person relations must be separated.

CLN-03 adds:

- family-level discriminating structure;
- high-value cross-family confusion points;
- medical/substance/developmental exclusion logic;
- explicit "do not infer from one salient feature" rules;
- a generic escalation boundary for cases requiring professional/medical assessment.

This block is **not** a DSM/ICD criteria encyclopedia.

It is a pattern-and-boundary qualification block.

---

# I. Question Card

## 1. Concrete dependency

Reliable individualized psychological reasoning requires knowing when apparently similar presentations belong to different diagnostic families or should not be interpreted psychiatrically at all.

Without this layer, high-risk errors include:

- depression mistaken for bipolar disorder or vice versa;
- psychosis mistaken for schizophrenia;
- intrusive thoughts mistaken for intent or psychosis;
- OCD mistaken for generalized worry or personality style;
- trauma exposure mistaken for PTSD;
- dissociation mistaken for psychosis;
- somatic symptom disorder inferred from "normal tests";
- eating pathology missed because body weight looks ordinary;
- personality pathology inferred from a temporary episode;
- substance/medication effects mistaken for a primary mental disorder;
- developmental traits mistaken for episodic psychopathology.

## 2. Question types

- [x] diagnosis/classification;
- [x] differential inference;
- [x] guideline/manual interpretation;
- [x] measurement/assessment boundary;
- [ ] treatment-effect comparison;
- [ ] individualized diagnosis.

## 3. Target construct

High-value discriminators among major disorder families, not full diagnostic criteria.

## 4. Scientific population/context

General clinical and research psychology across adolescents/adults, with lifespan and cultural constraints retained.

## 5. Comparator / alternative explanation

For every family:

- neighboring psychiatric family;
- substance/medication effect;
- medical/neurological condition;
- sleep-related state where relevant;
- developmental pattern where relevant;
- normal/contextual reaction.

## 6. Target outcome

A calibrated generic conclusion such as:

- "presentation is more compatible with family A than B";
- "family remains unresolved";
- "medical/substance exclusion has priority";
- "professional diagnostic assessment is required";
- "evidence is insufficient for family-level inference".

Not:

- formal diagnosis;
- treatment prescription;
- person-specific label from generic pattern.

## 7. Time/course

Course and temporal relation are central discriminators:

- episodic vs persistent;
- pre-existing vs new onset;
- exposure-linked vs independent;
- state-like vs enduring;
- trauma-linked vs non-trauma-linked;
- intoxication/withdrawal-linked vs independent.

## 8. Eligible evidence

Primary classification/manual sources and current authoritative public clinical information:

- APA DSM-5-TR current resources;
- WHO ICD-11 CDDR and current ICD-11 release;
- NIMH condition resources;
- SAMHSA co-occurring-disorder guidance where substance differentiation matters;
- prior canonical CLN/PSYCHOMETRICS/DEV/PER blocks.

## 9. Applicability boundary

This block cannot establish:

- a formal diagnosis;
- a person-specific cause;
- a mechanism from category membership;
- one correct diagnosis from self-report snippets;
- exclusion of medical disease without medical assessment.

## 10. Privacy transform

No private case initiated this block.

No individualized facts are stored.

## 11. Closure criterion

The block closes when:

1. major disorder families have high-value discriminators;
2. common cross-family confusions have explicit rules;
3. medical/substance/developmental exclusions are integrated;
4. one-symptom/one-label reasoning is blocked;
5. no additional CLN block is required for reliable generic reasoning before moving to NDEV.

# II. Current classification anchors

## 11. DSM-5-TR remains the current DSM

As of the evidence cutoff:

- DSM-5-TR remains APA's latest DSM edition;
- it was published in 2022;
- APA maintains approved criteria/text updates;
- APA lists 2025 updates as current;
- APA states no ICD-10-CM code changes are planned for DSM-5-TR in 2026 and criteria/text changes are expected in early 2027.

Therefore exact diagnostic details are version-sensitive.

## 12. ICD-11 remains the current WHO classification

WHO's ICD browser lists **2026-01** as the latest ICD-11 MMS release as of the evidence cutoff.

The current clinical manual for mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders remains the **2024 ICD-11 CDDR** publication.

## 13. DSM and ICD organize families differently in places

High-value implication:

**family labels are framework-dependent organizational tools, not proof of one natural causal partition.**

For example:

- "internalizing" is useful in research/HiTOP-style organization;
- DSM/ICD use more specific clinical chapters/families;
- ICD-11 uses a notably dimensional approach for personality pathology.

Therefore:

`research spectrum != official diagnostic family != mechanism`.

# III. The five-axis differential architecture

Before comparing labels, specify five axes.

## 14. Axis A — phenomenology

What is actually happening?

Examples:

- depressed mood;
- fear/anxiety;
- intrusive unwanted thought;
- fixed belief;
- hallucination;
- dissociation;
- binge eating;
- restriction;
- compulsive behavior;
- identity/memory disruption.

Do not infer family from the ordinary-language word alone.

## 15. Axis B — function of the symptom/behavior

Ask what the behavior appears to do.

Examples:

- reduce obsession-triggered anxiety;
- escape social evaluation;
- avoid trauma reminders;
- control weight/shape;
- regulate intoxication/withdrawal;
- maintain rigid self/relationship patterns.

Same outward behavior can have different functions.

## 16. Axis C — time/course

Ask:

- episodic or longstanding?
- abrupt or gradual onset?
- linked to intoxication/withdrawal?
- linked to trauma?
- continuous across contexts?
- returned to baseline between episodes?
- preceded by developmental traits?

## 17. Axis D — reality testing / insight

Ask separately:

- Is the experience recognized as internal/unusual?
- Is belief held with delusional conviction?
- Is there preserved reality testing despite unreality/detachment?
- Does insight vary across time or severity?

Insight is a dimension, not a binary universal discriminator.

## 18. Axis E — exclusion priority

Before psychiatric interpretation ask whether the presentation could be materially explained by:

- medical/neurological condition;
- delirium;
- substance intoxication/withdrawal;
- medication;
- sleep deprivation;
- developmental condition;
- acute contextual reaction.

These axes usually discriminate more reliably than one "signature symptom."

# IV. Internalizing architecture

## 19. "Internalizing" is a research umbrella

Internalizing broadly captures covariation among:

- depression;
- anxiety/fear;
- some trauma-related and obsessive-compulsive presentations depending on model.

It is useful for dimensional organization.

It is not one formal diagnosis.

## 20. Anxiety/fear-related core

High-value pattern:

- fear/anxiety is central;
- anticipatory threat/worry and avoidance are prominent;
- the feared object/context differs across disorders.

Current NIMH material distinguishes:

- generalized worry across everyday domains;
- panic episodes;
- social-evaluative fear;
- phobic fear.

## 21. Anxiety versus normal stress

Normal stress/anxiety:

- follows ordinary demands;
- can be transient;
- need not impair functioning.

Anxiety-disorder inference requires more than temporary worry/fear.

## 22. Generalized anxiety versus OCD

GAD-style worry tends to concern ordinary life domains and uncertain future outcomes.

OCD-style obsessions are characteristically:

- intrusive;
- unwanted;
- recurring;
- often followed by compulsions/rituals or mental acts.

But content can overlap.

Therefore distinguish:

- **form/function of cognition**;
- not just topic.

## 23. Anxiety versus trauma-related disorder

Fear/hyperarousal alone does not establish trauma-related disorder.

Trauma-related inference requires a qualifying relation to a traumatic/stress event under the relevant classification.

A person can have severe anxiety without PTSD.

## 24. Depression versus anxiety

Overlap includes:

- sleep problems;
- concentration difficulty;
- irritability;
- fatigue;
- negative affect.

Discriminators include:

- dominant depressed mood/anhedonia versus threat/worry;
- course;
- triggers;
- activity/avoidance pattern.

Comorbidity remains possible.

# V. Depressive versus bipolar-spectrum architecture

## 25. Depression does not imply unipolar disorder

A current depressive presentation can occur in:

- major depressive disorder;
- bipolar disorder;
- trauma-related disorders;
- substance/medication effects;
- medical conditions;
- other syndromes.

Therefore:

`depressive symptoms -> unipolar depression`

is invalid.

## 26. Bipolar inference is course-based

NIMH explicitly emphasizes that bipolar diagnosis depends on:

- severity;
- duration;
- frequency;
- lifetime course;
- history of manic/hypomanic episodes.

A current depressive state alone is insufficient.

## 27. High-value bipolar discriminator

The strongest family-level clue is not "mood swings."

It is a **distinct episode** involving a marked change from baseline in:

- mood;
- energy/activity;
- sleep need;
- thought/speech speed;
- goal-directed/impulsive behavior.

## 28. Reduced sleep versus decreased need for sleep

Insomnia:

- wants/needs sleep but cannot obtain it;
- often produces fatigue.

Manic/hypomanic decreased need for sleep:

- markedly less sleep;
- subjectively less need/tiredness in episode context.

This distinction is useful but not sufficient alone.

## 29. Mood reactivity is not mania

Brief emotional shifts after interpersonal events do not by themselves establish bipolar episodes.

Episode structure, duration, activity/energy change and course matter.

## 30. Psychosis does not decide bipolar versus schizophrenia

Severe bipolar episodes can include psychotic symptoms.

Severe depression can include psychotic symptoms.

Therefore psychosis must be interpreted relative to:

- mood episode timing;
- longitudinal course;
- independent psychotic periods;
- medical/substance alternatives.

## 31. Medical mimics matter

Current NIMH bipolar guidance explicitly notes:

- thyroid disease can produce similar symptoms;
- prescribed/recreational/illicit drugs can mimic or worsen mood symptoms.

Therefore a first apparent manic episode is not a purely psychological pattern-recognition problem.

# VI. Psychosis architecture

## 32. Psychosis is a symptom domain, not schizophrenia

NIMH defines psychosis as loss/disruption of contact with reality involving phenomena such as:

- delusions;
- hallucinations;
- disorganized thinking/speech/behavior.

Psychosis can occur:

- in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders;
- bipolar disorder;
- severe depression;
- medical/neurological disease;
- substance/medication states;
- severe sleep deprivation.

## 33. Psychotic symptom != primary psychotic disorder

Before inferring schizophrenia-spectrum family, ask:

1. Is the experience actually psychotic?
2. Does it occur exclusively in a mood episode?
3. Is there a substance/medication temporal relation?
4. Is there a medical/neurological explanation?
5. Is there delirium?
6. Is it dissociative rather than psychotic?
7. What is the longitudinal course?

## 34. Hallucination-like experiences require phenomenology

Ask:

- modality;
- internal/external quality;
- degree of control;
- conviction;
- insight;
- context;
- sleep transition;
- substance use;
- dissociative context;
- trauma context.

The word "voice" alone cannot classify the experience.

## 35. Delusion-like beliefs require context

Strong/unusual belief is not automatically delusion.

Assess:

- conviction;
- revisability;
- evidence standards;
- cultural/religious context;
- shared belief system;
- functional impact;
- mood congruence;
- reality-testing changes.

## 36. Negative symptoms versus depression

Overlap can include:

- low motivation;
- reduced pleasure;
- social withdrawal;
- low expression;
- low activity.

Therefore:

`withdrawal + flatness -> schizophrenia`

is invalid.

Need course, positive psychotic symptoms, mood syndrome, medication effects and functional history.

## 37. Cognitive symptoms are nonspecific

Attention/memory/executive problems occur across:

- schizophrenia;
- depression;
- bipolar disorder;
- ADHD;
- sleep deprivation;
- substance effects;
- medical conditions.

Cognitive impairment cannot classify psychosis family by itself.

---

# VII. Dissociation versus psychosis

## 38. Dissociation is not psychosis

Dissociative disorders involve disruptions in:

- memory;
- identity;
- perception;
- sense of self;
- continuity of experience.

## 39. Depersonalization/derealization has a key boundary

APA's current public description emphasizes that in depersonalization/derealization:

- experiences can feel unreal/detached;
- **reality awareness is retained**.

This is a major differential clue versus psychotic loss of reality testing.

## 40. Preserved insight is not universal across all dissociation

Do not generalize the depersonalization/derealization rule to every dissociative presentation.

Dissociation is heterogeneous.

## 41. Dissociative voices can be confused with psychosis

APA explicitly notes dissociative identity disorder can be mistaken for schizophrenia, including because dissociated identities may be experienced as auditory voices.

Therefore voice-hearing must be phenotyped, not automatically classified.

## 42. Memory gaps matter

Clinically meaningful amnesia/identity discontinuity can be more informative for dissociative-family hypotheses than generic "feeling detached."

But neurological/substance causes of amnesia must be excluded.

## 43. Trauma association is not a one-step diagnostic proof

Dissociative disorders and PTSD can be trauma-associated.

But:

`trauma history -> dissociative disorder`

is invalid.

Trauma exposure is neither necessary nor sufficient for every dissociative inference.

---

# VIII. OCD-related architecture

## 44. OCD core structure

Current NIMH description:

- obsessions = intrusive/unwanted recurring thoughts, urges or images;
- compulsions = repetitive behaviors/mental acts often performed in response;
- symptoms are time-consuming/distressing/impairing.

## 45. Intrusive thought != intent

Unwanted aggressive/sexual/harm thoughts can occur as obsessions.

Therefore:

`intrusive thought content -> desire/intent`

is a severe inference error.

Assess:

- ego-dystonicity;
- fear of the thought;
- avoidance/reassurance;
- compulsive neutralization;
- actual intent/planning;
- insight.

Safety assessment remains separate.

## 46. Obsession versus delusion

Useful dimensions:

- intrusiveness;
- unwantedness;
- resistance;
- insight;
- conviction;
- neutralizing rituals.

But OCD can have poor insight.

Therefore insight alone cannot fully separate OCD from psychosis.

## 47. Compulsion versus habit

Repetition alone is insufficient.

Compulsion is commonly linked to:

- obsession;
- feared consequence;
- rule/urge;
- temporary anxiety reduction.

Habit/routine may be automatic without obsessional threat.

## 48. OCD versus GAD

Both can involve repetitive cognition.

High-value distinction:

- GAD: broad worry about plausible life concerns;
- OCD: intrusive/ego-dystonic obsessional form plus neutralization/compulsion more typical.

Content can overlap.

## 49. OCD versus OCPD

APA explicitly distinguishes obsessive-compulsive personality disorder from OCD.

OCPD concerns an enduring pattern of:

- perfectionism;
- order/control;
- rigidity/inflexibility.

OCD concerns obsessions/compulsions.

"Perfectionism" alone does not establish either.

---

# IX. Trauma- and stressor-related architecture

## 50. Trauma exposure != PTSD

NIMH notes most people exposed to traumatic events do **not** develop PTSD.

Therefore:

`trauma happened -> PTSD`

is invalid.

## 51. PTSD architecture

Core family-level pattern includes a qualifying traumatic exposure plus a syndrome involving domains such as:

- re-experiencing/intrusion;
- avoidance;
- negative cognition/mood changes;
- arousal/reactivity.

Exact criteria remain classification/version dependent.

## 52. Acute post-trauma reaction versus PTSD

Many people have early symptoms after trauma.

Current APA public guidance distinguishes acute stress disorder from PTSD partly by time course.

Therefore early distress is not automatically PTSD.

## 53. PTSD versus depression

Overlap:

- withdrawal;
- low mood;
- guilt;
- sleep problems;
- concentration problems;
- anhedonia.

PTSD becomes more likely when trauma-linked:

- intrusions;
- cue-triggered distress;
- avoidance;
- hyperarousal

organize the syndrome.

## 54. PTSD versus panic/anxiety

Panic-like physiological surges can occur in PTSD.

Distinguish:

- trauma cue structure;
- re-experiencing;
- avoidance;
- broader panic pattern.

## 55. PTSD and dissociation can coexist

APA notes depersonalization/derealization can occur in PTSD.

Therefore dissociation does not automatically displace PTSD.

## 56. Complex PTSD is classification-specific

ICD-11 CDDR includes complex PTSD as a distinct category.

DSM-5-TR does not organize the system identically.

Therefore:

`CPTSD label -> same formal status across DSM and ICD`

is false.

## 57. Adjustment disorder is not "weak PTSD"

A stressor-related presentation can be clinically significant without meeting trauma-based PTSD architecture.

Do not force all stress-linked impairment into PTSD.

---

# X. Eating-disorder architecture

## 58. Eating disorder cannot be inferred from weight alone

Current NIMH guidance emphasizes eating disorders occur across:

- ages;
- sexes;
- racial/ethnic backgrounds;
- body weights.

Therefore:

`normal/high body weight -> no eating disorder`

is invalid.

## 59. High-value discriminator: behavioral pattern + function

Differentiate:

- restriction;
- binge eating/loss of control;
- compensatory behaviors;
- weight/shape fear/overvaluation;
- sensory/aversive consequences;
- appetite/interest.

## 60. Anorexia-family pattern

NIMH emphasizes severe restriction plus:

- intense fear of weight gain;
- pursuit of thinness/body-image distortion in typical anorexia presentations.

Medical consequences can be severe.

## 61. Bulimia versus binge-eating disorder

Both include recurrent binge/loss-of-control patterns.

Bulimia additionally includes recurrent compensatory behaviors aimed at preventing weight gain.

Therefore binge eating alone does not establish bulimia.

## 62. ARFID is not simply anorexia without low weight

NIMH describes ARFID restriction driven by mechanisms such as:

- fear of consequences like choking/vomiting;
- sensory characteristics;
- low interest/appetite.

This differs from classic weight/shape-driven restriction.

## 63. Eating pathology versus OCD

Food rituals, checking and rigidity can occur in both.

Ask:

- Is the behavior organized around weight/shape/eating consequences?
- Is it driven by intrusive obsessional threat?
- Is there loss of control/bingeing?
- Are compensatory behaviors present?

Comorbidity remains possible.

## 64. Eating disorders carry medical risk

Restriction, purging and severe nutritional disturbance can produce:

- electrolyte problems;
- cardiovascular consequences;
- malnutrition;
- organ complications.

Therefore severe eating symptoms are not purely psychological assessment questions.

---

# XI. Somatic symptom and health-anxiety architecture

## 65. Somatic symptom disorder is not "medically unexplained symptoms"

APA's current guidance explicitly states:

- physical symptoms may or may not have an identified medical condition;
- diagnosis is not made solely because no medical cause is found;
- the focus is excessive thoughts/feelings/behaviors related to symptoms and impairment/distress.

## 66. Normal test != psychological cause

Invalid inference:

`tests normal -> symptom is psychological`.

Possible alternatives include:

- undetected medical condition;
- functional disorder;
- fluctuating disease;
- measurement limitation;
- somatic symptom disorder;
- anxiety;
- multiple processes.

## 67. Medical illness and somatic symptom disorder can coexist

Presence of a real medical condition does not automatically exclude clinically excessive health-related cognition/behavior.

Conversely, psychiatric history does not justify ignoring new medical symptoms.

## 68. Illness anxiety differs from somatic symptom emphasis

Current APA description distinguishes health anxiety with relatively little somatic symptom burden from somatic symptom disorder focused on distressing physical symptoms and related responses.

## 69. Safety principle

New, changing, severe, focal or otherwise medically concerning physical symptoms require medical evaluation rather than psychological reinterpretation.

---

# XII. Substance-related architecture

## 70. Three separate questions

For a presentation involving substance exposure, ask separately:

1. Is there a substance use disorder?
2. Is there intoxication/withdrawal?
3. Are psychiatric symptoms substance/medication-induced or independent?

These are not interchangeable.

## 71. Co-occurrence != substance-induced

SAMHSA defines co-occurring disorders as the coexistence of mental disorder and SUD.

A primary mental disorder can coexist with SUD.

Therefore:

`substance use present -> psychiatric symptoms are substance-induced`

is invalid.

## 72. Temporal relation is high value

For possible substance/medication-induced symptoms, inspect:

- onset relative to exposure;
- dose/change;
- intoxication;
- withdrawal;
- sleep loss;
- persistence after acute effects;
- prior independent episodes;
- recurrence without substance.

## 73. Psychosis requires substance/medication check

NIMH explicitly lists:

- prescription medications;
- alcohol/drug misuse;
- sleep deprivation;
- neurological/medical disease

as possible psychosis causes.

Therefore first-episode psychosis requires exclusion logic before primary psychotic-disorder inference.

## 74. Mood symptoms require exposure check

NIMH bipolar and depression guidance also notes medical conditions/medications/substances can mimic or contribute to mood symptoms.

## 75. Treatment response cannot retroactively classify cause

Improvement after stopping a substance or taking medication can support a temporal hypothesis.

It does not prove one unique mechanism if other changes occurred simultaneously.

---

# XIII. Personality-pathology architecture

## 76. Personality pathology is pattern-based

APA describes personality disorders as long-standing patterns of:

- thinking;
- emotional response;
- relating;
- behavior/control

that are rigid/inflexible and impairing.

## 77. One episode cannot establish personality disorder

A transient state during:

- depression;
- mania;
- trauma crisis;
- intoxication;
- acute stress

cannot by itself establish enduring personality pathology.

## 78. Cross-context persistence matters

Strong personality-pathology inference requires evidence that the pattern:

- persists over time;
- appears across relevant contexts;
- is not better explained by a temporary episode;
- is developmentally coherent.

## 79. Personality trait != personality disorder

Extreme trait does not automatically establish disorder.

Need dysfunction/impairment and pattern-level evidence.

## 80. DSM and ICD personality models differ

APA DSM-5-TR retains specific personality disorder categories in its main classification.

WHO ICD-11 CDDR explicitly incorporates a more dimensional personality approach.

Therefore:

`one personality label -> same construct across DSM and ICD`

is unsafe.

## 81. OCPD versus OCD

OCPD:

- enduring perfectionistic/control/rigidity pattern.

OCD:

- obsession/compulsion syndrome.

Do not infer one from the other.

## 82. Borderline-like features versus bipolar episodes

Shared surface features can include:

- affective instability;
- impulsivity;
- relationship disruption.

High-value distinctions include:

- episode structure;
- duration/course;
- baseline personality pattern;
- trigger relationship;
- energy/activity/sleep changes.

No one feature settles the differential.

## 83. Personality pathology versus trauma adaptation

Longstanding trauma-related adaptations can resemble personality features.

Need developmental course and syndrome structure.

Do not equate a coping adaptation with fixed identity.

# XIV. Medical and neurological exclusion architecture

## 84. Psychiatric-looking symptoms can be medical

High-value examples already supported by current official sources include:

- thyroid disease mimicking mood symptoms;
- neurological disorders causing psychosis;
- medication effects;
- sleep deprivation;
- intoxication/withdrawal.

## 85. New onset changes prior probability

Abrupt new-onset:

- psychosis;
- severe personality change;
- cognitive decline;
- unusual perceptual change;
- marked agitation/confusion

should increase attention to medical/neurological/substance causes.

## 86. Delirium is a different problem

Fluctuating attention/awareness and acute confusion require medical assessment.

Do not interpret delirium as personality, psychosis family, or ordinary anxiety.

## 87. Neurological symptoms matter

New:

- seizures;
- focal deficits;
- major cognitive change;
- altered consciousness;
- severe headache with neuropsychiatric change

are medical/neurological routing signals, not psychology-only problems.

This block does not define medical diagnosis.

## 88. Sleep deprivation can mimic/amplify psychiatric features

Severe sleep loss can contribute to:

- mood lability;
- cognitive impairment;
- perceptual/psychotic-like symptoms.

Therefore sleep history belongs in differential assessment.

---

# XV. Developmental exclusion architecture

## 89. Developmental pattern versus acquired episode

High-value question:

> Was the pattern present from childhood/development and relatively stable, or is it a new episodic change from baseline?

This helps route:

- ADHD/autism/neurodevelopmental hypotheses;
- acquired mood/psychotic/trauma states.

## 90. Neurodevelopmental conditions can coexist with psychopathology

A developmental condition does not exclude:

- depression;
- anxiety;
- bipolar disorder;
- trauma disorder;
- SUD;
- eating disorder.

## 91. Masking/compensation complicates retrospective history

Absence of obvious childhood recognition does not automatically mean absence of developmental traits.

This belongs to NDEV for detailed treatment.

## 92. ADHD-like activation versus hypomania/mania

Surface overlap may include:

- distractibility;
- talkativeness;
- impulsivity;
- activity.

Key generic discriminator:

- developmental baseline and chronicity versus discrete episode/change from baseline.

Detailed differential belongs to NDEV-01.

# XVI. Cross-family confusion matrix

## 93. Low mood

Possible:

- depressive disorder;
- bipolar depressive episode;
- trauma-related disorder;
- SUD/substance-induced state;
- medical condition;
- grief/contextual reaction;
- personality-related crisis.

Need course and context.

## 94. Anxiety

Possible:

- anxiety disorder;
- OCD;
- PTSD;
- substance/medication effect;
- mood disorder;
- medical condition;
- eating disorder;
- psychosis-related distress.

Need object/function/temporal structure.

## 95. Racing thoughts / high activity

Possible:

- mania/hypomania;
- anxiety;
- ADHD;
- stimulant effect;
- sleep deprivation;
- stress.

Need episode structure and baseline.

## 96. "Voices"

Possible:

- psychosis;
- dissociative phenomena;
- trauma-related intrusions;
- sleep-transition phenomena;
- substance effect;
- neurological condition.

Need phenomenology.

## 97. Repetitive behavior

Possible:

- compulsion;
- habit;
- tic;
- stereotypy;
- eating-disorder ritual;
- personality style;
- self-soothing.

Need function and antecedent.

## 98. Avoidance

Possible:

- phobia/anxiety;
- PTSD;
- OCD;
- eating pathology;
- depression;
- interpersonal/personality pattern.

Avoidance is a transdiagnostic behavior, not diagnosis.

## 99. Emotional instability

Possible:

- bipolar episode;
- personality pattern;
- trauma response;
- ADHD-related dysregulation;
- substance effect;
- situational reaction.

"Emotionally unstable" is not a diagnosis.

## 100. Somatic complaint

Possible:

- medical condition;
- medication effect;
- anxiety/panic;
- somatic symptom disorder;
- depression;
- trauma-related arousal;
- substance withdrawal.

Medical exclusion remains primary where appropriate.

# XVII. Differential ordering

## 101. Step 1 — describe, do not label

Record:

- exact experience;
- source;
- onset;
- duration;
- frequency;
- severity;
- impairment.

## 102. Step 2 — identify safety/medical priority

Ask whether immediate medical/safety evaluation is needed.

If yes:

**routing overrides fine-grained psychological classification.**

## 103. Step 3 — substances/medications/sleep

Map temporal relation.

## 104. Step 4 — episode architecture

Look for:

- manic/hypomanic episode;
- depressive episode;
- psychotic episode;
- trauma-linked syndrome;
- intoxication/withdrawal state.

## 105. Step 5 — developmental/trait baseline

Determine whether pattern is longstanding versus new.

## 106. Step 6 — family-specific function

Examples:

- threat worry;
- obsession-neutralization;
- trauma intrusion/avoidance;
- weight/shape control;
- identity/memory discontinuity.

## 107. Step 7 — co-occurrence

Do not force one diagnosis if multiple independent syndromes are supported.

## 108. Step 8 — uncertainty

If discriminating evidence is missing:

- keep differential open;
- specify what evidence is needed;
- do not "choose the closest label."

# XVIII. Pattern recognition is not diagnosis

## 109. Family-level compatibility is weaker than diagnosis

Allowed generic statement:

`features are more compatible with an anxiety/OCD/trauma/psychotic family pattern`.

Stronger diagnostic language requires:

- complete assessment;
- exclusion review;
- criterion-level evaluation;
- professional judgment where applicable.

## 110. Salient features create anchoring risk

Examples:

- "mood swings" -> bipolar;
- "voices" -> schizophrenia;
- "ritual" -> OCD;
- "trauma" -> PTSD;
- "physical symptoms + normal tests" -> somatic symptom disorder;
- "rigidity" -> personality disorder.

All are invalid shortcuts.

## 111. Family classification does not identify mechanism

Even if family is correct:

`diagnostic family -> causal mechanism`

remains invalid.

CLN-02 continues to govern mechanism claims.

# XIX. High-value failure modes

## Failure A — psychosis = schizophrenia

Incorrect.

Psychosis is a symptom domain with multiple psychiatric, medical, substance and sleep-related causes.

## Failure B — depression = unipolar depression

Incorrect.

Bipolar depression and several other conditions can present depressively.

## Failure C — mood swings = bipolar

Incorrect.

Episode architecture and change from baseline matter.

## Failure D — less sleep = mania

Incorrect.

Differentiate insomnia from decreased need for sleep within a broader episode.

## Failure E — intrusive harm thought = intent

Incorrect.

OCD-type unwanted intrusive cognition must be distinguished from intention/planning.

## Failure F — poor insight = psychosis

Incorrect.

Insight varies in OCD and other disorders.

## Failure G — voice = hallucination = schizophrenia

Incorrect.

Phenomenology and differential causes matter.

## Failure H — trauma = PTSD

Incorrect.

Most trauma-exposed people do not develop PTSD.

## Failure I — dissociation = psychosis

Incorrect.

Depersonalization/derealization can preserve reality testing.

## Failure J — normal tests = somatic symptom disorder

Incorrect.

Somatic symptom disorder is not simply unexplained symptoms.

## Failure K — real medical illness excludes somatic symptom disorder

Incorrect.

They can coexist.

## Failure L — body weight excludes eating disorder

Incorrect.

Eating disorders occur across body weights.

## Failure M — binge eating = bulimia

Incorrect.

Bulimia requires the compensatory pattern; binge-eating disorder differs.

## Failure N — restriction = anorexia

Incorrect.

ARFID and medical conditions can also produce restriction.

## Failure O — substance use present = substance-induced disorder

Incorrect.

Independent mental disorder and SUD can coexist.

## Failure P — no substance use = no medication effect

Incorrect.

Prescribed medications can produce psychiatric symptoms.

## Failure Q — rigid perfectionism = OCD

Incorrect.

Could reflect OCPD/personality style, anxiety, eating pathology, autism, or context.

## Failure R — temporary crisis = personality disorder

Incorrect.

Personality pathology requires enduring pattern evidence.

## Failure S — personality label = fixed essence

Incorrect.

Classification is not identity or immutable mechanism.

## Failure T — one diagnosis must explain everything

Incorrect.

Comorbidity and layered explanations are common.

## Failure U — developmental trait = acquired psychiatric episode

Incorrect.

Baseline and developmental course matter.

## Failure V — new psychiatric-looking symptom = psychology-only problem

Incorrect.

Medical/neurological/substance causes may have priority.

## Failure W — classifier certainty = mechanism certainty

Incorrect.

They are separate inference layers.

# XXII. Evidence-strength map

## High-confidence family rules

1. psychosis != schizophrenia;
2. depressive presentation != unipolar disorder;
3. bipolar-family inference requires longitudinal episode architecture;
4. medical/substance effects must be considered in mood/psychotic presentations;
5. OCD is characterized by obsession/compulsion structure, not simple repetition;
6. intrusive thought content does not equal intent;
7. trauma exposure != PTSD;
8. dissociation != psychosis;
9. depersonalization/derealization can preserve reality testing;
10. eating disorder cannot be excluded by ordinary/high body weight;
11. binge-eating disorder and bulimia differ by compensatory behavior pattern;
12. ARFID differs from classic weight/shape-driven restriction;
13. somatic symptom disorder is not diagnosed simply because tests are normal;
14. medical disease and somatic symptom disorder can coexist;
15. substance-induced and independent mental disorders must be distinguished temporally;
16. personality pathology requires enduring pattern-level evidence;
17. one temporary episode cannot establish personality disorder;
18. DSM and ICD personality organization differs;
19. developmental baseline versus acquired episode is a major differential axis;
20. pattern recognition remains weaker than formal diagnosis.

## Useful but non-unique discriminators

1. insight;
2. mood congruence of psychosis;
3. trigger relation;
4. avoidance pattern;
5. sleep pattern;
6. age of onset;
7. family history;
8. body weight;
9. treatment response.

None should be treated as single-feature proof.

# XXV. Canonical working rule

**Classify the architecture of the presentation before naming the family.**

Use:

`phenomenology -> function -> time/course -> reality/insight -> medical/substance/developmental exclusions -> competing families -> co-occurrence -> uncertainty`.

Never:

`mood swings -> bipolar`

`voices -> schizophrenia`

`intrusive thought -> intent`

`ritual -> OCD`

`trauma -> PTSD`

`unreality -> psychosis`

`normal tests -> psychological cause`

`body weight -> eating-disorder exclusion`

`substance use -> substance-induced`

`rigidity -> personality disorder`

`one salient feature -> diagnosis`.
