## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a physiological model of skeletal-muscle force generation that is sufficient to support later work on:

- fiber phenotype;
- contraction modes;
- acute force decline and fatigue;
- hypertrophy and strength physiology;
- muscle damage/recovery;
- exercise technique and programming.

The model must explain the chain:

**muscle-fiber excitation → T-tubule voltage sensing → SR Ca²⁺ release → thin-filament activation → actomyosin cycling → force → Ca²⁺ reuptake/relaxation**

without making any of the following errors:

1. treating actin/myosin filaments as if they themselves shorten;
2. treating extracellular Ca²⁺ influx as the normal trigger in the same way as cardiac muscle;
3. reducing the triad to only CaV1.1 and RyR1 while ignoring required/auxiliary proteins;
4. treating the troponin–tropomyosin switch as perfectly binary;
5. presenting one exact molecular substep of the cross-bridge cycle as fully settled when force-generation timing remains actively investigated;
6. treating titin as merely an inert rope or, conversely, as an already proven replacement for the cross-bridge model;
7. treating twitch summation/tetanus as simple arithmetic addition;
8. treating satellite cells, myonuclei, mitochondria, ECM and capillaries as the direct contractile motor.

### Question type

Mechanism / structural-function foundation.

### Critical output

A bounded, evidence-linked mechanism sufficient to interpret later training evidence.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 28 exact Section 2–3 items must be covered at content level, major current uncertainties must be explicit, and no project-relevant content blocker should remain before a separate closure audit/synthesis.

## 3. Structural hierarchy of skeletal muscle cell

### 3.1 Muscle fiber

A skeletal muscle fiber is a large multinucleated cell.

For current project use:

**muscle → fascicles → muscle fibers → myofibrils → sarcomeres → interacting thick/thin filament systems**

The exact connective-tissue wrapping hierarchy was already handled in Anatomy; here the focus is intracellular function.

### 3.2 Myofibril

Myofibrils are longitudinal contractile assemblies packed inside the fiber. Their repeating structural units are sarcomeres.

Important distinction:

**a myofibril is not a single protein filament.**

It contains serial sarcomeres built from organized thick/thin filaments and associated structural/regulatory proteins.

### 3.3 Sarcomere

The sarcomere is the repeating contractile unit between Z-discs.

Core architecture:

- thin filaments are actin-based;
- thick filaments are myosin-based;
- titin spans a major longitudinal structural path from Z-disc toward the M-line;
- force arises from coordinated filament interaction and transmission through the sarcomeric/cytoskeletal system.

During ordinary active shortening, the dominant classic mechanism is **sliding of thick and thin filaments relative to each other**, not meaningful shortening of actin and myosin filaments themselves.

### 3.4 Actin

Actin is the principal structural backbone of the thin filament.

Its interaction sites for myosin are regulated by the troponin–tropomyosin system rather than being constitutively fully available.

### 3.5 Myosin

Myosin forms the thick filament and contains ATPase motor domains capable of cyclic actin interaction.

Project-level rule:

**myosin converts chemical free energy from ATP-linked cycling into force-producing molecular transitions; the exact microscopic timing of force generation relative to Pi release is more nuanced than a single textbook arrow.**

### 3.6 Titin

Titin is a giant sarcomeric protein spanning from the Z-disc toward the M-line and contributes strongly to:

- sarcomere organization;
- passive elasticity/stiffness;
- structural integrity;
- force transmission/mechanosensing.

Current evidence also supports active, length- and activation-dependent roles beyond a purely passive spring.

Boundary:

**the project will not treat the magnitude/mechanism of titin's contribution to active force and residual force enhancement as fully settled.**

### 3.7 Z-disc

Z-discs define sarcomere boundaries and anchor/organize thin-filament and titin-related structures.

They are mechanical/structural integration sites, not merely visual stripes.

## 4. Sarcolemma, T-tubules, SR and triad

### 4.1 Sarcolemma

The sarcolemma is the excitable muscle-fiber plasma membrane.

An action potential propagates along it after successful neuromuscular transmission.

Detailed NMJ generation/recruitment belongs to NEU; PHY-02 begins from the muscle-fiber electrical event.

### 4.2 T-tubules

T-tubules are invaginations/continuations of the sarcolemmal membrane that carry depolarization deep into the large muscle fiber.

This architecture reduces the distance between membrane excitation and internal Ca²⁺ release machinery.

### 4.3 Sarcoplasmic reticulum

The SR is the principal specialized intracellular Ca²⁺ store for rapid contraction–relaxation cycling.

Its terminal regions are closely apposed to T-tubules at triadic Ca²⁺ release units.

### 4.4 Triad / excitation-contraction unit

Current model:

1. action potential depolarizes T-tubular membrane;
2. CaV1.1/DHPR functions as a voltage sensor;
3. conformational/mechanical coupling to RyR1 promotes rapid SR Ca²⁺ release;
4. cytosolic Ca²⁺ rises near the myofilaments;
5. thin-filament regulatory proteins change state;
6. actomyosin cycling and force rise.

Current structural work directly supports physical organization of DHPR/CaV1.1 and RyR1 in native triads.

Important update:

The functional release unit is not just two proteins. Current reviews identify required/core and modulatory components including CaV1.1 subunits, RyR1, STAC3, junctophilins and multiple regulatory/luminal proteins.

### 4.5 Skeletal vs cardiac boundary

In normal adult skeletal muscle, excitation-contraction coupling is dominated by voltage-controlled conformational coupling between the T-tubular voltage sensor and RyR1.

A 2025 RyR1 study supports the conclusion that RyR1-mediated Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release contributes negligibly to normal skeletal-muscle ECC.

Project rule:

**do not import the cardiac “trigger Ca²⁺ influx → CICR” model wholesale into skeletal muscle.**

## 5. Calcium as the activation signal

### 5.1 Release

RyR1 opening rapidly raises myoplasmic Ca²⁺.

Ca²⁺ is therefore a key coupling signal between membrane excitation and contractile-protein activation.

### 5.2 Troponin / tropomyosin regulation

Ca²⁺ binding to troponin C shifts the regulatory state of the thin filament and moves tropomyosin away from strongly inhibitory positioning.

The modern structural model is not a perfect binary cover/uncover switch.

Thin-filament activation is better represented as a cooperative transition among regulatory states influenced by:

- Ca²⁺ binding;
- troponin;
- tropomyosin position;
- myosin binding.

Project simplification:

**Ca²⁺ makes productive actin–myosin interaction much more probable; strong myosin binding can further stabilize the active thin-filament state.**

## 6. Cross-bridge / actomyosin cycle

A useful bounded cycle is:

1. ATP binding to myosin promotes detachment from actin.
2. ATP hydrolysis primes the myosin motor into an ADP + Pi-associated pre-force configuration.
3. When thin-filament regulation allows it, myosin can bind actin.
4. Structural transitions convert the bound state into force/displacement.
5. Pi and then ADP-associated transitions complete the strongly bound portion of the cycle.
6. New ATP permits detachment and repetition.

Two critical corrections:

### 6.1 ATP is not only “fuel for contraction”

ATP is required both for continued cycling and for detachment of myosin from actin.

Thus the contraction-relaxation system is not “energy used only to pull.”

### 6.2 Exact force-generating substep is not fully settled

Current mechanistic work continues to refine whether force develops before, during or around Pi-release-associated transitions and how thick-filament state regulates motor availability.

Project rule:

**retain the cross-bridge cycle as the core active-force model while marking its molecular substeps as a refined, still-active research problem.**

## 7. ATPase activity

At minimum two ATP-consuming processes matter directly for the contraction-relaxation loop:

1. **myosin ATPase** — powers actomyosin cycling;
2. **SERCA Ca²⁺-ATPase** — pumps Ca²⁺ back into the SR.

Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and other transport systems support excitability/homeostasis but are outside the narrow contractile motor itself.

Detailed energetic accounting belongs to BIO.

## 8. Relaxation

Relaxation is an active physiological process, not simply “stopping stimulation.”

Core sequence:

1. membrane excitation ceases;
2. SR Ca²⁺ release falls;
3. SERCA actively returns Ca²⁺ to the SR;
4. cytosolic free Ca²⁺ declines;
5. troponin–tropomyosin shifts toward inhibition;
6. productive cross-bridge recruitment falls and attached bridges detach through ATP-dependent cycling;
7. active force decays.

Recent primary evidence showing SERCA1 regulation changing relaxation reinforces that Ca²⁺ removal kinetics are functionally important.

## 9. Twitch, temporal summation and tetanus

### 9.1 Twitch

A twitch is the transient mechanical response to a single effective excitation event.

The action potential is brief; the Ca²⁺ transient and mechanical response persist longer.

Therefore electrical excitation and mechanical force are not temporally identical signals.

### 9.2 Temporal summation

If another excitation arrives before full Ca²⁺ removal and mechanical relaxation:

- cytosolic activation can remain elevated;
- more regulatory sites remain permissive;
- more force-bearing cross-bridges can be supported;
- force can rise above the preceding twitch.

The force response is nonlinear and history-dependent, not a simple arithmetic sum of identical independent twitches.

### 9.3 Tetanus

At sufficiently high repeated excitation frequency, individual mechanical responses fuse into sustained high force.

This is a **frequency-dependent activation state**, not a change in action-potential amplitude.

Exact force-frequency curves depend on fiber phenotype, Ca²⁺ handling, temperature, fatigue and experimental context.

Detailed motor-unit firing/recruitment belongs to NEU.

## 10. Force–length and force–velocity boundaries

These relations are listed in the exact Section 3 scope, but detailed mechanics belong primarily to PHY-04 / biomechanics.

### 10.1 Force–length

Active force depends on sarcomere/muscle length because geometry changes the probability and arrangement of productive actin–myosin interactions; passive structures including titin/connective elements increasingly contribute at longer lengths.

Project rule:

**force at a given activation is not length-invariant.**

### 10.2 Force–velocity

Force and shortening/lengthening velocity are coupled through cross-bridge kinetics and dynamic filament/mechanical behavior.

Project rule:

**force capacity is not velocity-invariant.**

Do not use this PASS to prescribe eccentric/concentric training; mode-specific force, cost, fatigue and adaptation remain PHY-04.

## 11. Other cell/tissue components in Section 2

### 11.1 Mitochondria

Mitochondria support ATP production, redox balance and Ca²⁺-related signaling/homeostasis.

They are functionally coupled to contraction but are not the immediate force-generating motor.

Detailed oxidative metabolism belongs to BIO.

### 11.2 Myonuclei

Skeletal myofibers contain many myonuclei supporting a very large shared cytoplasmic volume.

Current evidence also emphasizes myonuclear spatial/functional heterogeneity rather than one perfectly homogeneous nuclear pool.

Exact myonuclear-domain rules during hypertrophy remain more nuanced than a rigid fixed-volume model.

### 11.3 Satellite cells

Satellite cells / muscle stem cells reside outside the mature myofiber cytoplasm under the basal lamina and support regeneration and, in relevant contexts, nuclear accretion/remodeling.

They are not part of the immediate excitation → force chain.

Detailed hypertrophy causality belongs to PHY-07.

### 11.4 Extracellular matrix

Muscle ECM:

- organizes tissue architecture;
- transmits/distributes mechanical force;
- shapes the cell/stem-cell niche;
- remodels with injury, disease and training.

It is not merely inert packing material.

Detailed connective-tissue adaptation belongs to PHY-12.

### 11.5 Capillary network

The capillary network supplies exchange capacity for oxygen, substrates, metabolites and signaling factors.

Recent work emphasizes that **distribution/local supply geometry matters, not capillary count alone**.

Capillaries support contractile function but do not generate force.

Detailed vascular adaptation belongs to PHY-14 and conditioning topics.

## 13. Established project-level model

The current bounded model is:

**NMJ output → muscle-fiber action potential → T-tubular depolarization → CaV1.1 voltage sensing / triadic coupling → RyR1-mediated SR Ca²⁺ release → troponin–tropomyosin activation of thin filament → ATP-dependent actomyosin cycling → sarcomeric force → transmission through myofibril/cytoskeleton/ECM/tendon → external force**

Relaxation:

**reduced excitation → reduced RyR1 release + SERCA-mediated Ca²⁺ reuptake → falling myoplasmic Ca²⁺ → thin-filament inhibition → cross-bridge disengagement → falling active force**

Confidence: **HIGH for the architecture and main causal chain.**

## 14. Important uncertainties / non-overclaims

### 14.1 Exact CaV1.1 → RyR1 molecular signal

HIGH confidence that physical/functional coupling is central.

But the complete molecular conformational sequence is not fully solved.

Status: **open mechanistic detail, not a project blocker.**

### 14.2 Titin in active force

HIGH confidence for structural/passive elastic roles.

Evidence increasingly supports activation-dependent and active-mechanics contributions.

Exact quantitative contribution and mechanism in normal dynamic human movement remain unresolved.

Status: **MODERATE / active research; do not promote to sole active-force mechanism.**

### 14.3 Exact force-generating cross-bridge substep

HIGH confidence that ATP-dependent actomyosin cycling is core.

The precise ordering/timing of force generation around Pi-release-related states remains under active mechanistic refinement.

Status: **mechanistic detail open, core model intact.**

### 14.4 Thick-filament activation

Current evidence supports regulation of motor availability beyond a naive model where every myosin head is always equally ready to bind.

Detailed thick-filament mechanosensing/super-relaxed-state physiology is not required to answer the current project question.

Status: **deferred unless later strength/fatigue evidence requires it.**

## 15. Training-relevant implications

1. **Neural activation is not force itself.** A neural/electrical signal still depends on ECC, Ca²⁺ handling and myofilament function to become force.
2. **Fatigue can occur downstream of neural drive.** Reduced Ca²⁺ release, altered Ca²⁺ sensitivity or cross-bridge function can reduce force without implying structural muscle loss.
3. **Muscle size is not the only determinant of force.** Contractile activation state, length, velocity and architecture matter.
4. **High stimulation frequency increases force through temporal activation/summation, not by making each action potential larger.**
5. **Relaxation costs energy.** Ca²⁺ reuptake is ATP-dependent.
6. **Mechanistic plausibility is not training-efficacy evidence.** A pathway changing acutely does not prove more hypertrophy/strength.
7. **Titin/ECM/mitochondria/capillaries matter to function but should not be collapsed into the actomyosin motor.**

These are foundation rules for later PHY-03…PHY-10, BIO, MEC, PRO and TST.

## 16. Applicability to Ilya

The core excitation–contraction architecture applies directly to healthy adult human skeletal muscle.

However:

- exact fiber phenotype and force-frequency behavior vary;
- aging, disease, medications, fatigue, temperature and training status can modify Ca²⁺ handling and contractile kinetics;
- no personal pathology is inferred from this general physiology;
- practical load/exercise choices must wait for downstream mechanics, programming and personal assessment.
