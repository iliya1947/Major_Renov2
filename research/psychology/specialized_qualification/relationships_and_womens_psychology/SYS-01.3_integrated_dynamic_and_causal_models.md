## 3. Integrated model and evaluation
Separate state, observation, contextual inputs, initial conditions, transition rule, time unit, parameters, outcome and measurement process. A dynamic model is a representation of hypothesized change; a causal graph is a representation of assumptions about causal structure. Neither is established by a coherent narrative. Unfold possible feedback through time-indexed nodes. Compare own persistence, shared context, independent changes, cross-person predictive coupling, genuine causal feedback, nonlinear state dependence and measurement/selection artifacts. Same-day or lagged association cannot by itself choose between these models.

Three distinct evaluation targets: (i) descriptive fit and residual structure, (ii) prospective accuracy on withheld data at a specified horizon, (iii) causal identification under stated assumptions. An estimated model can succeed on one and fail on another. Separate between-person from within-person estimands, observable behavior from inferred states and actual choices from hypothetical interventions. A fitted cross-lag or random-intercept model does not automatically address hidden time-varying common causes, interference, selection, unequal lags or measurement error.

For a causal contrast specify exposure, comparator, target population/unit, outcome and horizon. Make alternative graphs explicit. Determine adjustment roles by graph rather than significance; distinguish common cause, mediator and collider. In sequential settings consider exposure-confounder feedback and joint exposure regimes. Identification is distinct from estimation; observational equivalence can remain even with excellent predictions. Sensitivity analysis tests dependence on chosen assumptions, not whether the true causal structure has been found. Evaluate omitted common causes, sampling/lag choices, variable definitions, missingness and selection, alternative model forms and plausible data-generating mechanisms. For qualitative sparse evidence, use explicit competing explanations and disconfirming conditions rather than manufacture numerical robustness values.

For prediction, define the criterion and horizon before outcomes, use only information available at prediction time, compare against appropriate simple baselines (persistence, historical mean or empirical base rate), and evaluate chronological holdouts with uncertainty. Distinguish within-person future predictions from new-person generalization. More complex models are justified by demonstrated incremental value for the particular outcome and design, not biological plausibility or retrospective narrative fit. A stable pattern is not a welfare criterion or proof of consent; no dynamic or causal argument overrides autonomy or safety.

## 4. Two worked synthetic checks
Invented numerical series unrelated to private material. Training observations 2, 4, 6, 8; withheld observations 8, 8, 8. Before evaluation define a fixed-origin three-step forecast and MAE. Persistence forecast 8, 8, 8 has MAE 0; extrapolated training trend 10, 12, 14 has MAE 4. The trend fits training observations exactly but loses on this constructed holdout. Calculated directly in JavaScript before writing this record. This deliberately simple example demonstrates separation of retrospective fit and future error, not a representative performance study or general ranking. A plateau, context shift, measurement ceiling or coincidence remain plausible; one holdout does not identify the mechanism or establish calibration.

Synthetic causal sensitivity check: let U, eA and eY be independent, mean-zero variables with unit variance. A=U+eA, Y=U+eY yields Cov(A,Y)=1, Var(A)=2 and population regression slope .5 despite no causal arrow A->Y. Under intervention fixing A while retaining U and eY, E[Y] is unchanged. A different generator, A with variance 2 and Y=.5A+e with independent error variance 1.5, gives the same covariance matrix (Var(Y)=2, Cov(A,Y)=1) and an intervention slope .5. If all inputs are Gaussian, the observable joint distributions coincide. Verified algebra: .5*2=1 and .25*2+1.5=2. No observation-based linear fit can discriminate these examples; external assumptions or design are needed. This is not a formal Cinelli–Hazlett implementation or an individual psychological estimate.

## 5. Operational procedure
1. Define exact claim, observation unit, time horizon, evidence quality, available criterion and purpose. Preserve missing information.
2. Compare the simplest adequate description with alternative dynamic and causal hypotheses, including external inputs and measurement.
3. State model assumptions, causal contrast, temporal order, possible confounding, mediation, selection and partner/shared effects. Do not infer a causal mechanism from a predictive lag.
4. Specify observations or independent criteria that could distinguish the alternatives. Use ordinary legitimate information; no engineered perturbation, surveillance or psychological pressure.
5. Check sensitivity to timing, context, measurement, specification and plausible omitted variables; restrict inference when assumptions cannot be defended. Quantify only when appropriate data and validated methods exist.
6. Evaluate prospective predictions separately against baseline and relevant outcome; revise on disconfirming evidence. Keep actual decisions and costs distinct from scientific certainty.
7. Respect freely expressed choices, privacy and safety. Do not use system representations as prescriptions to manipulate people. Record unresolved alternatives and next evidence needed.

## 6. Internal transfer review
Rubric: C1 construct/estimand distinction, C2 credible alternative, C3 discriminating evidence or assumption, C4 inference boundary. Critical errors: causal inflation, invented data, selection bias ignored, group-to-individual determinism, neglect of contrary evidence, coercion or privacy breach. Six original self-authored neutral cases; not independent or held-out.
A. The numerical trend example above. C1 fit versus forecast error; C2 plateau or measurement ceiling; C3 comparable further chronological holdouts; C4 no inference of true mechanism. 4/4.
B. The synthetic common-cause example. C1 prediction versus intervention; C2 hidden common cause versus direct causal effect; C3 independent identifying design or justified assumptions; C4 equal covariance cannot settle causation. 4/4.
C. Estimated coupling differs between hourly and daily observations. C1 lag-specific parameters; C2 aggregation or different underlying time processes; C3 matched time-scale model and sensitivity checks; C4 no automatic causal direction or universal sign. 4/4.
D. A significant regression coefficient changes markedly under a plausible omitted-variable sensitivity scenario. C1 statistical uncertainty versus identification uncertainty; C2 shared cause; C3 justify confounder strength and compare alternative designs; C4 no proof of effect or absence. 4/4.
E. A nonlinear model forecasts one structured dataset better, but a simple mean is almost as accurate in another. C1 distinct estimands, datasets and prediction horizons; C2 predictor/measurement and sample differences; C3 aligned held-out comparison; C4 no universal ranking or transfer. 4/4.
F. A recurring collaboration pattern is stable but one participant reports dissatisfaction and declines further participation. C1 stability, welfare and choice; C2 role constraints or ordinary preferences; C3 actual stated goals and context; C4 respect decision without causal or consent inference. 4/4.
