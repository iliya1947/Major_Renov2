# NDEV-01 — Neurodevelopmental foundations: developmental onset/course, ADHD–autism boundaries, executive-function variability, masking/compensation, comorbidity, and differential limits

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Establish the foundational neurodevelopmental reasoning layer needed after CLN.

The goal is not to memorize diagnostic criteria.

The goal is to prevent high-impact inference errors when developmental traits, executive difficulties, social-communication differences, psychiatric symptoms, masking, and adult recognition overlap.

Core questions:

1. What makes a presentation neurodevelopmental rather than acquired/episodic?
2. How should ADHD and autism be differentiated without pretending they are mutually exclusive?
3. What can executive-function evidence actually establish?
4. How should masking/compensation affect diagnostic confidence?
5. How should late recognition in adolescence/adulthood be interpreted?
6. How should comorbidity and overlapping symptoms be handled without double counting?
7. What evidence is required before translating a generic developmental pattern into a person-level conclusion?

Canonical rule:

`developmental history + current phenotype + cross-context function + alternative explanations + co-occurrence + measurement limits -> calibrated neurodevelopmental inference`.

Never:

`current symptom -> developmental diagnosis`

or:

`executive dysfunction -> ADHD/autism`.

# I. Question Card

## 1. Concrete dependency

CLN-03 established that developmental baseline must be separated from acquired/episodic change.

The unresolved Phase C dependency is a generic framework for ADHD/autism and broader neurodevelopmental reasoning.

Without it, common errors include:

- adult diagnosis mistaken for adult onset;
- absent childhood diagnosis mistaken for absent childhood features;
- current distractibility mistaken for ADHD;
- social difficulty mistaken for autism;
- executive-test deficits treated as diagnostic biomarkers;
- masking treated as proof of autism;
- good performance treated as proof against developmental impairment;
- ADHD and autism treated as mutually exclusive;
- the same symptom counted twice toward both conditions without functional differentiation;
- mood/anxiety/sleep/substance symptoms retrofitted into a developmental diagnosis;
- a developmental diagnosis treated as an immutable deficit profile.

- [x] diagnosis/classification;
- [x] developmental inference;
- [x] measurement;
- [x] differential diagnosis;
- [x] evidence-to-individual transfer;
- [ ] treatment algorithm;
- [ ] individualized diagnosis.

## 3. Target constructs

- neurodevelopmental onset/course;

- autism spectrum disorder / autism;
- executive-function variability;
- masking/camouflaging;
- compensation;
- co-occurrence/comorbidity;
- developmental versus acquired/episodic pattern.

## 4. Population/context

Children, adolescents, and adults in general clinical/research contexts.

Sex/gender, cultural context, environmental demands, and delayed recognition are treated as modifiers of detection and manifestation rather than as diagnostic shortcuts.

## 5. Main comparators

ADHD/autism versus:

- each other;
- anxiety;
- depression;

- substance/medication effects;

- normative individual variation.

A calibrated generic inference such as:

- developmental pattern plausible;
- ADHD-family evidence stronger/weaker;
- autism-family evidence stronger/weaker;
- true co-occurrence remains possible;
- acquired/episodic explanation has priority;
- developmental history is insufficient;
- neuropsychological evidence is nonspecific;
- formal assessment is required.

Not:

- formal diagnosis;
- person-specific causal mechanism;
- treatment prescription.

## 7. Time/course

Developmental history is mandatory.

Distinguish:

- early developmental presence;
- early recognition;
- later manifestation under increased demands;
- later diagnosis;
- acquired adult-onset symptoms;
- episodic psychiatric change;
- longitudinal persistence/remission.

## 8. Eligible evidence

- current official diagnostic/public-health sources;
- systematic reviews/meta-analyses;
- high-quality current reviews;
- longitudinal studies where onset/course claims are made;
- current project DEV/EXE/PSYCHOMETRICS/CLN boundaries.

## 9. Applicability boundary

Even strong group evidence cannot:

- diagnose one person;
- prove childhood onset from current symptoms alone;
- infer mechanism from a label;
- infer autism from camouflaging alone;
- infer ADHD from executive deficits alone.

No private individualized facts are used or stored.

NDEV-01 closes when:

1. developmental onset and recognition are separated;
2. ADHD and autism core architectures are separated;
3. co-occurrence is allowed without double counting;
4. EF evidence is bounded;
5. masking/compensation is bounded;
6. adult/late diagnosis is handled;
7. major psychiatric/sleep/substance mimics are represented;
8. assessment requires multi-source developmental evidence;
9. person-level transfer remains calibrated.

# II. Current classification anchor

## 12. Neurodevelopmental means developmental origin, not fixed phenotype

ADHD and autism are classified as neurodevelopmental conditions.

This implies developmental origin.

It does **not** imply:

- symptoms are always obvious in early childhood;
- manifestation is identical across age;
- impairment is constant;
- development stops;
- traits cannot be compensated for;
- environment/support has no effect.

## 13. Developmental onset and diagnostic recognition are different events

Four separate times may exist:

1. first underlying developmental difference;
2. first observable manifestation;
3. first clinically significant impairment;
4. first formal diagnosis.

They need not coincide.

## 14. Late diagnosis does not imply late onset

Current CDC material states that some autistic people are not diagnosed until adolescence or adulthood.

Current NIMH guidance states ADHD can be diagnosed at any age although symptoms must have begun in childhood.

Therefore:

`diagnosed in adulthood -> began in adulthood`

is invalid.

## 15. Early onset does not require continuous identical phenotype

Development changes:

- symptom expression;
- demands;
- coping;
- opportunities;
- environmental support;
- observable impairment.

A developmental condition can change presentation substantially over time.

# III. ADHD architecture

## 16. Core ADHD phenotype

Current NIMH guidance defines ADHD around persistent patterns of:

- inattention;
- hyperactivity;
- impulsivity.

The relative prominence changes by person and age.

## 17. Developmental onset is required

Current NIMH guidance states symptoms must begin in childhood, before age 12.

This is a diagnostic requirement under current DSM-style criteria.

## 18. Childhood onset != childhood diagnosis

NIMH explicitly notes adults may have gone undiagnosed because:

- family/teachers did not recognize the pattern;
- symptoms were milder;
- earlier environments were supportive;
- adult demands later exposed impairment.

Therefore missing an early diagnosis is weak evidence against ADHD.

## 19. Current symptoms alone cannot establish childhood onset

Adult inattention/restlessness can arise from:

- anxiety;
- depression;
- sleep disorders;
- substances;
- medical conditions;
- stress;
- other psychiatric states.

Current NIMH explicitly requires thorough evaluation of alternatives.

## 20. Cross-setting requirement matters

ADHD is not defined by difficulty in one unusually demanding context alone.

Current diagnostic guidance requires symptoms across two or more settings and functional impairment.

## 21. Context can modulate expression

Performance can improve when:

- task is highly motivating;
- external structure is strong;
- novelty is high;
- deadlines are immediate;
- support/scaffolding is present.

Context-sensitive performance does not automatically disprove ADHD.

It also does not prove ADHD.

## 22. Adult presentation differs from stereotyped childhood presentation

NIMH notes adult ADHD may show:

- inattention;
- restlessness;
- impulsivity;
- organizational/time-management difficulties;
- less overt motor hyperactivity.

Therefore:

`not visibly hyperactive adult -> no ADHD`

is invalid.

## 23. Emotional dysregulation is clinically relevant but not a unique core marker

Current adult-ADHD literature treats emotional dysregulation as an important but still debated component.

It is not diagnostically specific.

Mood instability can also reflect:

- anxiety;
- depression;
- bipolar disorder;
- trauma;
- personality processes;
- sleep/substance effects.

## 24. "Late-onset ADHD" remains controversial

Current adult-ADHD reviews support genuine adult ADHD while treating de novo adult onset as unresolved/controversial.

Many apparent late-onset cases may reflect:

- previously subthreshold childhood symptoms;
- compensation/scaffolding;
- missed historical symptoms;
- psychiatric/medical mimics;
- measurement error.

Therefore:

**first recognized in adulthood != established adult-onset ADHD.**

## 25. Weak retrospective evidence lowers certainty; it does not automatically falsify ADHD

Adult developmental reconstruction can be limited by:

- missing school records;
- unavailable caregivers;
- recall bias;
- compensation;
- changing demands.

The correct response is graded confidence, not binary dismissal.

# IV. Autism architecture

## 26. Core autism phenotype has two major domains

Current NIMH/CDC material describes autism around:

1. persistent social-communication/social-interaction differences;
2. restricted/repetitive behaviors, interests, or activities.

Social difficulty alone is insufficient.

## 27. Autism is developmental

NIMH notes signs are often recognizable in the first two years of life.

Current DSM-derived CDC guidance requires symptoms in the early developmental period.

## 28. Early developmental symptoms may become fully visible later

CDC's current diagnostic criteria explicitly state symptoms may not become fully manifest until social demands exceed capacities.

This prevents the error:

`not obvious in early childhood -> cannot be autism`.

## 29. Learned strategies can mask observable features

Current DSM-derived CDC guidance explicitly notes symptoms can be masked by learned strategies later in life.

Therefore:

`good eye contact / fluent conversation / learned social skill -> no autism`

is invalid.

## 30. Masking does not erase developmental origin

A compensated presentation can appear less externally atypical while requiring effort or strategy.

The developmental history and functional pattern remain relevant.

## 31. Social communication must be interpreted developmentally

Social reciprocity/communication should be compared with:

- developmental level;
- language ability;
- intellectual ability;
- cultural context.

## 32. Intellectual disability and autism can coexist

CDC's DSM-derived criteria explicitly state:

- intellectual disability does not automatically exclude autism;
- social communication should be below what would be expected for general developmental level if both are diagnosed.

## 33. Restricted/repetitive pattern is not optional in DSM-style autism diagnosis

Persistent social difficulty without sufficient restricted/repetitive features should not be automatically labeled autism.

Other explanations include:

- social anxiety;
- pragmatic communication disorder;
- ADHD;
- language disorder;
- trauma;
- personality/temperament variation.

## 34. Sensory differences are informative but not unique

Sensory hyper/hyporeactivity is part of current autism criteria architecture.

But sensory differences also occur:

- in ADHD;
- other neurodevelopmental conditions;
- anxiety;
- trauma;
- general population variation.

Therefore:

`sensory sensitivity -> autism`

is invalid.

# V. ADHD–autism overlap and co-occurrence

## 35. ADHD and autism are not mutually exclusive

Modern DSM/ICD classification permits co-occurring ADHD and autism.

The older diagnostic veto no longer applies.

## 36. Co-occurrence is clinically meaningful

Current reviews and population research show substantial symptom and diagnostic co-occurrence.

Therefore:

`autism explains everything, so ADHD cannot also be present`

is invalid.

The reverse is also invalid.

## 37. Co-occurrence does not mean every overlapping symptom gets counted twice

A major current review warns against **double counting**.

One manifestation should not automatically be assigned to both conditions.

## 38. Functional analysis matters

Example:

A person interrupts conversations.

Possible contributors:

- impulsivity/inhibitory-control difficulty;
- difficulty tracking reciprocal conversation;
- anxiety;
- enthusiasm/context;
- hearing/language issue.

The behavior itself does not specify the diagnosis.

## 39. Social impairment occurs in both conditions

At the group level:

- autism centrally involves social-communication/interaction differences;
- ADHD can impair social functioning through inattention, impulsivity, poor timing, interruption, and regulation difficulties.

This distinction is useful but not absolute for an individual.

## 40. Restricted/repetitive interests and routines are more structurally central to autism

But ADHD can also involve:

- intense engagement;
- preference patterns;
- repetitive behavior;
- sensory differences.

Therefore unusual interest intensity alone is insufficient.

## 41. Inattention can be secondary to autistic-context mismatch

Examples:

- overload;
- sensory distraction;
- low relevance;
- social processing demand;
- fatigue from compensation.

Do not automatically interpret all inattention in an autistic person as comorbid ADHD.

## 42. Conversely, ADHD can obscure autism

Impulsivity, conversational interruption, emotional dysregulation, and variable attention can dominate the clinical picture.

Persistent social-communication and restricted/repetitive developmental features must still be assessed separately.

## 43. True co-occurrence requires incremental evidence

Ask whether each diagnosis explains clinically significant features not fully accounted for by the other.

This is a generic reasoning rule, not a formal criterion substitute.

# VI. Executive-function variability

## 44. Executive dysfunction is not a diagnosis

Executive functions include multiple constructs such as:

- working memory;
- inhibition;
- shifting;
- planning;
- monitoring;
- control allocation.

Difficulty in one or more domains is not equivalent to ADHD or autism.

## 45. ADHD does not equal executive dysfunction

Some people with ADHD perform within typical ranges on many neuropsychological tasks.

Current adult-ADHD review evidence emphasizes average neuropsychological differences are much smaller than symptom differences.

## 46. Autism does not equal one executive profile

Autistic people are highly heterogeneous in executive performance.

No universal autism EF signature is established.

## 47. ADHD and autism EF profiles overlap substantially

A 2024 Nature Reviews Psychology review concludes:

- both groups tend to perform moderately worse than neurotypical groups across broad neuropsychological tasks;
- unique versus shared deficits remain poorly resolved;
- co-occurrence has often been inadequately handled;
- traditional task/rating measures have limited specificity and construct validity.

Therefore:

`EF profile -> ADHD versus autism classifier`

is not justified.

## 48. Task performance and everyday executive difficulty are distinct

Laboratory tasks and informant/self-report ratings:

- sample different behaviors;
- have different measurement properties;
- may correlate modestly.

Do not treat one as ground truth.

## 49. Neuropsychological testing can characterize strengths/weaknesses

Current NIMH ADHD guidance includes cognitive testing as useful for:

- strengths/challenges;
- learning-disability differential.

Current adult-ADHD review cautions that neuropsychological tests do not reliably distinguish ADHD from other clinical groups.

Thus:

**useful profile != diagnostic biomarker.**

## 50. Good task performance does not exclude developmental impairment

Possible reasons:

- high general ability;
- compensatory strategies;
- structured testing context;
- novelty;
- low ecological load;
- uneven profile.

## 51. Poor task performance is nonspecific

It can arise from:

- sleep deprivation;
- anxiety/depression;
- stress;
- low motivation;
- medication/substances;
- pain/illness;
- learning difficulties;
- test misunderstanding.

# VII. Masking, camouflaging, and compensation

## 52. Camouflaging is not one simple construct

A 2025 integrative systematic review describes autistic camouflaging as multi-definitional and organizes multiple strategy types.

Commonly discussed components include:

- masking/suppression of observable traits;
- compensation through learned strategies;
- assimilation/effort to fit social expectations.

## 53. Masking can reduce observable signs without removing difficulty

Observed behavior may look more typical while:

- strategy use is effortful;
- social processing remains nonautomatic;
- fatigue/overload persists;
- difficulties emerge in less structured contexts.

This is compatible with current DSM language about learned strategies masking symptoms.

## 54. Camouflaging is not diagnostic proof

Non-autistic people can also manage impressions, rehearse social behavior, or suppress traits.

A 2026 meta-analysis found only a moderate average association between autistic traits and camouflaging, with substantial heterogeneity.

Therefore:

`reports masking -> autism`

is invalid.

## 55. Camouflaging is not exclusively female

Current evidence does not support treating camouflaging as a female-only phenomenon.

A 2026 meta-analysis found the autistic-trait/camouflaging association was comparable across sexes.

This does not mean prevalence, strategy type, diagnostic bias, or lived experience is identical across sexes.

## 56. Masking may contribute to delayed recognition

Current diagnostic-inequity literature and women's-autism reviews support the possibility that less externally obvious presentations and camouflaging can contribute to delayed identification.

But delayed recognition has multiple determinants:

- clinician expectations;
- instrument sensitivity;
- access;
- language/culture;
- comorbidity;
- family/school recognition.

## 57. Camouflaging and mental health are associated, causal direction remains uncertain

Cross-sectional/scoping evidence often links more camouflaging with poorer mental health.

However longitudinal evidence does not yet justify the simple causal rule:

`camouflaging -> later mental-health deterioration`.

Therefore retain:

- association;
- plausible burden;
- uncertain causal direction.

## 58. Compensation is not "faking normal"

Compensation can be understood as learned strategy use that helps performance despite underlying difficulty.

Avoid moralized interpretations.

## 59. Successful compensation can delay impairment recognition

Environmental support and high ability may keep performance adequate until demands increase.

This logic applies to both autism and ADHD-like developmental difficulties.

# VIII. Adult and late recognition

## 60. Adult diagnosis can be valid

Current adult-ADHD literature supports adult ADHD as a valid and often underrecognized condition.

Autism can also be diagnosed in adults.

## 61. Adult assessment requires developmental reconstruction

High-value evidence may include:

- school history;
- childhood behavior;
- caregiver/family report;
- old reports;
- longstanding patterns across contexts;
- developmental milestones;
- retrospective self-report.

No single source is perfect.

## 62. Missing records should lower certainty, not force a binary answer

A developmental history can be incomplete.

Use:

- converging evidence;
- plausibility;
- alternative explanations;
- confidence grading.

## 63. Increased adult demands can reveal previously compensated difficulties

Examples:

- independent scheduling;
- university/work complexity;
- unstructured workload;
- relationship demands;
- parenting;
- reduced external scaffolding.

This can produce **late functional manifestation** without true late developmental onset.

## 64. Adult-onset-like ADHD requires aggressive differential checking

Current adult reviews emphasize alternatives such as:

- mood/anxiety disorders;
- substance use;
- sleep problems;
- traumatic brain injury;
- thyroid disease;
- medication effects.

## 65. Adult autism recognition can be delayed

CDC notes some autistic people are not diagnosed until adolescence/adulthood.

Delayed diagnosis should not be equated with symptom fabrication or new onset.

# IX. Developmental trait versus acquired/episodic psychopathology

## 66. Baseline-change question is mandatory

Ask:

> Was this pattern present developmentally and relatively consistently, or is it a marked change from prior baseline?

## 67. ADHD-like symptoms versus anxiety/depression

Overlap:

- concentration difficulty;
- restlessness;
- procrastination;
- sleep disruption;
- forgetfulness;
- irritability.

Differentiators include:

- childhood developmental pattern;
- cross-context persistence;
- mood/anxiety episode relationship;
- state dependence.

## 68. ADHD versus bipolar-spectrum episode

Overlap:

- distractibility;
- impulsivity;
- talkativeness;
- activity;
- emotional intensity.

High-value distinction:

- ADHD = developmental persistent pattern;
- mania/hypomania = distinct episodic change from baseline with broader mood/energy/sleep architecture.

CLN-03 controls the bipolar side of this differential.

## 69. Autism versus social anxiety

Both can involve:

- reduced eye contact;
- social avoidance;
- conversational difficulty;
- exhaustion after social interaction.

Ask:

- developmental social-communication pattern;
- restricted/repetitive features;
- fear of negative evaluation;
- desire/ability versus anxiety inhibition;
- context/history.

## 70. Autism versus trauma-related social withdrawal

Trauma can alter:

- trust;
- avoidance;
- arousal;
- communication;
- sensory tolerance.

A post-trauma change from a different baseline weighs against explaining everything as developmental autism.

## 71. ADHD/autism versus sleep problems

Sleep disorders/deprivation can worsen:

- attention;
- inhibition;
- emotion regulation;
- memory;
- sensory tolerance.

Sleep should be assessed rather than treated as a minor nuisance variable.

## 72. ADHD/autism versus substance/medication effects

Substances/medications can mimic or amplify:

- inattention;
- activation;
- restlessness;
- cognitive slowing;
- mood instability.

Temporal relation matters.

## 73. Autism versus personality pathology

Longstanding social/relationship patterns can resemble personality descriptions.

Developmental social-communication/repetitive architecture and age of onset must be separated from later-emerging personality pathology.

# X. Assessment architecture

## 74. No single autism tool should determine diagnosis

Current CDC guidance explicitly states no single assessment tool should be the sole basis for autism diagnosis.

Assessment usually integrates:

- developmental history;
- caregiver/informant information;
- direct professional observation;
- structured tools when appropriate.

## 75. ADHD screening/checklists are inputs, not diagnosis

Current NIMH guidance places rating scales/checklists inside a broader evaluation including:

- medical/mental-health history;
- childhood behavior/school experiences;
- informants;
- alternative causes.

## 76. Self-report is important but not sufficient for all developmental claims

Adults may have strong access to:

- internal effort;
- distractibility;
- sensory experience;
- compensation.

But retrospective developmental onset may require additional corroboration when available.

## 77. Informants are informative but not ground truth

Informants can miss:

- internal symptoms;
- compensatory effort;
- context-specific difficulty.

They can also introduce:

- recall bias;
- expectation bias;
- limited observation.

Use convergence rather than source hierarchy by default.

## 78. School/work records can be useful but are imperfect

Records may reflect:

- behavior;
- achievement;
- disciplinary history;
- support needs.

They may miss:

- high-effort compensation;
- inattentive symptoms;
- social exhaustion;
- supportive environment effects.

## 79. Diagnosis is not a cognitive-test threshold

There is no single EF score that establishes ADHD or autism.

## 80. Screening sensitivity can produce false certainty

A high screening score means:

`more assessment warranted`

not:

`diagnosis established`.

This is controlled by PSYCHOMETRICS.

# XI. Heterogeneity

## 81. ADHD is heterogeneous

Variation occurs in:

- inattentive/hyperactive-impulsive balance;
- symptom severity;
- executive functioning;
- emotion regulation;
- comorbidity;
- persistence;
- impairment.

## 82. Autism is heterogeneous

Variation occurs in:

- language;
- intellectual ability;
- social communication;
- restricted/repetitive behavior;
- sensory profile;
- adaptive functioning;
- support needs;
- co-occurring conditions.

## 83. Group averages are poor person-level templates

Average executive/social differences cannot be assumed for one diagnosed person.

Use direct person-specific evidence in private work.

## 84. High ability can coexist with major impairment

Strong reasoning/verbal ability does not exclude:

- ADHD;
- autism;
- uneven adaptive functioning;
- executive difficulty.

Ability and functional adaptation are separate constructs.

## 85. Functional impairment can be demand-dependent

A developmental difference may become impairing only when:

- structure decreases;
- workload increases;
- social complexity increases;
- sensory demands increase.

This is not proof of new onset.

# XII. Sex/gender and diagnostic detection

## 86. Diagnostic rates differ by sex/gender

Current NIMH ADHD material notes males more often show hyperactive/impulsive presentations, while females are more often diagnosed with inattentive presentations.

Current autism surveillance also shows sex differences in identified prevalence.

## 87. Identification differences are not automatically biological prevalence differences

Observed diagnostic rates reflect a mixture of:

- underlying prevalence;
- referral;
- recognition;
- symptom expression;
- measurement;
- access;
- clinician expectations.

## 88. Girls/women may be diagnosed later in some autism samples

Recent systematic review evidence reports delayed recognition and diagnostic inequities in females.

However:

- evidence base has sampling limitations;
- not all women camouflage;
- not all men present stereotypically;
- sex/gender cannot be used as a diagnostic shortcut.

## 89. Instruments may be less sensitive to underrepresented phenotypes

Diagnostic tools are built from particular samples and constructs.

A negative score does not automatically close the case when developmental evidence is strong.

But "instrument bias" also cannot be invoked to rescue any favored diagnosis from disconfirming evidence.

---

# XIII. Co-occurring conditions

## 90. ADHD/autism frequently coexist with other conditions

Examples include:

- anxiety;
- depression;
- sleep problems;
- learning difficulties;
- other developmental conditions.

## 91. Comorbidity can alter presentation

Anxiety may increase avoidance.

Depression may worsen executive performance.

Sleep difficulty can amplify attention/emotion problems.

Therefore current phenotype may reflect layered processes.

## 92. Comorbidity is not diagnostic noise

If a second condition has independent evidence and incremental explanatory value, retain it.

## 93. But shared symptoms should not automatically multiply diagnoses

Use:

- independent developmental evidence;
- distinct functional pattern;
- temporal course;
- non-overlapping symptom structure;
- incremental impairment.

---

# XIV. Neurodiversity and clinical reasoning

## 94. Difference and disorder are not moral categories

Neurodevelopmental labels describe patterns relevant to functioning/support under current classifications.

They do not imply:

- inferiority;
- lack of agency;
- fixed inability;
- one personality type.

## 95. Strengths do not cancel impairments

A person can show:

- high expertise;
- intense interest;
- strong memory;
- creativity;
- persistence

while also experiencing significant impairment in other domains.

## 96. Impairment does not cancel competence

Do not convert support need into global incapacity.

## 97. Terminology preferences vary

Some individuals prefer:

- "autistic person";
- "person with autism";
- "neurodivergent";

and preferences should not be inferred universally.

Repository science should remain respectful and classification-precise.

# XV. High-value failure modes

## Failure A — adult diagnosis = adult onset

Incorrect.

Recognition and onset are distinct.

## Failure B — no childhood diagnosis = no developmental condition

Incorrect.

Earlier symptoms may be missed, compensated for, or insufficiently impairing.

## Failure C — current distractibility = ADHD

Incorrect.

Many acquired/state conditions mimic ADHD symptoms.

## Failure D — executive dysfunction = ADHD

Incorrect.

EF deficits are nonspecific and heterogeneous.

## Failure E — normal EF tests = no ADHD

Incorrect.

Task sensitivity and ecological validity are limited.

## Failure F — poor EF tests = ADHD

Incorrect.

Many conditions impair EF performance.

## Failure G — social difficulty = autism

Incorrect.

Autism requires broader developmental architecture including restricted/repetitive patterns under current criteria.

## Failure H — good eye contact = no autism

Incorrect.

Observable social behavior can be learned/masked and varies individually.

## Failure I — masking = autism

Incorrect.

Camouflaging is neither unique nor diagnostically sufficient.

## Failure J — masking causes mental-health problems

Too strong as a general causal claim.

Association is better supported than causal direction.

## Failure K — autism and ADHD are mutually exclusive

Incorrect under current classification.

## Failure L — overlapping symptom counts for both diagnoses automatically

Incorrect.

Double counting must be checked.

## Failure M — sensory sensitivity = autism

Incorrect.

It is not diagnostically unique.

## Failure N — hyperfocus = ADHD/autism proof

Incorrect.

Intense engagement is nonspecific.

## Failure O — emotional dysregulation = ADHD core proof

Incorrect.

It is clinically relevant but nonspecific.

## Failure P — late manifestation under demand = late onset

Incorrect.

Demands can expose previously compensated developmental differences.

## Failure Q — retrospective childhood report = objective history

Incorrect.

Recall is evidence with uncertainty.

## Failure R — missing retrospective corroboration = definite absence

Incorrect.

Confidence should be graded.

## Failure S — screening score = diagnosis

Incorrect.

Screening is triage evidence.

## Failure T — diagnosis = fixed cognitive profile

Incorrect.

Within-diagnosis heterogeneity is substantial.

## Failure U — high IQ/ability = no neurodevelopmental disorder

Incorrect.

Ability and adaptive/executive function are separable.

## Failure V — neurodevelopmental = immutable

Incorrect.

Development, compensation, learning and environment alter manifestation.

## Failure W — gender stereotype = diagnostic rule

Incorrect.

Detection bias and phenotype heterogeneity prevent this.

# XVI. 90-question Neurodevelopmental Foundations Inference Gate

## A. Developmental history

1. When were the earliest relevant features observed?
2. Who observed them?
3. Were they present before major psychiatric episodes?
4. Were they present across developmental stages?
5. Did demands later increase?
6. Was external scaffolding present earlier?
7. Were features masked/compensated?
8. Are school/developmental records available?
9. Are retrospective reports consistent?
10. What uncertainty remains about onset?

## B. Current phenotype

11. What exact current difficulty is present?
12. Inattention?
13. Hyperactivity/restlessness?
14. Impulsivity?
15. Social-communication difference?
16. Restricted/repetitive behavior?
17. Sensory difference?
18. Executive difficulty?
19. Functional impairment?
20. Which features are absent?

## C. ADHD architecture

21. Was there childhood onset evidence?
22. Are symptoms persistent rather than episodic?
23. Are they present across settings?
24. Is there meaningful impairment?
25. Are symptoms developmentally inappropriate?
26. Could anxiety explain them?
27. Depression?
28. Sleep?
29. Substance/medication?
30. Medical/neurological condition?

## D. Autism architecture

31. Is there persistent social-communication/interaction evidence?
32. Is it developmental?
33. Are restricted/repetitive features present?
34. Is sensory evidence present but over-weighted?
35. Could social anxiety explain social avoidance?
36. Could trauma explain change?
37. Could language/pragmatic difficulty explain it?
38. Is intellectual/developmental level considered?
39. Could learned strategies mask signs?
40. Is masking being overinterpreted as proof?

## E. ADHD–autism overlap

41. Which features are clearly ADHD-like?
42. Which are clearly autism-like?
43. Which are overlapping/nonspecific?
44. Is the same manifestation being double counted?

# XVIII. Evidence-strength map

## High-confidence practical boundaries

1. ADHD and autism are developmental conditions;
2. onset and recognition are distinct;
3. adult diagnosis does not imply adult onset;
4. current ADHD criteria require childhood onset;
5. autism symptoms may become fully manifest later as demands increase;
6. autism features may be masked by learned strategies;
7. ADHD and autism can co-occur;
8. one overlapping symptom should not automatically be counted toward both;
9. executive dysfunction is not diagnostically specific;
10. neuropsychological tests do not provide a unique ADHD/autism biomarker;
11. no single autism tool should determine diagnosis;
12. multi-source developmental assessment is preferred;
13. ADHD-like symptoms have many psychiatric/medical/sleep mimics;
14. social difficulty alone does not establish autism;
15. screening scores do not establish diagnosis;
16. group-average cognitive profiles do not define an individual;
17. high cognitive ability does not exclude developmental impairment;
18. neurodevelopmental conditions are heterogeneous and change in manifestation across the lifespan.

## Supported but context-sensitive

1. compensation/scaffolding can delay clinically obvious impairment;
2. camouflaging can contribute to delayed autism recognition;
3. females/women may be under-recognized in some autism contexts;
4. ADHD/social impairment and autism/social impairment can arise through partly different pathways;
5. camouflaging is associated with poorer mental health in several studies;
6. executive difficulties contribute to impairment in subsets of ADHD/autism.

## Not established as universal

1. one executive-function signature for ADHD;
2. one executive-function signature for autism;
3. objective cognitive testing as a diagnostic separator;
4. camouflaging as uniquely autistic;
5. camouflaging as uniquely female;
6. camouflaging as a proven universal cause of mental-health difficulty;
7. genuinely de novo adult-onset ADHD as a settled common entity;
8. one social-behavior pattern distinguishing all ADHD from all autism;
9. one neurobiological marker sufficient for diagnosis.

## Remaining NDEV dependency

**NDEV-02 — Broader neurodevelopmental differential: intellectual developmental disorder/adaptive functioning, language and social-communication disorders, specific learning disorder, developmental coordination disorder, tic/Tourette spectrum, uneven developmental profiles, and cross-condition comorbidity/boundaries.**

Why required:

ADHD/autism alone do not cover the full neurodevelopmental domain.

Reliable general reasoning still requires distinguishing:

- intellectual ability from adaptive functioning;
- autism from language/pragmatic communication disorders;
- ADHD from specific learning problems;
- motor coordination from executive/motivational explanations;
- tics from compulsions/stereotypies/habits;
- broad developmental delay from uneven specific profiles.

This block should remain boundary-oriented rather than encyclopedic.

# XXI. Canonical working rule

**Neurodevelopmental inference requires evidence of developmental architecture, not merely current resemblance.**

Use:

`developmental history -> current phenotype -> cross-setting/context function -> demands/support/compensation -> measurement quality -> psychiatric/medical/sleep/substance alternatives -> co-occurrence -> narrow conclusion`.

Never:

`adult diagnosis -> adult onset`

`inattention -> ADHD`

`executive dysfunction -> ADHD`

`social difficulty -> autism`

`sensory sensitivity -> autism`

`masking -> autism`

`normal cognitive test -> no ADHD/autism`

`poor cognitive test -> diagnosis`

`one overlapping symptom -> two diagnoses`

`high ability -> no developmental disorder`

`neurodevelopmental -> immutable`.

## Source-boundary note

Official NIMH/CDC/WHO material is used for current classification and assessment boundaries.

Recent reviews are used to refine adult ADHD uncertainty, ADHD–autism overlap, executive-function specificity, and camouflaging.

The 2026 camouflaging meta-analysis supports a moderate association between autistic traits and camouflaging, not diagnostic specificity.

The current executive-function review supports broad group-average differences and substantial heterogeneity; it does not support an EF diagnostic fingerprint.

The ADHD–autism co-occurrence review is a targeted narrative review rather than a formal systematic review; its clinical overlap claims are therefore used as synthesis/support, not as a unique high-certainty source.

No source here is used to infer any individualized diagnosis.
