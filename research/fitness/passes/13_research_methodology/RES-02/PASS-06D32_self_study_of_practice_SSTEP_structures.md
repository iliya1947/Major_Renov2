# 3. Lineage and current naming

Self-study research emerged as a recognizable movement in teacher education in the early 1990s.

Current AERA history and 2025/2026 scholarship support the following lineage:

teacher educators systematically studying their own practice  
→ AERA Self-Study of Teacher Education Practices (S-STEP) SIG in 1993  
→ Castle Conference from 1996  
→ International Handbook in 2004  
→ dedicated journal / mature methodology community  
→ later extension from teacher education into broader professional-practice contexts.

Common labels include:

- Self-Study of Teacher Education Practices;
- S-STEP;
- S-STTEP in some literature;
- self-study research;
- self-study teacher research;
- self-study of teaching;
- self-study of practice;
- self-study of professional practice;
- S-SP.

### Project taxonomy decision

Use **Self-Study of Practice / S-STEP** as the parent family.

Store the author's exact label separately.

Do not treat every label as a separate methodology unless its architecture materially differs.

# 4. Canonical scientific object: self-in-practice

The defining object is not simply:

> the researcher as a person.

It is:

> **the practitioner-self in relation to professional practice, context, other people, assumptions, identity, and action.**

Canonical architecture:

practitioner/researcher  
+  
own professional practice as site of inquiry  
+  
self/identity/beliefs/assumptions made analytically visible  
+  
interaction with others and alternative perspectives  
+  
systematic evidence trail  
+  
professional learning / improved understanding or practice  
+  
public contribution beyond private reflection.

### Critical rule

Self-study is not:

> “I studied something that happened in my workplace.”

The researcher’s own **self-in-practice** must be part of the scientific object.

---

# 5. The “self” is relational, not isolated

S-STEP does not conceptualize the self as a sealed individual detached from context.

The self is studied in relation to:

- students;
- athletes;
- clients;
- colleagues;
- co-coaches;
- institutional constraints;
- curriculum;
- professional norms;
- identity;
- power;
- history of practice.

This is why interaction/collaboration is repeatedly treated as a defining methodological commitment.

### Project implication

Extract separately:

1. what aspect of self is examined;
2. what professional practice is examined;
3. which others constitute the practice context;
4. how those others' perspectives enter the inquiry.

# 6. LaBoskey’s five canonical characteristics

A widely used S-STEP quality architecture derived from LaBoskey identifies five characteristics.

## 6.1 Self-initiated and self-focused

The inquiry originates in a problem/tension/question of the practitioner’s own practice.

It is not primarily an external evaluator studying a practitioner.

## 6.2 Improvement-aimed

The inquiry aims to improve:

- practice;
- understanding of practice;
- professional learning;
- alignment between beliefs and actions.

“Improvement” should not be reduced to a numeric performance gain.

It may mean:

- better understanding;
- reframing;
- changed professional judgement;
- changed pedagogy;
- recognition of contradiction.

## 6.3 Interactive

The inquiry engages other perspectives.

Possible interaction:

- critical friend;
- collaborator;
- colleague;
- student;
- athlete;
- coach;
- participant;
- reader/professional community.

## 6.4 Multiple, primarily qualitative methods

Self-study has no single prescribed data-generation method.

Common sources include:

- reflective journals;
- field notes;
- lesson plans;
- coaching plans;
- video;
- audio;
- student/athlete work;
- artifacts;
- emails;
- portfolios;
- interviews;
- focus groups;
- critical-friend dialogue;
- recorded collaborative meetings;
- personal narratives;
- observation;
- documents.

## 6.5 Validation through exemplars / concrete evidence

Self-study claims should be grounded in concrete instances of practice rather than unsupported introspection.

This is frequently described as exemplar-based validation.

### Critical rule

These five characteristics are a methodological coherence architecture, not a rigid procedural checklist requiring every study to use identical methods.

# 7. Samaras Five Foci

Samaras organizes high-quality self-study around five complementary foci:

1. **personal situated inquiry**;
2. **critical collaborative inquiry**;
3. **improved learning**;
4. **transparent and systematic research process**;
5. **knowledge generation and presentation**.

This architecture adds two especially useful distinctions for the project.

## First

Private reflection is insufficient.

## Second

A self-study should generate and communicate knowledge beyond the researcher's private learning.

### Project representation

LaBoskey and Samaras are complementary rather than competing taxonomies.

Use:

- LaBoskey = foundational methodological characteristics;
- Samaras = practical research-design / quality foci.

---

# 8. Improvement is not synonymous with proving effectiveness

A self-study can ask:

- Why do my stated coaching beliefs conflict with my actual behavior?
- How did I learn to enact a new pedagogy?
- What changed in my professional judgement?
- How did critical dialogue expose assumptions in my teaching?
- How did my identity shape the way I coached?
- How did my practice evolve across an intervention-development process?

These are improvement/professional-learning questions.

They are not the same as:

> Did intervention X cause outcome Y?

### Critical rule

Self-study can document and interrogate practice change.

It does not, by itself, identify a causal treatment effect.

---

# 9. Public knowledge contribution is constitutive

The mature S-STEP literature repeatedly emphasizes a dual ambition:

1. improve/deepen the practitioner’s own professional learning;
2. contribute publicly inspectable knowledge to a professional field.

Vanassche & Kelchtermans characterize self-study as research that:

- improves practice;
- makes professional expertise explicit;
- validates/scrutinizes that expertise;
- contributes to the knowledge base of teacher education.

Fletcher & Ovens similarly emphasize bridging:

> personal understanding ↔ public professional understanding.

### Critical boundary

A private reflective diary can improve practice.

That alone does **not** make it research self-study.

Public research contribution requires some combination of:

- systematic inquiry;
- transparent evidence;
- analytic articulation;
- methodological accountability;
- presentation/publication for critique.

---

# 10. Reflection vs research self-study

Reflective practice is broader than self-study research.

A practitioner may reflect:

- informally after a session;
- privately in a journal;
- through supervision;
- in conversation with a colleague.

That can be professionally valuable without becoming research.

## Self-study requires additional architecture

- explicit research question/problem of practice;
- systematic evidence generation/collection;
- analytic process;
- interaction/criticality;
- transparent methodological account;
- claims grounded in data/examples;
- public knowledge contribution.

### Project rule

**reflection is necessary/common but not sufficient.**

Do not code:

> reflective journal

as:

> S-STEP methodology

unless the wider inquiry architecture is present.

---

# 11. Critical friendship

Critical friendship is one of the most characteristic tools in self-study.

Schuck & Russell describe the critical friend as someone who:

- acts as a sounding board;
- asks challenging questions;
- helps reframe events;
- offers alternative perspectives;
- participates in professional learning.

Current critical-friendship research stresses that the term should not be used superficially.

### Critical friend is not

- any co-author;
- any supportive colleague;
- an informal friend who agrees with the researcher;
- an external auditor by label alone.

### Strong critical-friend architecture should report

1. who the person is;
2. why they were selected;
3. relationship to the researcher;
4. what data/material they saw;
5. how often interaction occurred;
6. how they challenged assumptions;
7. how disagreement was handled;
8. how their input changed analysis or practice.

---

# 12. Critical friendship and objectivity

Critical friends do not magically make practitioner self-research “objective.”

Their value is better represented as:

- analytic challenge;
- alternative lens;
- disconfirming perspective;
- reframing;
- dialogical scrutiny;
- credibility/trustworthiness support.

### Critical rule

Do not translate:

> critical friend checked my interpretation

into:

> bias was eliminated.

Self-study remains situated and reflexive.

---

# 13. Is a named critical friend universally mandatory?

The literature strongly treats **interaction/collaboration** as a defining feature.

Critical friendship is one of the dominant mechanisms for achieving that interaction.

However, exact enactment varies:

- one external critical friend;
- internal collaborators;
- collaborative self-study group;
- students/colleagues as interactive partners;
- multiple layers of critical friendship.

### Project decision

Require evidence of **meaningful interaction / alternative perspective / collaborative criticality**.

Do **not** require the exact label “critical friend” as an absolute universal naming rule.

If authors claim critical friendship, extract how it was operationalized.

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

# 14. Multiple data sources

Self-study usually draws on more than one form of empirical material because the central risk is unchallenged self-account.

Common combinations:

journal  
+  
lesson/coaching artifacts  
+  
critical-friend dialogue  
+  
video/observation  
+  
student/athlete feedback.

The 2025 Kucera methodology guide used:

- narratives/creative writing;
- artifacts;
- critical-friend dialogue;
- journaling;
- developmental portfolio.

The 2024 physical-literacy study used:

- session protocols;
- discussions with another coach;
- scientist/stakeholder panel discussions;
- field observations/impressions;
- group interviews with children.

### Project rule

Multiple data sources strengthen self-study when they provide genuinely different windows on practice.

Five versions of the practitioner’s own retrospective narrative are not automatically five independent perspectives.

---

# 15. Triangulation in self-study

“Triangulation” can be useful but should not be interpreted mechanically.

Potential purposes:

- compare espoused belief with observed action;
- compare self-account with colleague/student perspective;
- compare contemporaneous record with later interpretation;
- inspect convergence and divergence;
- expose contradictions.

### Critical rule

The goal is not always to make all sources agree.

Disagreement can be analytically important.

Example:

> coach says they prioritize autonomy  
> athlete feedback suggests controlling behavior  
> video shows mixed patterns.

That contradiction may be the key self-study finding.

---

# 16. Method flexibility: self-study is methodology, not one method

Self-study does not prescribe one fixed:

- interview format;
- coding method;
- analytic procedure;
- data type;
- representation form.

It can borrow from:

- thematic analysis;
- content analysis;
- narrative inquiry;
- heuristic inquiry;
- arts-based methods;
- action research cycles;
- observation;
- quantitative descriptive measures.

### Project rule

Store separately:

1. **parent methodology** = Self-Study / S-STEP;
2. **data-generation methods**;
3. **analysis method**;
4. **secondary methodology**, if explicitly nested/hybrid.

This prevents errors such as:

> thematic analysis = design.

---

# 17. Primarily qualitative does not mean qualitative-only by logical necessity

LaBoskey's formulation emphasizes multiple, **primarily qualitative** methods.

Vanassche & Kelchtermans likewise describe a strong qualitative orientation.

Therefore the project routes S-STEP into the qualitative branch.

But a self-study may include:

- descriptive quantitative data;
- survey measures;
- student outcome indicators;
- performance metrics.

### Taxonomy rule

If quantitative data are added:

- retain Self-Study as parent practitioner methodology when self-in-practice remains the central scientific architecture;
- separately record the quantitative component;
- do not infer that the study becomes an RCT/cohort/impact design unless those design requirements are independently met.

---

# 18. Exemplar-based validation

LaBoskey’s “validation based in exemplars” is often misunderstood.

It does not mean:

> one vivid anecdote proves a general claim.

It means claims about practice should be made inspectable through concrete, sufficiently elaborated examples.

Possible exemplars:

- episode transcript;
- lesson segment;
- coaching interaction;
- artifact;
- journal passage;
- contrasting episodes;
- participant response;
- critical-friend exchange.

### Critical rule

Exemplar supports interpretation.

It does not create population representativeness.

---

# 19. Bullough–Pinnegar quality contribution

Bullough & Pinnegar developed quality guidance particularly for autobiographical forms of self-study.

Their central concern is the movement from:

> private/personal trouble

toward:

> publicly meaningful professional/social issue.

For project appraisal, their contribution supports checks on:

- honesty and vulnerability of stance;
- contextual specificity;
- connection between self and others;
- interpretive insight;
- professional/public significance;
- responsibility toward those implicated.

### Critical rule

Autobiographical depth is not an excuse for self-absorption.

The work must illuminate practice and its wider significance.

# 20. Trustworthiness architecture

Useful S-STEP trustworthiness checks include:

1. explicit self-study purpose;
2. transparent problem of practice;
3. self-in-practice made visible;
4. multiple/appropriate data sources;
5. contemporaneous data where possible;
6. meaningful interaction/collaboration;
7. critical-friend role operationalized;
8. analytic trail;
9. concrete exemplars;
10. contradictory/disconfirming material retained;
11. reflexivity about researcher role;
12. claims calibrated to context;
13. ethical treatment of implicated others;
14. public contribution beyond private reflection.

### Important

This is a **project appraisal router**, synthesizing established self-study principles.

It is not presented as one universally published checklist.

# 21. Improvement claim must be evidenced

A common weak structure is:

> I reflected → I improved.

That is not enough.

A stronger self-study asks:

- What changed?
- In what evidence?
- Compared with what prior pattern?
- Did others observe the change?
- Did the researcher’s interpretation change?
- Did practice change?
- Did the change persist?
- Are alternative explanations visible?

### Critical rule

Professional transformation is a legitimate self-study claim only when grounded in an evidence trail.

# 22. Identity and practice

One reason self-study is not reducible to action research is that it explicitly examines how the practitioner’s:

- identity;
- beliefs;
- assumptions;
- biography;
- values;
- positionality;

shape professional action.

A problem of practice may therefore be:

> Why do I enact a practice inconsistent with the pedagogy I say I value?

rather than only:

> Which new procedure works better?

This self↔practice relationship is central in Ovens–Fletcher's physical-education framing.

# 23. Self-study vs action research

The two families overlap heavily.

Both may be:

- practitioner-led;
- improvement-oriented;
- iterative;
- practice-based;
- qualitative;
- collaborative.

Feldman, Paugh & Mills argue the distinction lies more in methodology than in surface methods.

## Action research

Typical emphasis:

practice problem  
→ planning/action  
→ observation/data  
→ reflection  
→ next action cycle.

## Self-study

Defining emphasis:

self-in-practice  
→ practitioner identity/beliefs/actions made visible  
→ critical inquiry into the practitioner’s role  
→ professional learning/improvement  
→ public knowledge.

### Critical rule

Action-reflection cycles can occur inside self-study.

They are **not required** to define every S-STEP study.

Likewise:

> practitioner changed practice

does not automatically make the study self-study.

The self must be analytically foregrounded.

# 24. Self-study vs reflective practice

## Reflective practice

Can be:

- informal;
- private;
- unsystematic;
- non-research;
- without public dissemination.

## Self-study research

Requires a disciplined inquiry architecture:

- question/problem;
- evidence;
- analysis;
- interaction;
- transparency;
- contribution.

### Rule

Every self-study is strongly reflective.

Not every reflective practitioner is doing self-study research.

---

# 25. Self-study vs autoethnography

Both may involve:

- first person;
- researcher as data source;
- autobiography;
- reflexivity;
- identity;
- narrative;
- vulnerability.

But their default scientific targets differ.

## Autoethnography

self-experience  
→ cultural/social/institutional world.

Constitutive question:

> What does my experience reveal about culture/social life?

## Self-study of practice

self-in-practice  
→ professional action/learning/identity  
→ improved understanding/practice  
→ professional knowledge.

Constitutive question:

> What does systematic inquiry into myself-in-practice reveal about how I enact and can improve/understand professional practice?

### Project rule

Do not collapse S-STEP into autoethnography.

A study may legitimately be hybrid if it explicitly uses both.

---

# 26. Self-study vs Moustakas heuristic inquiry

PASS-06D26 already established the distinction.

## Heuristic inquiry

Scientific target:

- meaning/essence of a phenomenon personally experienced by the researcher;
- tacit knowing;
- self-dialogue;
- immersion/incubation;
- illumination;
- creative synthesis.

## Self-study

Scientific target:

- professional practice;
- practitioner-self within that practice;
- professional learning/improvement;
- public professional knowledge.

### Current direct sport example

The 2025 coach-developer study explicitly used:

- **Self-Study of Teaching Practice** as overarching methodology;
- **Heuristic Inquiry** as guiding method.

### Project rule

Preserve both layers rather than forcing one label to absorb the other.

---

# 27. Self-study vs “study of oneself”

A psychologist, physiologist or biomechanist can collect measurements on themselves.

Example:

- own HRV;
- own lifting performance;
- own blood glucose;
- own gait.

That is not automatically S-STEP.

S-STEP specifically requires the practitioner/professional-practice inquiry architecture.

### Rule

Self as participant ≠ Self-Study of Practice methodology.

This is especially important in fitness research where self-experimentation is common.

---

# 28. Self-study vs N-of-1 / single-case experimental design

N-of-1 / SCED asks causal or functional questions using repeated measurement and experimental manipulation.

Self-study asks practitioner-learning/practice questions.

A coach might do both:

- S-STEP to investigate how their coaching identity/practice evolves;
- N-of-1 to test whether a training intervention changes one athlete’s outcome.

### Critical rule

Same person / repeated observations do not make the designs equivalent.

---

# 29. Collaborative self-study

Self-study can be:

- individual practitioner with critical friends;
- paired;
- small collaborative group;
- larger professional learning community.

In collaborative self-study, multiple practitioners may each examine:

- own practice;
- shared practice;
- relational practice;
- collective professional learning.

### Critical rule

Multiple researchers ≠ collaborative self-study automatically.

They must jointly turn inquiry onto their own practice/self-in-practice.

---

# 30. Internal vs external critical friendship

The 2019 football-coaching / teacher-education study demonstrates a useful layered architecture.

## Internal

Practitioner-researchers challenge each other while both are inside the practice.

## External

A third person adds additional critical distance.

Potential value:

internal lived knowledge  
+  
external challenge.

### Project implication

Store:

- critical friend internal/external?;
- participant in practice?;
- data-generating role?;
- analytic role?;
- decision authority?

---

# 31. Data from students / athletes / clients

Self-study often includes data generated by people affected by the practitioner’s work.

Examples:

- student interviews;
- athlete feedback;
- child focus groups;
- learning artifacts;
- observation of responses.

These data serve to understand the practitioner’s practice.

### Critical boundary

If the research question shifts primarily to:

> What are participants’ experiences?

then another qualitative methodology may become the better parent design.

Self-study remains coherent only if the practitioner/self-in-practice remains central.

---

# 32. Ethics and implicated others

The researcher may be the central subject, but self-study can implicate:

- students;
- athletes;
- patients/clients;
- colleagues;
- supervisors;
- institutions.

Therefore:

> “I am studying myself”

does not automatically imply:

> no ethics issue exists.

The 2025 Kucera self-study reported formal institutional ethics approval.

The 2026 engineering self-study reported that its institution did not classify that specific project as human-subjects research requiring review.

### Project rule

Ethics status is jurisdiction/institution/design-specific.

Never infer exemption from the label “self-study.”

---

# 33. Power asymmetry

Sport/PE/coaching self-study has an especially important ethics issue when the practitioner is also:

- teacher grading students;
- coach selecting athletes;
- lecturer assessing trainees;
- clinician treating clients.

Participant feedback may be affected by:

- dependency;
- authority;
- fear of consequences;
- social desirability.

### Project extraction

Record:

1. practitioner authority over participants;
2. recruitment by practitioner?;
3. consent process;
4. who collected feedback;

# 43. What Self-Study / S-STEP can establish

Depending on implementation, it can support contextual claims about:

- practitioner learning;
- professional identity;
- contradictions between beliefs and practice;
- tacit assumptions;
- pedagogical/coaching judgement;
- professional decision-making;
- evolution of practice;
- relational dynamics in practice;
- how context constrains practice;
- how a practitioner makes sense of change;
- how critical dialogue changes interpretation;
- how a pedagogical innovation is enacted;
- transferable professional insights/hypotheses.

# 44. What Self-Study / S-STEP cannot establish by itself

It does not by itself estimate:

- population prevalence;
- incidence;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- average treatment effect;
- biological mechanism;
- causal mediation;
- comparative intervention efficacy;
- population distribution of practitioner types.

### Critical examples

> “My athletes responded positively after I changed my communication”

does not establish:

> this communication strategy causes better outcomes in athletes generally.

Likewise:

> “I became a more autonomy-supportive coach”

does not establish:

> athlete autonomy increased because of the coaching change

unless a separate design validly supports that causal claim.

# 45. Student/athlete outcome data inside self-study

Self-study can include outcome data.

But outcome data can play different roles:

## As feedback to practitioner learning

Example:

- student survey helps expose mismatch in teaching.

Parent design can remain S-STEP.

## As formal intervention-effect evidence

Then a separate experimental/quasi-experimental/observational design is required.

### Project rule

Do not let the presence of outcome metrics silently upgrade a self-study into an efficacy study.

# 46. Generalization / transferability

Self-study contributions usually move from:

specific situated professional inquiry  
→ explicit account of mechanism/meaning/tension in practice  
→ reader/professional critique  
→ possible transfer or theoretical development.

This is not statistical generalization.

### Better language

- transferable insight;
- professional knowledge;
- conceptual contribution;
- analytic resonance;
- hypothesis generation;
- practice theory.

### Avoid

- “all coaches”;
- “all PE teachers”;
- “this always works”;

unless supported by other evidence.

# 47. Knowledge accumulation problem

One historical criticism of self-study is that many highly contextual single self-studies can be difficult to accumulate into a broader knowledge base.

Vanassche & Kelchtermans' review and later field discussions emphasize the dual ambition of:

- local improvement;
- theoretical/public contribution.

### Project implication

When extracting self-study evidence, separate:

1. local practitioner finding;
2. proposed professional concept;
3. cross-study pattern;
4. claim actually replicated across settings.

Do not automatically convert repeated narrative themes into universal laws.

---

# 48. Tension: relevance vs rigor

Self-study is valuable because it is close to practice.

That same closeness creates methodological risk.

Potential strengths:

- rich contextual access;
- tacit knowledge;
- continuous observation;
- authentic practice problem.

Potential risks:

- confirmation bias;
- selective memory;
- defensiveness;
- self-justification;
- power over participants;
- poorly separated professional and research aims.

### Quality response

Not “remove the self.”

Instead:

- expose the self;
- collect systematic evidence;
- invite challenge;
- retain contradiction;
- show analytic decisions;
- calibrate claims.

---

# 49. Tension: effectiveness vs insight

A self-study may report a changed practice without proving that the change objectively improved participant outcomes.

Therefore distinguish:

## Improvement of understanding

> I now understand why my teaching repeatedly produced this tension.

## Change in practice

> I changed my feedback structure.

## Participant response

> students reported more clarity.

## Causal effectiveness

> the new feedback structure caused better learning.

Only the last requires a causal design.
