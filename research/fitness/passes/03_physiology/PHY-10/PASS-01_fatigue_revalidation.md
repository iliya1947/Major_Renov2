## 3. Definition of fatigue

For this project, fatigue is not defined as “feeling tired.”

The operational core is:

**exercise/task-induced reduction in the ability to produce the force, power or performance required by the task.**

Useful distinctions:

### Performance fatigability
Objective reduction in performance capacity.

Examples:
- lower maximal force;
- lower repetition velocity;
- lower power;
- lower evoked twitch force.

### Perceived fatigability
Subjective sensations/effort that can influence task continuation.

### Task failure
The point at which the required task can no longer be maintained.

Project rule:

**fatigue can develop before task failure; task failure is an endpoint, not a synonym for fatigue.**

---

## 4. Neuromuscular fatigue

Neuromuscular fatigue is an umbrella construct describing exercise-induced reduction in the neuromuscular system's capacity to produce force/power.

It can contain both:

- central components;
- peripheral components.

Resistance-exercise evidence indicates that acute fatigue is often more consistently visible in:

- contractile function;
- voluntary performance;
- afferent/perceptual responses

than in simple muscle-activation measures.

Project rule:

**neuromuscular fatigue is multidimensional and cannot be reduced to one EMG metric.**

---

## 5. Central fatigue

Central fatigue refers to an exercise-induced reduction in voluntary neural drive/activation to the muscle.

Potential contributors include changes at:

- supraspinal level;
- spinal/motoneuron level;
- afferent feedback interacting with motor output;
- motor-command/perceptual regulation.

Possible experimental measures include:

- voluntary activation from twitch interpolation;
- TMS-based measures;
- motor-unit behavior;
- task-specific neural outputs.

Boundaries:

1. central fatigue is not synonymous with mental fatigue;
2. perceived effort is not identical to voluntary activation;
3. surface EMG amplitude alone cannot quantify central fatigue.

Project rule:

**central fatigue = reduced voluntary neural contribution to force production, measured with an appropriate method.**

Detailed mechanisms → NEU.

---

## 6. Peripheral fatigue

Peripheral fatigue refers to performance loss caused by processes distal to central motor drive, especially within the neuromuscular junction–muscle fiber–contractile apparatus chain.

Potential sites include:

- sarcolemmal/T-tubular excitability;
- excitation–contraction coupling;
- SR Ca²⁺ release/handling;
- myofilament Ca²⁺ sensitivity;
- cross-bridge force production;
- metabolic/ionic state.

Evoked twitch/torque reduction after exercise is useful evidence of peripheral/contractile impairment, but it does not by itself uniquely identify the molecular mechanism.

Project rule:

**peripheral fatigue is a location class, not one mechanism.**

---

## 7. Central vs peripheral contribution is task dependent

No fixed “central vs peripheral percentage” applies to all exercise.

The contribution varies with:

- contraction intensity;
- contraction duration;
- dynamic vs isometric task;
- muscle mass;
- exercise selection;
- environmental/systemic state;
- proximity to task failure;
- testing method.

Current resistance-exercise systematic evidence suggests contractile impairment and afferent/perceptual responses are often prominent, while simple muscle-activation measures change less consistently.

Project rule:

**do not assign one universal central/peripheral fatigue ratio.**

---

## 8. Metabolic fatigue

“Metabolic fatigue” is not a single-metabolite diagnosis.

High ATP turnover causes changes including:

- phosphocreatine depletion;
- inorganic phosphate increase;
- ADP changes;
- H⁺/pH disturbance;
- substrate utilization;
- interactions with ion transport and Ca²⁺ handling.

These can impair:

- cross-bridge force/power;
- myofibrillar Ca²⁺ sensitivity;
- SR Ca²⁺ release;
- membrane excitability.

Project rule:

**metabolic disturbance is one family of peripheral fatigue mechanisms, not a synonym for lactate accumulation.**

---

## 9. Excitation–contraction failure

Fatigue can reduce force even when neural activation reaches the muscle.

Important E–C coupling mechanisms include:

- altered membrane/T-tubule excitability;
- reduced voltage-sensor/RyR1-linked Ca²⁺ release;
- reduced SR Ca²⁺ availability/release;
- altered myofilament Ca²⁺ sensitivity.

The current 2026 E–C coupling review retains impaired Ca²⁺ release/handling as a mechanism relevant to fatigue.

A 2026 experimental paper further supports task-dependent intracellular Ca²⁺ handling as an important determinant of fatigue behavior.

Project rule:

**fatigue can occur between the action potential and cross-bridge force even without a primary decline in central drive.**

---

## 10. Substrate depletion

### Phosphocreatine

During high-rate ATP turnover, PCr falls rapidly and contributes to the inability to maintain the initial ATP-resynthesis rate.

PCr recovery is relatively rapid compared with delayed muscle-damage recovery, helping explain why short inter-set rests can restore some performance without restoring every fatigue domain.

### Glycogen

A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis found a single RT session significantly reduced vastus-lateralis glycogen, with greater depletion associated with:

- more sets;
- longer session duration;
- training status/intensity context.

However:

**glycogen depletion is not the sole immediate cause of force failure in a short resistance set.**

It is more appropriately treated as one substrate constraint on repeated high-volume/high-intensity work and subsequent recovery, especially when starting stores are low.

Detailed fuel physiology → BIO/NUT.

---

## 11. Metabolite accumulation

### Inorganic phosphate

Pi has strong mechanistic support as a fatigue-relevant metabolite.

Potential actions include:

- direct impairment of cross-bridge force;
- reduced myofibrillar Ca²⁺ sensitivity;
- impaired SR Ca²⁺ release/availability.

Current 2025 literature continues to support a major role for Pi in vivo.

### H⁺ / acidosis

The older claim “lactic acid causes fatigue” is rejected.

Current evidence supports a more nuanced statement:

- severe intracellular acidosis can reduce force/shortening velocity/power;
- H⁺ effects interact with Pi and other ionic/metabolic changes;
- lactate itself is not a simple toxic fatigue molecule;
- the contribution of acidosis varies by task, temperature, fiber and exercise intensity.

Project rule:

**Pi/H⁺ can contribute materially, but no single metabolite universally explains fatigue.**

---

## 12. Ionic disturbances

Fatiguing contractions disturb trans-sarcolemmal ion gradients, especially:

- K⁺;
- Na⁺;
- Cl⁻;
- Ca²⁺.

Relevant mechanisms include:

- K⁺ efflux during repeated action potentials;
- Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase compensation;
- altered membrane excitability;
- ClC-1/KATP channel effects under metabolic stress;
- interaction between ionic and acid-base changes.

Current human evidence supports exercise-induced K⁺ disturbances as physiologically meaningful for performance, while modern reviews reject a simplistic “extracellular K⁺ alone causes fatigue” model.

Project rule:

**ionic fatigue is an interacting excitability problem, not a one-ion threshold.**

---

## 13. Reduction in neural drive

Voluntary neural drive can fall during/after fatiguing exercise.

Possible drivers include:

- supraspinal/spinal adjustments;
- inhibitory/afferent feedback;
- motor-unit discharge changes;
- high perceived effort;
- protective/task-regulatory strategies.

But:

- neural drive can remain high while peripheral force capacity falls;
- changes in EMG amplitude do not map uniquely to neural drive;
- different tasks show different central-fatigue profiles.

Project rule:

**reduced neural drive is one possible limiting mechanism, not the universal endpoint of fatigue.**

---

## 14. Local vs systemic fatigue

### Local fatigue
Dominant impairment within the exercised muscle/MTU or its neural control.

Examples:
- reduced local twitch force;
- metabolite/ion disturbance;
- local E–C coupling impairment.

### Systemic fatigue
Broader whole-person performance impairment involving multiple systems/large muscle mass and potentially:

- central/perceptual load;
- cardiorespiratory strain;
- whole-body thermal/metabolic state;
- generalized performance reduction.

Critical boundary:

**local vs systemic describes distribution/scale; central vs peripheral describes mechanism/site. They are not the same taxonomy.**

A local task can contain central fatigue; a whole-body task can contain strong peripheral fatigue.

---

## 15. Acute vs accumulated fatigue

### Acute fatigue
Develops during or after a specific bout and resolves over minutes-to-days depending on the outcome.

### “Accumulated fatigue”
A practical label for persistent/recurrent performance impairment when recovery between stressors is incomplete.

Current evidence does **not** support imagining a single acute metabolite progressively accumulating across normal RT sessions.

The 2021 review found evidence for a unique physiological “fatigue accumulation” state after RT to be equivocal.

Persistent impairment can instead reflect combinations of:

- unresolved neuromuscular performance loss;
- muscle damage;
- repeated high training stress;
- inadequate sleep/nutrition;
- psychological/perceptual stress;
- illness/other systemic load.

Project rule:

**accumulated fatigue is an observed longitudinal state requiring defined outcomes; do not infer a hidden fatigue substance.**

Detailed monitoring → TST/REC.

---

## 16. Task dependency

Task dependency is a core fatigue principle.

The limiting mechanism changes with:

- required force;
- duration;
- contraction mode;
- movement speed;
- muscle mass;
- duty cycle;
- rest;
- blood flow;
- environment;
- skill/coordination.

Current 2026 evidence shows intracellular Ca²⁺ handling can differentiate task-dependent fatigue behavior even when contraction mode itself does not explain the difference.

Broader task-failure research likewise shows central/peripheral/perceptual contributions depend on intensity domain.

Canonical rule:

**the question is not “what causes fatigue?” but “what limits this task under these conditions?”**

---

## 17. Effects on force, velocity and power

Fatigue commonly causes:

- lower force capacity;
- lower repetition velocity;
- lower power;
- longer repetition duration;
- altered RFD.

Resistance-exercise systematic evidence shows impairments grow especially with:

- longer sets;
- closer proximity to failure;
- larger volume;
- greater density.

Current 2026 experimental data similarly show neuromuscular, perceptual and metabolic fatigue increase as RT is performed closer to momentary failure.

Project rule:

**force/velocity loss are useful fatigue outcomes, but their magnitude is protocol and task specific.**

---

## 18. Effects on technique / movement strategy

Fatigue can change kinematics and kinetics.

The 2025 systematic review of squat, bench press and deadlift found:

- velocity consistently declines with increasing intensity/fatigue;
- power and joint mechanics vary more across studies/individuals;
- lifters may adopt different compensatory mechanical strategies.

Therefore:

**fatigue can alter technique, but there is no single universal “fatigue technique pattern.”**

Detailed biomechanical consequences → MEC/TEC.

---

## 19. Recovery kinetics

Recovery is multidimensional.

Different outcomes can normalize on different timelines:

- metabolites/PCr — relatively rapid;
- force/velocity — minutes to days depending on protocol;
- central/voluntary activation — task dependent;
- evoked twitch/contractile function — can remain impaired after voluntary performance partially recovers;
- soreness/damage markers — can peak later and persist longer.

Resistance-exercise evidence shows:

- training closer to failure, larger volume and higher density generally increase acute fatigue;
- failure-oriented protocols can delay mechanical recovery into the 24–48 h window compared with matched/lower-effort work;
- exercise selection also changes recovery kinetics.

Project rule:

**there is no universal recovery time because there is no single fatigue variable.**

---

## 20. One-marker recovery failure

A person is not proven recovered because one of the following normalized:

- lactate;
- glycogen estimate;
- soreness;
- CK;
- bar velocity;
- CMJ;
- perceived readiness;
- voluntary activation;
- twitch force.

Project rule:

**recovery must be defined against the target function and time point.**

This extends PHY-05 and routes practical monitoring to TST/REC.

## 21. What actually limits repeated force production?

Integrated model:

`required task output`
vs
`available neural drive + membrane/E–C coupling + contractile capacity + bioenergetic/ionic state + task mechanics`.

Repeated force production declines when one or more components can no longer sustain the required output.

The dominant limiter can move during the same task.

Examples:

- early high-power repetitions → PCr/rapid energetic and neural constraints;
- longer near-failure set → larger Pi/H⁺/ionic/E–C/afferent burden;
- repeated sets → incomplete recovery + substrate/contractile/perceptual effects;
- delayed postexercise → muscle damage/contractile impairment may dominate rather than acute metabolites.

Project rule:

**fatigue is an emergent task-specific limitation, not a single molecule or organ.**

## 23. Established project-level model

Fatigue is represented as:

`task demand > momentary sustainable neuromuscular capacity`

where sustainable capacity emerges from:

- voluntary neural drive;
- membrane excitability;
- E–C coupling/Ca²⁺ handling;
- cross-bridge/contractile state;
- metabolic/ionic state;
- substrate availability;
- muscle–tendon/task mechanics;
- perceptual/systemic context.

The relative contribution changes with the task and across time.

## 24. Training-relevant implications

1. **Fatigue is not one mechanism.**
2. **Task failure is not the definition of fatigue.**
3. **Central and peripheral fatigue can coexist.**
4. **Surface EMG alone cannot partition central vs peripheral fatigue.**
5. **Pi, H⁺ and ionic disturbance can impair force, but lactate is not a universal fatigue toxin.**
6. **Glycogen is depleted by RT, especially with more sets/longer sessions, but is not the sole cause of short-set failure.**
7. **E–C coupling/Ca²⁺ handling can limit force independently of central drive.**
8. **Near-failure, high-volume and high-density work generally produces more acute fatigue.**
9. **Fatigue alters force/velocity and may alter technique/strategy.**
10. **Local/systemic and central/peripheral are different classification axes.**
11. **Accumulated fatigue must be inferred from repeated outcome impairment, not from assumed metabolite accumulation.**
12. **Recovery time must be tied to the exact function being monitored.**
13. **Programming should manage stimulus–fatigue tradeoffs rather than maximize fatigue.**
