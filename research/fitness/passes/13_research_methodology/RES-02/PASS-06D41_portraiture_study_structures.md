# 3. Lineage

Portraiture was developed by sociologist **Sara Lawrence-Lightfoot**.

Her own methodological account connects its development to:

- social science;
- sociology;
- educational research;
- ethnographic observation;
- biography/life-story work;
- phenomenological and narrative traditions;
- aesthetic/artistic portrait-making.

The foundational sequence is:

**The Good High School (1983)  
→ subsequent portrait-writing practice  
→ Lawrence-Lightfoot & Davis, The Art and Science of Portraiture (1997)  
→ later adaptations across education, health, social research and participatory inquiry.**

### Critical rule

Portraiture is not simply:

> “write a vivid participant profile.”

It is a defined qualitative methodology with its own epistemic, relational, analytic and compositional architecture.

---

# 4. Canonical scientific target

Portraiture seeks to capture:

- richness;
- complexity;
- dimensionality;
- dynamics;
- subtlety

of human experience and organizational/social life in context.

Its core purpose can be represented as:

> construct a systematic, empirically grounded and aesthetically composed portrait that conveys actors’ perspectives while making visible the wider social, cultural, historical and relational world in which those perspectives are lived.

### Canonical project representation

systematic empirical engagement  
+  
context  
+  
researcher/participant voice relation  
+  
relationship as knowledge-producing  
+  
emergent themes  
+  
aesthetic whole  
+  
authenticity / resonance  
+  
critical-generous interpretation  
+  
contextual rather than population-causal claims.

---

# 5. Art and science are deliberately joined

Lawrence-Lightfoot explicitly describes Portraiture as an attempt to combine:

- systematic empirical description;
- aesthetic expression;
- humanistic sensibility;
- scientific rigor.

### Critical rule

The aesthetic element is not post-hoc decoration.

The scientific and aesthetic dimensions are intended to work together through:

- observation;
- interpretation;
- narrative construction;
- composition;
- writing.

### Project implication

A study does not become Portraiture because it is:

> qualitative research written beautifully.

The five-element methodological architecture must be materially present.

---

# 6. Portraiture pushes against a simple ethnography/fiction divide

Portraiture is empirical.

But it also accepts that:

- selection;
- sequencing;
- composition;
- metaphor;
- voice

shape the final account.

### Critical rule

This does **not** mean:

> facts are optional because the final text is aesthetic.

Portraiture still depends on:

- systematic empirical engagement;
- careful description;
- transparent interpretation;
- fidelity to the actors/context;
- authenticity.

---

# 7. Search for goodness

One of Portraiture’s most distinctive commitments is a **search for goodness**.

Historically this developed partly in response to deficit/pathology-oriented social science.

The portraitist intentionally looks for:

- strength;
- resilience;
- competence;
- worth;
- possibility;
- what works;
- what people/institutions preserve under difficulty.

### Critical rule

“Goodness” is a methodological lens.

It is not equivalent to:

- praise;
- positive psychology;
- success-only sampling;
- advocacy for the participant.

---

# 8. Goodness ≠ perfection

Lawrence-Lightfoot’s own descriptions are explicit that goodness is:

- imperfect;
- changing;
- historically situated.

A “good” institution can contain:

- tension;
- contradiction;
- failure;
- conflict;
- inequity.

### Critical rule

A strong portrait can be simultaneously:

- appreciative;
- admiring;
- critical.

---

# 9. Generous and critical stance

The portraitist’s stance is best represented as:

**critical + generous.**

This means:

- search for strength without romanticization;
- attend to imperfection without reducing actors to pathology;
- seek complexity rather than a hero/villain binary.

### Sport relevance

A portrait of a coach can examine:

- care;
- relational skill;
- power;
- institutional constraints;
- harmful norms

without classifying the coach as simply:

> good or bad.

---

# 10. Five defining elements

The foundational methodology identifies five interrelated elements:

1. **context**;
2. **voice**;
3. **relationship**;
4. **emergent themes**;
5. **aesthetic whole**.

### Critical rule

These are not five sequential procedural steps.

They operate across:

- design;
- fieldwork;
- interpretation;
- composition.

---

# 11. Context

Context is constitutive.

Relevant context can include:

- physical setting;
- geography;
- history;
- culture;
- institutional routines;
- social norms;
- values;
- power structures;
- aesthetic/visual environment;
- temporal moment.

### Critical rule

Context is not a short demographics paragraph placed before the findings.

It helps explain:

> why actions, meanings and relationships take the forms that they do.

---

# 12. Context can itself be analytically active

Examples in sport:

- locker-room architecture;
- travel schedules;
- selection systems;
- training venue;
- coaching hierarchy;
- federation policy;
- parent-care arrangements;
- gender norms;
- sport culture.

### Project rule

Store contextual elements that materially shape the portrait.

Do not reduce portraiture to individual psychology.

---

# 13. Voice

Portraiture does not try to eliminate the researcher’s voice.

The portraitist is:

- interpreter;
- witness;
- selector;
- writer;
- research instrument.

Participant voices remain central, but the final portrait is not a transcript dump.

### Critical rule

The researcher’s voice should be visible enough to make interpretation accountable, but not so dominant that participant perspectives disappear.

---

# 14. Voice is relational

The final text may contain:

- participant voice;
- researcher voice;
- institutional/cultural voices;
- silences;
- dissonant voices.

### Project implication

Store:

- whose voice is foregrounded?;
- whose voice is mediated?;
- whose voice is missing?;
- how researcher perspective shaped the narrative?

---

# 15. Researcher as primary instrument

Lawrence-Lightfoot explicitly recognizes the self as a central research instrument.

The portraitist brings:

- biography;
- theoretical interests;
- values;
- sensitivities;
- relationships;
- interpretive lens.

### Critical rule

This is not a license for unchecked subjectivity.

The methodological response is:

- reflexivity;
- transparency;
- systematic empirical grounding;
- attention to dissonance;
- relational accountability.

---

# 16. Perspective-taking

Portraiture includes an active process of perspective-taking.

The portraitist moves among:

- insider perspectives;
- outsider interpretation;
- participant meanings;
- institutional structure;
- particular experience;
- wider significance.

### Critical rule

Perspective-taking ≠ claiming total access to another person’s interior world.

The portrait is a relational interpretation.

---

# 17. Relationship

Relationship is constitutive of knowledge.

Lawrence-Lightfoot’s current description emphasizes:

- access;
- trust;
- intimacy;
- reciprocity;
- responsibility;
- boundary negotiation;
- knowledge construction.

### Critical rule

The relationship is not merely:

> rapport needed to extract better data.

It is part of how knowledge is produced.

---

# 18. Symmetry and reciprocity

Portraiture tends toward relationships that are:

- more reciprocal;
- more dialogic;
- less objectifying

than strongly detached research models.

### Caveat

Research relationships are never automatically equal.

The researcher may still control:

- question;
- publication;
- selection;
- interpretation;
- institutional resources.

### Project rule

Record enacted reciprocity rather than assuming symmetry from methodology label.

---

# 19. Boundary negotiation

Portraiture can create intimacy.

This increases ethical complexity.

The researcher must negotiate:

- closeness;
- professional boundaries;
- emotional disclosure;
- participant expectations;
- future contact;
- representation.

### Critical rule

Empathy does not eliminate role/power differences.

---

# 20. Search for goodness as relationship practice

Lawrence-Lightfoot links trust/rapport partly to the search for goodness.

The portraitist approaches actors expecting to find:

- competence;
- dignity;
- complexity;
- worth.

### Ethical value

This can reduce objectification and deficit framing.

### Risk

It can become:

- idealization;
- conflict avoidance;
- selective reporting.

Therefore the critical-generous balance must be explicit.

---

# 21. Empirical sources

Portraiture can draw on multiple sources such as:

- interviews;
- observation;
- participant observation;
- informal conversations;
- documents;
- letters;
- diaries;
- records;
- photographs;
- artifacts;
- institutional materials;
- researcher fieldnotes;
- impressionistic records.

### Critical rule

No single data source defines Portraiture.

The defining architecture is methodological/interpretive/compositional.

---

# 22. Sustained engagement is common but not one fixed threshold

Lawrence-Lightfoot emphasizes careful:

- watching;
- listening;
- interacting

over time.

However portraits can differ substantially in:

- duration;
- number of encounters;
- setting;
- intimacy.

### Project rule

Duration is metadata, not an absolute validity cutoff.

But a very thin empirical base has a higher burden to justify a rich portrait.

---

# 23. Emergent themes

Themes do not simply arise automatically from data.

Portraiture combines:

- systematic attention;
- interpretation;
- synthesis;
- convergence;
- contrast.

The portraitist seeks the central story while remaining alert to:

- contradictions;
- dissonance;
- silence;
- complexity.

---

# 24. Five common modes for constructing emergent themes

Lawrence-Lightfoot & Davis are widely operationalized through five modes of synthesis/convergence/contrast:

1. **repetitive refrains**;
2. **resonant metaphors**;
3. **institutional and cultural rituals**;
4. **triangulation**;
5. **revealing patterns**, including convergence and dissonance.

### Project status

This five-mode analytic structure is strongly established in Portraiture applications.

It should not be treated as a rigid universal coding algorithm.

---

# 25. Repetitive refrains

Repetitive refrains are recurring:

- words;
- phrases;
- meanings;
- actions;
- concerns.

### Critical rule

Frequency alone does not establish importance.

A refrain gains meaning through:

- context;
- relation;
- repetition;
- resonance.

---

# 26. Resonant metaphors

Portraiture attends to:

- symbols;
- metaphors;
- vernacular;
- images.

These can condense wider meanings.

### Sport example

A coach repeatedly describing the team as:

> family

may reveal:

- care;
- loyalty;
- obligation;
- hierarchy;
- boundary problems.

### Critical rule

Metaphor is an analytic clue, not literal proof.

---

# 27. Institutional and cultural rituals

Routines can reveal:

- norms;
- values;
- power;
- identity.

Sport examples:

- pregame rituals;
- weigh-ins;
- selection meetings;
- training punishment;
- team meals;
- injury disclosure;
- parent-care arrangements.

### Scientific value

Ritual can connect:

individual story  
↔ institutional culture.

---

# 28. Triangulation

Portraiture can use multiple empirical sources to examine convergence.

Examples:

- interview;
- observation;
- documents;
- artifacts;
- fieldnotes.

### Critical rule

Triangulation does not mean:

> one objective truth is mechanically proven by three sources.

It strengthens the portrait by testing:

- coherence;
- contradiction;
- contextual interpretation.

---

# 29. Dissonance is not noise

Portraiture does not require all voices to converge.

Dissonant evidence can be analytically important.

Examples:

- coach says athlete welfare comes first;
- observed schedule leaves no recovery;
- athlete interview reports fear of disclosure.

### Project rule

Do not delete dissonant material to make the portrait more aesthetically tidy.

---

# 30. Aesthetic whole

The aesthetic whole integrates:

- context;
- voice;
- relationship;
- themes

into a coherent final composition.

### It concerns

- form;
- rhythm;
- sequence;
- balance;
- metaphor;
- narrative structure;
- inclusion/exclusion.

### Critical rule

The aesthetic whole is not merely:

> “write a good Discussion.”

It is the culminating methodological act of composing the portrait.

---

# 31. Composition is an analytic act

Decisions about:

- what appears first;
- which scene anchors the portrait;
- which quotation is retained;
- which silence is shown;
- where contradiction appears

change meaning.

### Project rule

Treat writing/composition as part of analysis.

---

# 32. Selection and omission are power

Every portrait is selective.

The portraitist decides:

- what becomes central;
- what is omitted;
- what is juxtaposed;
- which metaphor organizes the piece.

### Critical rule

Aesthetic coherence must not erase contradictory evidence.

### Project extraction

Record whether authors discuss:

- selection;
- omission;
- dissonance;
- researcher influence.

---

# 33. Authenticity

Portraiture foregrounds **authenticity** rather than conventional notions of reliability/validity as its primary internal standard.

The aim is a portrait that is:

- credible;
- believable;
- resonant;
- contextually convincing.

### Critical rule

Authenticity does not mean:

> “it feels true, therefore it is rigorous.”

Authenticity should remain grounded in:

- empirical detail;
- interpretive transparency;
- context;
- relationship;
- systematic analysis.

---

# 34. Resonance

Portraiture aims for resonance with:

- actors/participants;
- readers/audiences;
- portraitist/researcher.

### Useful interpretation

Resonance is a form of:

- recognition;
- plausibility;
- felt/analytic coherence.

### Critical rule

Reader emotional reaction alone is not scientific validation.

---

# 35. Authenticity vs factual reproduction

An authentic portrait need not be:

- a verbatim transcript;
- a complete chronological record.

It may be analytically composed.

But it must not falsely present:

- invented events;
- invented quotations;
- fabricated participant characteristics

as empirical fact.

### Project rule

Distinguish:

**analytic composition**  
from  
**fictional invention**.

---

# 36. Portraiture can portray more than a person

Lawrence-Lightfoot’s own corpus includes portraits of:

- individuals;
- institutions;
- relationships;
- developmental stages;
- processes;
- concepts.

### Taxonomy consequence

Do not define:

> portrait = participant profile.

The unit can be broader.

# 38. Process / relationship / concept portraits

Portraiture can also target:

- a process;

Potential portraits:

- retirement transition;

A portrait need not have one bounded human subject.

# 39. Full Portraiture vs portrait-style representation

This distinction is mandatory for RES-02.

## Full Portraiture methodology

Requires coherent enactment of the Lawrence-Lightfoot architecture:

- context;
- voice;
- relationship;
- emergent themes;
- aesthetic whole;
- researcher/portraitist positionality;
- authenticity/resonance;
- empirical-aesthetic integration.

## Portrait-style / CNF portrait

May be:

- one representational format;
- one analytic writing mode;
- nested inside another methodology.

### Critical rule

Do not code every paper containing the word:

> portrait

as full Portraiture methodology.

---

# 40. Creative nonfiction portraiture

Creative nonfiction can transform empirical material into:

- analytically informed narrative;
- vivid scene;
- participant-centered story.

### But

CNF portraiture may retain a different parent methodology.

Example:

**Raudasoja & Ryba 2026**

explicitly use:

- Narrative Inquiry;
- creative-nonfiction portraiture.

### Project route

`Narrative Inquiry parent + CNF portraiture analytic/representational layer`.

Do not overwrite the parent as Lawrence-Lightfoot Portraiture unless the full architecture is explicitly enacted.

---

# 41. Single-participant portrait

A portrait can be built around one participant.

This allows:

- depth;
- contextual layering;
- narrative coherence.

### Claim limit

One person’s portrait does not establish:

- prevalence;
- typicality;
- population distribution.

---

# 42. Composite portraits

Some qualitative traditions create composites by blending material across participants.

### Important boundary

Composite writing can be used alongside portraiture-like representation, but it changes:

- traceability;
- identity;
- authenticity;
- ownership.

### Project rule

If a composite is used, record explicitly:

- how many participants contributed;
- whether quotations were merged;
- whether chronology was altered;
- whether characteristics were combined;
- why;
- how participants were informed.

---

# 43. Composite ≠ individual portrait

A composite may protect confidentiality or illuminate a shared pattern.

But it cannot be presented as:

> the actual biography of one participant

if it blends multiple people.

### Critical rule

Researcher should disclose the construction status.

---

# 44. Ethics of recognizability

Even pseudonymized portraits can be highly identifiable because they contain:

- context;
- biography;
- relationships;
- distinctive events;
- quotations;
- institutional details.

### Sport risk

A portrait of:

- one national coach;
- one elite athlete;
- one rare sport role

may be obvious to insiders.

### Project fields

- deductive disclosure risk?;
- contextual masking?;
- participant approval?;
- altered details?;
- risk of distorted meaning?

---

# 45. Altering details for confidentiality

Current sport CNF portraiture explicitly reports altering identifying details where needed.

### Critical trade-off

Changing details can protect participants but may also alter:

- context;
- chronology;
- meaning.

### Project rule

Record:

- what kinds of details were altered;
- whether core meaning was retained;
- whether construction was disclosed.

---

# 46. Relationship creates ethical obligations after data collection

Because Portraiture values relational intimacy and reciprocity, publication may affect:

- participant self-understanding;
- relationships;
- institution reputation;
- professional identity.

### Critical rule

Ethics is not complete at consent.

Representation itself is an ethical act.

---

# 47. Participant review / member reflection

Participant feedback can help assess:

- factual errors;
- recognizability;
- resonance;
- harmful exposure.

### Caveat

Participant approval is not an absolute criterion of truth.

A participant may disagree with a critical interpretation.

### Project rule

Record:

- what participants reviewed;
- what changes they could request;
- what the researcher retained despite disagreement;
- rationale.

# 48. Portraiture vs Narrative Inquiry

## Narrative Inquiry

D7 core target:

- story;
- temporality;
- plot;
- identity;
- telling/performance;
- cultural narrative.

## Portraiture

Adds a distinctive methodology-level architecture:

- explicit context;
- researcher voice;
- relationship;
- emergent-theme synthesis;
- aesthetic whole;
- authenticity;
- goodness/strength orientation.

### Critical rule

Narrative Inquiry can produce portraits.

Portraiture can use stories.

They are not synonyms.

---

# 49. Portraiture vs phenomenology

## Phenomenology

Targets:

- lived experience;
- experiential meaning/structure.

## Portraiture

Targets:

- contextualized person/institution/process/relationship/concept;
- empirical + aesthetic whole.

### Example

Ingman 2017 explicitly uses:

**phenomenological portraiture**.

### Project route

`phenomenology orientation + Portraiture architecture`

when both are genuinely enacted.

Do not force exclusive classification.

---

# 50. Portraiture vs ethnography

Portraiture shares with ethnography:

- observation;
- context;
- culture;
- field relationships;
- thick description.

### Portraiture differs through

- explicit art/science synthesis;
- portraitist voice;
- search for goodness;
- five-element architecture;
- aesthetic whole;
- authenticity/resonance standard.

### Critical rule

Portraiture may borrow ethnographic techniques without becoming conventional ethnography.

---

# 51. Portraiture vs Case Study

Case Study asks:

> What is the bounded case and why is it analytically informative?

Portraiture asks:

> How can a context-rich, relational, empirically grounded and aesthetically composed portrait reveal complexity and meaning?

### Overlap

A study can be:

- case-like;
- bounded;
- site-specific.

### Critical rule

Boundedness alone does not establish Portraiture.

---

## A/r/tography

## Portraiture

Core:

- portraitist;

- authenticity.

### Critical rule

Both blend art and research.

But their epistemic architectures are materially different.

# 53. Portraiture vs broad arts-based research

Broad arts-based research remains:

**heterogeneous non-atomic umbrella.**

Portraiture qualifies as a separate route because it has:

- stable lineage;
- stable defining elements;
- characteristic analytic/compositional logic;
- distinct quality concept.

### Taxonomy decision

Portraiture is **atomic enough** for RES-02.

---

# 54. Portraiture vs creative nonfiction

Creative nonfiction is often:

- analytic writing;
- representation;
- arts-based mode.

It does not require:

- context/voice/relationship/emergent themes/aesthetic whole architecture.

### Rule

CNF can be nested inside:

- Narrative Inquiry;
- autoethnography;
- Portraiture;
- other qualitative approaches.

No standalone CNF parent needed.

# 55. Direct sport exemplar — Finnish parenthood 2026

Raudasoja & Ryba 2026 studied parenthood in Finnish elite/competitive sport.

Architecture:

- life-story interviews;
- Narrative Inquiry;
- care ethics;
- reproductive justice;
- creative-nonfiction portraiture.

They created three individual portraits.

### Important

The authors explicitly state the portraits were:

- not transcript reproductions;
- not fictional accounts;
- analytically crafted narratives.

### Taxonomy lesson

# 60. Questions well suited to full Portraiture

Examples:

- How does one high-performance training environment embody both care and performance pressure?
- How does a coach’s relational practice appear when biography, institutional culture and athlete relationships are considered together?
- What does “goodness” look like in a rehabilitation service while retaining contradictions and imperfections?

# 61. Poor-fit questions

Portraiture is usually a poor parent design for:

- injury incidence;
- prevalence;
- treatment efficacy;
- diagnostic sensitivity;
- prediction;
- hypertrophy effect;
- dose-response estimation;
- causal mediation.

Those require other designs.

- local strengths;
- contradictions;

- rituals;

# 63. What Portraiture cannot establish by itself

Portraiture does not establish:

- population prevalence;
- incidence;
- average treatment effect;
- comparative efficacy;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism;
- causal mediation;
- representativeness.

### Critical rule

A resonant portrait is not a frequency estimate.

# 64. “In the particular, the general” requires careful interpretation

Portraiture often works from a rich particular toward broader recognition.

### Appropriate transfer

Readers may gain:

- conceptual insight;
- analytic recognition;
- transferable questions;
- theoretical propositions.

### Inappropriate transfer

Do not infer:

> because one coach/institution was portrayed this way, most coaches/institutions are this way.

2. Using “portrait” only as a metaphor.
3. Beautiful writing without empirical grounding.
4. Empirical themes followed by decorative prose.
5. Ignoring context.
6. Removing researcher voice while claiming Portraiture.
7. Researcher voice dominating participant perspective.
8. Treating relationship only as recruitment access.
9. Assuming empathy eliminates power.
10. Search for goodness becoming praise.
11. Goodness becoming positivity-only selection.
12. Contradictions omitted to preserve an attractive portrait.
13. Dissonant evidence removed for narrative coherence.
14. Emergent themes treated as frequency counts only.
15. Metaphor treated as literal evidence.
16. Aesthetic whole treated as literary polish only.
17. Authenticity reduced to “felt true.”
18. Reader emotion treated as validation.
19. Composite participant presented as one real biography.
20. Altered details not disclosed.
21. Contextual identifiability ignored.
22. Participant review treated as absolute truth criterion.
23. Narrative Inquiry mislabeled as full Portraiture because portraits are written.
24. Phenomenological study mislabeled as Portraiture because it describes one person.
25. Case study mislabeled as Portraiture because it is in-depth.
