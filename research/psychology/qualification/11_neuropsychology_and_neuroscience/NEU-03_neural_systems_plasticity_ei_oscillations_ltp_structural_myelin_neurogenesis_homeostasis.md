## Purpose

NEU-01 established method-specific inference boundaries for imaging, electrophysiology, lesions, stimulation, connectivity, and decoding.

NEU-02 established that neurochemical signals are receptor-, projection-, region-, timescale-, and state-specific, and that PET binding/occupancy/release and pharmacological effects are noninterchangeable quantities.

NEU-03 addresses systems plasticity:

**What can excitation/inhibition measures, neural oscillations, LTP/LTD, structural MRI changes, myelination, adult neurogenesis, and homeostatic plasticity legitimately establish about learning and psychological function, and where do measurement proxies or mechanistic narratives outrun the evidence?**

## Core rule

**Neural plasticity is a family of processes across multiple scales, not one mechanism or one scalar capacity. Excitation/inhibition, oscillatory activity, synaptic strength, structural MRI, myelin, neurogenesis, and network homeostasis measure different levels. A valid inference must specify the biological process, timescale, spatial scale, measurement proxy, perturbation, and behavioral consequence. Learning can recruit several plasticity mechanisms, but no single plasticity marker is equivalent to learning, memory, adaptation, or psychological change.**

Canonical reasoning:

\`define psychological process -> specify neural scale/timescale -> identify the directly measured variable -> separate measurement proxy from biological process -> distinguish correlation from perturbational/necessity evidence -> model state, region, cell type, and network alternatives -> test persistence and behavioral specificity -> integrate complementary methods -> state only the narrowest plasticity claim supported\`.

# Excitation / inhibition

## 1. E/I balance is a family of concepts

"Excitation/inhibition balance" can refer to different quantities:
- excitatory versus inhibitory synaptic currents;
- conductances;
- firing output;
- excitatory/inhibitory cell activity;
- neurotransmitter/metabolite concentrations;
- spectral or criticality-derived proxies;
- network stability.

These are not interchangeable.

## 2. Local E/I balance can vary by scale

A circuit can show:
- balanced net population activity;
while
- individual neurons receive unbalanced inputs;
or
- different dendritic compartments show different E/I relations.

A single global ratio cannot summarize every relevant scale.

## 3. E/I can vary across region and state

E/I relationships can differ by:
- cortical area;
- layer;
- sleep/wake state;
- development;
- task;
- neuromodulatory state;
- disease.

"Brain E/I balance" without localization/timescale is underspecified.

## 4. E/I is dynamic

E/I can change over:
- milliseconds through synaptic conductances;
- seconds/minutes through network state;
- hours/days through plasticity;
- development through circuit maturation.

A static measurement cannot establish every timescale.

## 5. More excitation is not inherently better or worse

Increasing excitation can:
- improve responsivity in one regime;
- destabilize network activity in another.

Increasing inhibition can:
- sharpen selectivity;
- impair transmission if excessive.

Behavior depends on operating regime.

## 6. E/I balance is not equivalent to equal excitation and inhibition

"Balance" can mean matched dynamic regulation rather than:
- 50:50 current;
- equal transmitter concentration;
- equal cell counts.

The appropriate quantity depends on the model.

## 7. Glutamate concentration ≠ excitatory synaptic drive

Glutamate participates in:
- synaptic transmission;
- metabolism;
- glutamate–glutamine cycling.

Bulk glutamate/Glx measured by MRS is not a direct count of excitatory postsynaptic currents.

## 8. GABA concentration ≠ inhibitory synaptic drive

MRS GABA reflects a bulk tissue/metabolic pool.

It does not directly quantify:
- phasic inhibitory current;
- tonic inhibition;
- interneuron firing;
- receptor conductance.

## 9. MRS Glx/GABA+ is an E/I proxy, not literal synaptic ratio

Concurrent human work often operationalizes an MRS "E/I ratio" as:
\`Glx / GABA+\`.

But:
- Glx combines glutamate + glutamine;
- GABA+ includes co-edited macromolecules;
- both include nonsynaptic pools.

Therefore:
**MRS E/I is a model-derived neurochemical index**.

## 10. 2026 concurrent EEG–MRS evidence

A 2026 Journal of Neuroscience study:
- n=15 sleeping humans;
- concurrent occipital EEG and MRS;
- Glx/GABA+ measured in early visual cortex;
- multiple EEG-derived E/I algorithms.

Several EEG-derived measures showed positive between-subject associations with MRS-derived E/I, especially alpha-band measures.

This supports some shared variance across modalities.

## 11. The EEG–MRS correspondence was not a universal dynamic equivalence

In the same study:
- within-person associations were weaker and inconsistent;
- sample was small;
- recordings were during sleep;
- authors explicitly cautioned against treating either measure as ground truth.

Therefore:
**EEG-E/I ≠ validated continuous measurement of synaptic E/I**.

## 12. 1/f slope is not uniquely E/I

Aperiodic spectral slope can relate to multiple properties:
- synaptic timescales;
- population activity;
- filtering;
- arousal;
- E/I-related parameters.

An association with E/I does not make 1/f slope a unique E/I meter.

## 13. EEG E/I algorithms are model-dependent

Different algorithms use:
- aperiodic exponent;
- oscillatory amplitude relationships;
- criticality-based fluctuations;
- neural-mass-model fits.

Agreement among them is not guaranteed.

## 14. E/I biomarkers require exact validation target

A proposed biomarker must specify whether it predicts:
- synaptic physiology;
- MRS metabolites;
- behavior;
- clinical outcome.

Correlation with one proxy does not validate all interpretations.

# Oscillations and synchrony

## 15. Oscillation measurement must be separated from oscillatory process

The 2025 Trends in Cognitive Sciences framework distinguishes:
- field-potential measurement;
- underlying neural process;
- electric-field causal effects;
- computational role.

A spectral peak is a measured pattern, not automatically a functional mechanism.

## 16. Oscillations can be functional, epiphenomenal, or mixed

Some rhythms can:
- organize spike timing;
- coordinate excitability;
- influence plasticity windows.

Other rhythmic signatures can emerge as by-products of:
- recurrent network dynamics;
- common input;
- waveform shape.

Function requires process-specific evidence.

## 17. Frequency band ≠ psychological function

Theta, alpha, beta, gamma labels summarize frequency ranges.

Each band can appear in:
- multiple regions;
- multiple tasks;
- multiple states.

Therefore:
**theta = memory**
or
**alpha = inhibition**
is too broad.

## 18. Frequency boundaries are not universal

Band definitions vary across:
- studies;
- species;
- individuals;
- age;
- brain regions.

Individual peak frequencies can shift.

Fixed bins can mix different processes.

## 19. Oscillatory power is not process intensity

Power can change because of:
- more rhythmic events;
- larger amplitude;
- longer duration;
- waveform shape;
- background/aperiodic activity.

A power increase does not uniquely specify mechanism.

## 20. Burst structure matters

Oscillations can occur as transient bursts rather than sustained sinusoids.

Averaged spectral power can hide:
- burst probability;
- duration;
- timing;
- phase.

Temporal-event analysis can change interpretation.

## 21. Aperiodic and periodic components should be separated

Broadband 1/f-like activity can alter apparent band power.

Without spectral parameterization:
- aperiodic slope/offset changes
can appear as
- oscillatory band changes.

## 22. Phase is model-dependent near weak oscillations

Phase estimates become unstable when:
- signal amplitude is low;
- waveform is nonsinusoidal;
- filtering dominates.

"Phase" is not equally meaningful at all times.

## 23. Phase locking ≠ communication

Stable phase relation between signals can arise from:
- common input;
- source mixing;
- volume conduction;
- shared stimulus timing.

Phase coupling is evidence of statistical organization, not automatically information transfer.

## 24. Coherence ≠ causal influence

Coherence measures frequency-specific statistical dependence.

It does not establish:
- direction;
- mediation;
- synaptic communication.

Directed influence needs additional assumptions/perturbation.

## 25. Volume conduction can inflate sensor-level synchrony

One electrical source can contribute to multiple EEG sensors.

This can create:
- zero-lag correlation;
- coherence;
- phase-locking

without two interacting neural populations.

## 26. Source reconstruction does not eliminate leakage

Source-space EEG/MEG reduces but does not eliminate:
- spatial leakage;
- inverse-problem uncertainty.

Connectivity remains estimator- and pipeline-dependent.

## 27. Leakage-resistant metrics trade one error for another

Metrics such as:
- imaginary coherence;
- PLI;
- wPLI

reduce sensitivity to zero-lag mixing.

But they can miss genuine:
- near-zero-lag interactions;
- low-SNR coupling.

No connectivity metric is universally artifact-free.

## 28. Cross-frequency coupling is also vulnerable to artifacts

Phase–amplitude or phase–phase coupling can arise from:
- waveform nonsinusoidality;
- harmonics;
- filtering;
- transient events;
- common drive.

Cross-frequency statistics require surrogate/null tests and waveform controls.

## 29. Gamma is particularly artifact-sensitive in scalp recordings

High-frequency activity can be contaminated by:
- muscle;
- microsaccades;
- line noise.

Scalp gamma claims need careful artifact rejection.

## 30. Oscillatory communication remains a testable mechanism

A 2026 source-localized MEG study found that:
- oscillatory power;
- inter-areal phase coherence

in alpha, beta, and high-gamma bands tracked inferred communication, with region-specific heterogeneity.

This supports oscillatory communication models.

It does not make every coherence result a direct communication measure.

## 31. Spatial heterogeneity matters

The 2026 MEG study found the communication–oscillation relationship varied across regions.

Thus:
**one frequency = one routing mechanism across the brain**
is invalid.

## 32. Causal oscillation claims need perturbation

Stronger evidence can come from:
- rhythmic stimulation;
- phase-specific perturbation;
- closed-loop intervention;
- invasive manipulation.

But stimulation can also have:
- nonspecific effects;
- network propagation.

Perturbation must target the proposed rhythm/mechanism.

# Synaptic plasticity

## 33. Synaptic plasticity is a family of changes

Synaptic plasticity includes:
- short-term facilitation/depression;
- LTP;
- LTD;
- STDP;
- behavioral-timescale plasticity;
- homeostatic scaling;
- presynaptic changes;
- postsynaptic changes.

No one mechanism defines "plasticity."

## 34. LTP is not one molecular mechanism

The 2026 Physiological Reviews synthesis emphasizes:
- multiple induction protocols;
- presynaptic and postsynaptic forms;
- region/synapse-specific mechanisms.

Classical CA3–CA1 LTP is only one well-studied instance.

## 35. Classical CA3–CA1 LTP has a specific mechanism

Typical NMDAR-dependent LTP involves:
- glutamate;
- postsynaptic depolarization;
- NMDAR-mediated Ca2+ influx;
- CaMKII;
- increased AMPAR function/number.

This mechanism should not be generalized to every synapse.

## 36. Different synapses use different plasticity rules

Some forms of LTP/LTD can depend on:
- presynaptic NMDARs;
- voltage-gated calcium channels;
- metabotropic receptors;
- endocannabinoids;
- neuromodulators.

"Hebbian rule" does not specify the molecular implementation.

## 37. STDP is not a universal timing rule

Spike-timing plasticity depends on:
- cell type;
- dendritic location;
- neuromodulatory state;
- firing pattern;
- synapse.

The classic pre-before-post/post-before-pre rule is not universal.

## 38. Behavioral-timescale plasticity expands the timing window

Plasticity can be induced over behavioral timescales beyond millisecond STDP.

This matters for learning rules involving:
- delayed outcomes;
- eligibility traces;
- plateau potentials.

Synaptic learning rules are diverse.

## 39. Metaplasticity changes the plasticity rule itself

Prior activity can change:
- threshold;
- magnitude;
- direction;
- duration

of later LTP/LTD.

Thus plasticity depends on history.

## 40. LTP is strongly linked to learning and memory

Decades of evidence support LTP involvement in:
- learning;
- memory;
- development.

The 2026 review describes substantial mechanistic evidence across animals and some human preparations.

This is strong but not identity.

## 41. LTP ≠ learning

Learning is a behavioral/computational change.

LTP is a synaptic physiological change.

Learning can involve:
- multiple synapses;
- multiple plasticity forms;
- systems consolidation;
- neuromodulation;
- structural/myelin changes.

Therefore:
**LTP observed -> learning mechanism proven**
is too strong.

## 42. LTP ≠ memory trace by definition

The 2026 review notes that unequivocally proving LTP as memory encoding remains challenging.

Relevant evidence includes:
- correlation;
- occlusion;
- blocking;
- erasure;
- reinstatement.

One association is insufficient.

## 43. Necessary/sufficient synaptic plasticity can be memory-specific

A 2025 mouse study found potentiation of specific basolateral-amygdala engram inputs was:
- induced by context fear learning;
- necessary for recall;
- sufficient under specific optogenetic/biochemical manipulations to establish an association.

This is strong causal evidence for that circuit/task.

It does not establish:
**all memories are stored by this mechanism**.

## 44. LTD ≠ forgetting

LTD can support:
- refinement;
- reversal;
- learning;
- depotentiation;
- circuit reconfiguration.

Behavioral forgetting has multiple mechanisms.

## 45. Synaptic strengthening can be maladaptive

Plasticity can support:
- adaptive learning;
- pathological sensitization;
- maladaptive habits;
- seizure-related network change.

"More plasticity" is not automatically better.

# Hebbian and homeostatic plasticity

## 46. Hebbian plasticity is positive-feedback-like

Associative mechanisms can amplify synapses correlated with activity.

Without stabilizing constraints, unconstrained Hebbian change could destabilize networks.

## 47. Homeostatic plasticity provides negative-feedback regulation

Homeostatic mechanisms can adjust:
- synaptic strength;
- intrinsic excitability;
- inhibitory signaling;
- receptor trafficking

to preserve workable activity regimes.

## 48. Homeostatic plasticity is not one mechanism

Examples include:
- synaptic scaling;
- changes in intrinsic excitability;
- presynaptic homeostatic potentiation/depression;
- inhibitory plasticity;
- metaplasticity-like regulation.

Synaptic scaling is one family.

## 49. Synaptic scaling and LTP/LTD can share molecular machinery

The 2026 review of Hebbian/homeostatic plasticity notes overlapping mechanisms involving:
- AMPAR trafficking;
- scaffolding;
- intracellular signaling.

Similar molecules do not imply identical computations.

## 50. Hebbian and homeostatic plasticity differ in sign, scale, and timescale

They can respond differently to activity:
- associative strengthening/weakening;
- compensatory stabilization.

But the separation is not absolute.

Mechanisms can interact.

## 51. Homeostasis does not require one fixed firing-rate set point

Neural systems can regulate:
- ranges;
- distributions;
- context-dependent operating regimes.

A "set point" is a model, not necessarily one immutable number.

## 52. Homeostatic restoration can be local or global

Compensation can occur at:
- single synapse;
- dendritic branch;
- neuron;
- circuit.

A network-level stable outcome can arise from different local adjustments.

## 53. Homeostatic mechanisms can affect E/I balance

Changes in:
- AMPARs;
- GABA receptors;
- intrinsic excitability

can shift measured E/I proxies.

Therefore E/I and homeostasis are dynamically linked.

## 54. 2026 TNF review illustrates glial involvement

Current evidence describes glial TNF signaling as contributing to homeostatic plasticity through:
- AMPAR insertion;
- GABA_A receptor regulation

in specific experimental contexts.

Homeostasis is not purely neuronal.

## 55. Stability and plasticity are complementary

A functional network must:
- change enough to learn;
- remain stable enough to preserve function.

Homeostatic regulation can enable, constrain, or reshape learning.

It is not simply "anti-plasticity."

# Structural plasticity

## 56. Structural plasticity spans multiple biological processes

Possible mechanisms include:
- dendritic spine formation/elimination;
- dendritic remodeling;
- axonal remodeling;
- synaptogenesis;
- glial changes;
- vascular changes;
- extracellular-space changes;
- myelin changes.

One MRI metric cannot identify all of them.

## 57. Structural MRI is a mesoscopic proxy

T1-weighted morphometry estimates:
- cortical thickness;
- volume;
- surface area;
- tissue classification.

It does not directly count:
- neurons;
- synapses;
- dendrites.

## 58. Gray-matter volume change ≠ neuronal growth

Apparent GMV can change through:
- tissue water;
- blood flow;
- vascular effects;
- myelin-related contrast;
- segmentation.

A GMV increase should not be called "new gray matter."

## 59. Cortical thickness is algorithmic/anatomical boundary estimation

Thickness depends on:
- tissue contrast;
- segmentation;
- reconstruction;
- software.

It is not a direct histological ruler.

## 60. Rapid MRI changes challenge simple cellular narratives

A 2026 systematic review of 30 human studies found MRI-detectable structural changes within:
- minutes to <2 hours

after learning, exercise, stress, or pharmacological manipulation.

Such speed makes it especially important to consider:
- fluid;
- hemodynamic;
- metabolic

contributions.

## 61. Rapid structural signal can be real without identifying mechanism

A reproducible T1/diffusion change can show:
**experience-related tissue-property change**.

It does not specify whether the cause is:
- neurite remodeling;
- glia;
- water redistribution;
- vascular effects.

## 62. Perfusion can contaminate morphometric interpretation

The 2026 systematic review reports that combined perfusion/fMRI studies indicate some apparent GMV changes may reflect:
- cerebral blood flow;
- extracellular fluid.

Multimodal control is important.

## 63. Diffusion MRI is sensitive but nonspecific

Mean diffusivity, FA, and related metrics reflect:
- membrane barriers;
- orientation;
- extracellular space;
- edema;
- axon/myelin properties.

A diffusion change is not one cellular process.

## 64. Learning-related microstructural MRI can occur quickly

The 2026 review of learning-related microstructure reports:
- short-training changes in restricted diffusion/local tissue volume;
- longer-training changes in task-relevant gray/white matter.

The behavioral relationship remains inconsistent across studies.

## 65. Structural MRI pipelines are not interchangeable

A 2026 systematic review of 111 studies found morphometric agreement varied across:
- software;
- versions;
- regions;
- processing choices.

High correlation could coexist with systematic disagreement.

## 66. Software update can change measured anatomy

Pipeline/version changes can alter:
- cortical thickness;
- volume;
- normalized percentiles;
- downstream conclusions.

Longitudinal work should keep pipeline/version controlled.

## 67. Computational repeatability ≠ anatomical validity

A pipeline can be internally reproducible yet systematically biased relative to anatomy.

Reliability, agreement, and validity are distinct.

# Myelin and white-matter plasticity

## 68. Myelin is dynamic across life

The 2024 Nature Neuroscience review, corrected in 2025, describes:
- ongoing myelin turnover;
- remodeling of mature myelin;
- activity/experience-related modulation.

Myelin is not a permanently fixed sheath after development.

## 69. Myelin supports more than conduction speed

Myelin and oligodendroglia contribute to:
- conduction timing;
- metabolic support;
- circuit synchronization;
- axonal health.

"Myelin = insulation only" is incomplete.

## 70. Adaptive myelination is a plausible learning mechanism

Animal work supports activity-dependent:
- oligodendrogenesis;
- sheath formation/remodeling

during learning and experience.

This can alter circuit timing and function.

## 71. Human myelin-learning inference remains more indirect

Human evidence relies heavily on:
- diffusion MRI;
- magnetization transfer;
- myelin water imaging;
- relaxometry.

These are proxies with varying specificity.

## 72. FA ≠ myelin

Fractional anisotropy is influenced by:
- fiber orientation;
- axon density;
- crossing fibers;
- edema;
- myelin.

A training-related FA increase cannot be labeled myelination without convergent evidence.

## 73. No single MRI myelin marker is uniquely specific

A systematic review/meta-analysis validating MRI against histology found correlations for several markers, with meta-analytic R² values spanning roughly:
- .26 to .82

depending on modality/metric.

Methodological heterogeneity prevented selection of one universally best MRI myelin measure.

## 74. Quantitative myelin biomarkers can converge but remain distinct

A 2026 study found strong correlations among several qMRI myelin-related markers across tissue types.

The authors still concluded further histological validation is needed for biological specificity.

Convergence among MRI markers ≠ direct histology.

## 75. Mature myelin remodeling and de novo myelination are distinct

Plasticity can involve:
- new oligodendrocytes/sheaths;
- changes in existing sheaths;
- turnover.

A single MRI signal cannot usually separate them.

## 76. Myelin change can be adaptive or maladaptive

The 2026 review emphasizes that myelination dynamics can support cognition but can also participate in:
- neurological;
- psychiatric;
- injury-related

pathology.

"More myelin" is not universally better.

# Adult hippocampal neurogenesis

## 77. Adult human hippocampal neurogenesis has been methodologically controversial

Conflicting results have arisen from:
- fixation;
- postmortem delay;
- antigen retrieval;
- marker specificity;
- tissue quality;
- cell annotation;
- sequencing sensitivity.

Older null and positive findings should be interpreted in this context.

## 78. Immature-neuron markers are not automatically birthdating

Markers such as DCX can be expressed under conditions that complicate:
**marker-positive cell = newly born neuron**.

Multiple markers and lineage evidence strengthen inference.

## 79. 2025 Science evidence identified proliferating progenitors

A 2025 Science study combined:
- single-nucleus RNA sequencing;
- Ki67-based proliferation evidence;
- computational localization

and identified proliferating neural progenitor cells in adult human dentate gyrus.

This strengthens evidence for adult neurogenic capacity.

## 80. 2026 Nature multiomic evidence further strengthens cellular evidence

A 2026 Nature study analyzed:
- 355,997 nuclei

from young adults, cognitively healthy older adults, SuperAgers, preclinical pathology, and Alzheimer's disease.

It identified:
- neural stem cells;
- neuroblasts;
- immature granule neurons.

## 81. Existence ≠ cognitive mechanism

The 2026 Nature paper explicitly states that the relevance of adult human hippocampal neurogenesis to cognition remains unknown.

Therefore:
**adult neurogenesis exists -> human memory depends on it**
is not established.

## 82. Progenitor proliferation ≠ mature functional neuron integration

To establish functional neurogenesis requires more than proliferation:
- differentiation;
- maturation;
- survival;
- circuit integration;
- functional contribution.

Postmortem cellular evidence does not directly establish all steps.

## 83. Neurogenesis rate/magnitude remains difficult to quantify

Even with stronger cellular evidence, uncertainty remains about:
- absolute rate;
- lifespan trajectory;
- functional significance.

Human evidence is less experimentally direct than animal evidence.

## 84. Reduced neurogenesis markers in disease ≠ disease mechanism by itself

Disease-related differences can be:
- cause;
- consequence;
- compensatory response;
- correlated pathology.

Causal intervention evidence is needed.

# Cross-scale plasticity

## 85. Synaptic and structural plasticity can interact

Synaptic changes can precede:
- spine stabilization;
- circuit remodeling;
- myelination.

But temporal ordering varies.

One scale should not be assumed to cause the next without evidence.

## 86. Plasticity can be gated by neuromodulators

NEU-02 systems such as:
- dopamine;
- acetylcholine;
- norepinephrine;
- serotonin

can alter whether/when synaptic changes occur.

Neuromodulator presence is not itself the memory trace.

## 87. Plasticity is constrained by metabolic and homeostatic processes

A 2026 review proposes a hierarchical framing:
- energetic feasibility;
- homeostatic stability;
- neuromodulatory gating;
- experience-dependent plasticity.

This is a useful integrative model, not a proven universal hierarchy.

## 88. More plasticity is not always adaptive

Excessive/poorly regulated plasticity can contribute to:
- unstable networks;
- maladaptive learning;
- pathological sensitization.

Adaptive function requires controlled plasticity.

## 89. Neural plasticity ≠ psychological change

A neural measure can change without measurable:
- behavior;
- cognition;
- subjective experience.

A psychological claim needs psychological evidence.

## 90. Psychological change ≠ one identified plasticity mechanism

Behavior can improve through:
- strategy;
- attention;
- motivation;
- learning;
- network reweighting;
- multiple cellular mechanisms.

No single plasticity marker should be assumed.

## 91. Convergent evidence strengthens mechanism

A strong plasticity claim can combine:
- behavioral change;
- physiological timing;
- molecular/cellular evidence;
- perturbation;
- longitudinal persistence.

Convergence across genuinely complementary methods reduces ambiguity.

## 94. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- E/I balance is scale-, region-, state-, and method-dependent rather than one global scalar;
- MRS Glx/GABA+ and EEG-derived indices are proxies, not direct synaptic E/I ground truth;
- oscillation measurements must be separated from underlying causal processes;
- coherence/phase locking are statistical dependencies vulnerable to source mixing/common input;
- LTP/LTD are diverse synaptic mechanisms strongly implicated in learning/memory but not identical to them;
- Hebbian and homeostatic plasticity are complementary and partly overlapping;
- structural MRI changes do not uniquely identify cellular structural plasticity;
- diffusion/FA and myelin-sensitive MRI measures are biologically nonspecific to varying degrees;
- adult human hippocampal neurogenesis now has stronger cellular/multiomic evidence, but its cognitive function remains unresolved;
- network stability and plasticity coexist.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- some EEG-derived E/I metrics share variance with MRS Glx/GABA+ in early visual cortex;
- oscillatory power/phase relationships can contribute to communication/routing in specific networks;
- specific synaptic potentiation can be necessary/sufficient for specific memories in animal circuits;
- rapid human MRI tissue-property changes can occur within minutes/hours of experience;
- activity-dependent myelin remodeling can support learning in animal models;
- human myelin-related MRI can track tissue changes with imperfect histological specificity;
- adult human dentate gyrus contains proliferating progenitors/immature neuronal populations;
- homeostatic plasticity can stabilize networks through receptor/excitability changes.

### Not established as universal
- one E/I biomarker;
- one frequency–function dictionary;
- one oscillatory communication mechanism for all networks;
- one LTP/LTD learning rule;
- one fixed homeostatic set point;
- one MRI metric that directly identifies synapses/dendrites/myelin;
- one human training-related MRI change that proves adaptive myelination;
- a quantified universal rate of adult human hippocampal neurogenesis;
- adult human neurogenesis as an established mechanism of memory;
- greater neural plasticity as universally beneficial.
