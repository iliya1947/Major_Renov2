# WOM-03.2 — Women's sexual psychology: desire, arousal, context and individual variation
Date: 2026-11-25. Bounded research note complete; WOM-03 IN_PROGRESS. Nonclinical descriptive and conditional inference; not a universal female response model or clinical qualification.

## Question and dependencies
Distinguish general sexual motivation, person-specific desire, spontaneous and responsive experience, subjective and physiological arousal, intimacy, sexual satisfaction, behavior, intention and freely given consent. How do these vary within women and over time, and how do relational and contextual associations differ from causal claims? Reopened the final sections of WOM-03.1 and WOM-02.3 at head b52d22eba1c8cc4276b2d22f30e0a43dacb86b40; not full rereads. Retain REL-01, WOM-01, IND-02/03 and INT-02 boundaries. Competing accounts include general motivation, partner-specific appraisal, stress and resources, actual opportunities, role and social norms, developmental and biological factors, reporting/measurement and their interactions.

## Evidence
Targeted public searches 2026-11-25: gender differences sex drive meta-analysis; desire variability daily diary; women's sexual response model endorsement; subjective-genital concordance; intimacy and desire; women's household labor and desire; recent demographic and partner-specific desire studies. Selected primary and synthesis evidence with counterexamples, not systematic or exhaustive.

E1. Frankenbach et al. (2022), Sex Drive: Theoretical Conceptualization and Meta-Analytic Review of Gender Differences. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000366 . Complete abstract at author institution inspected; PubMed opening blocked. 211 studies, 856 effects, N=621,463; g=.69 (95% CI .58–.81), men higher in dispositional sexual motivation; adjusted for modeled response bias g=.54. Combines cognitive, affective and behavioral indicators; not a pure measure of partner-specific desire. Adjustment depends on assumptions; no causal partition. Conley & Yang (2024) https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000412 and authors' reply https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000444 (indexed abstracts) debate contextual experiences, gender norms and reporting. Authors acknowledge social conditions may matter even without measured moderation. Not independent new estimates or evidence of a fixed biological gap. Full methods not audited.

E2. Does Sexual Desire Fluctuate More Among Women than Men? (2023), https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-022-02525-y . Publisher HTML abstract, selected methods/results and general discussion inspected. Study 1: three waves over 13 years, 5,562 observations; greater net variability among women, beta=.18 (95% CI .08–.28), no significant difference in instability. Study 2: 112 mixed-sex couples, 6,656 observations over seven days; net variability difference nonsignificant, beta=-.08 (CI -.35–.19). Study 3: 255 individuals, 4,667 observations over seven days; no significant difference in variability. Only two of 19 tested contextual associations differed by sex across short-term studies; partner-directed anger and fatigue stronger negative correlates for women in Study 2, fatigue result not reproduced in Study 3. General vs partner-specific desire and sampling timescale differ. Nulls not equivalence. Three widely separated long-term observations cannot describe fine-grained dynamics. No causal inference from actor/partner associations.

E3. Basson (2000), The female sexual response: a different model, PMID10693116, and (2001), Human sex-response cycles, PMID11224952. Indexed primary theoretical abstracts only; responsive and spontaneous desire can occur in different sequences. These models are hypotheses, not universal sequences or instructions to initiate contact without consent. Sexual Response Models: Toward a More Flexible Pattern of Women's Sexuality (2016), https://academic.oup.com/jsm/article-abstract/13/9/1369/6940323 . Complete publisher abstract inspected. N=157 hospital employees; 66.9% endorsed experiences fitting both linear and circular models, 27% only linear, 5.4% only circular, .7% neither. Alternative stricter grouping yielded 64.3% linear, 20.4% both, 14.6% circular. Self-reported model endorsement, sensitive to coding, not directly measured temporal sequencing or a validated woman type. Narrow sample; full methods not inspected. No universal responsive-desire rule.

E4. Chivers et al. (2010), Agreement of Self-Reported and Genital Measures of Sexual Arousal in Men and Women: A Meta-Analysis. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-009-9556-9 . Publisher abstract, methodological and discussion excerpts directly inspected. 132 laboratory studies (1969–2007), 2,505 women, 1,918 men. Mean concordance r=.26 women and .66 men. Timing and stimulus variability moderate estimates; some subgroup contrasts nonsignificant; differing measurement technologies and zero-coding of some unreported nonsignificant correlations matter. Correlation not agreement in absolute levels or diagnostic accuracy. Physiological response is neither evidence of willingness nor consent and cannot override a person's words. No person-specific inference from physiology.

E5. Prekatsounaki, Loeys & Enzlin (2025 issue), Understanding the Ebbs and Flows of Sexual Desire, https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2024.2393378 . Full author-institution abstract inspected at https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/01JDW1S7QJWTS7AES7DRS389WM . 163 mixed-gender couples, 22 days; daily partner-specific desire covaried with reported interactions in affiliation, sexuality and exploration domains. Women's desire associated with partner-reported affiliative interaction and perceived, not enacted, responsiveness in certain domains. Study includes randomized assignments, but abstract associations are not verified intervention effects; full estimates and manipulation results not inspected. Counterexample: van Lankveld et al. (2018), https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407517743076 , indexed primary excerpts (PMC blocked), report intimacy–desire association weakened with lag and no significant gender moderation. No claim intimacy is necessary/sufficient for women's desire or general method to produce attraction.

E6. Harris, Gormezano & van Anders (2022), Gender Inequities in Household Labor Predict Lower Sexual Desire in Women Partnered with Men. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-022-02397-2 . Indexed primary abstract and article excerpts; two women-only studies N=677 and 396 among women partnered with men and with children. Greater reported share of housework associated with lower partner-specific desire. Social context is a plausible contributor; survey design does not establish causal mediation or individual mechanism. Follow-up Division of Household Labor and Sexual Desire: The Role of Gender and Benevolent Sexism (2026), PMID42041453, indexed abstract: two preregistered studies N=943, one dyadic-longitudinal and one cross-sectional; association strongest for women with more egalitarian beliefs, first-study moderation trend, second significant. Full effects, adjustment and sample overlap unverified. Treat as context/heterogeneity evidence, not an intervention prescription.

E7. Associations of Sexual Desire with Demographic and Relationship Variables (2026), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-23483-0 . Publisher HTML selected methods, results, discussion and limitations inspected. Estonian Biobank cross-sectional sample reported N=67,334; sex subgroup counts 46,879 women and 20,456 men sum to 67,335 (one-person inconsistency, unresolved). Two-item general desire score; age, sex, relationship and demographic associations, men higher overall (eta-squared .18), age association more pronounced among women. Authors report positive association with recent childbirth, contrasting some longitudinal postpartum findings discussed in E2. Cross-sectional group comparison does not establish within-person change; relationship status, selection, cohort, specific timing and general vs partner-specific measures differ. No inference of hormonal mechanism or individual developmental trajectory. Do not treat eta-squared as interchangeable with standardized mean difference.

E8. Associations Between Sexual Coercion and Desire Among Women Partnered with Men (2026), https://doi.org/10.1007/s11199-026-01682-x . Publisher abstract and introduction inspected; N=174, cross-sectional. Greater reported coercion frequency associated with lower general/recent desire, association varied by relationship length. Exact estimates and full limitations not audited. No causal or individual conclusion; pressure and coercion are unacceptable regardless of any statistical association or desire. Use solely for recognition of harm and respect for autonomy, never optimization of coercion.

Evidence limits: E2/E4/E7 selected substantive sections directly inspected, E8 partial text, E1/E3/E5/E6 primarily abstract/excerpts. No complete journal article end-to-end, raw-data reanalysis, systematic source-status audit or independent assessment. Related follow-up studies and replies not counted as independent replication without verification. Recent lead on general vs partner-specific desire PMID42053762 remains abstract-only and reserved for integration; no numerical claim.

## Operational distinctions and checks
1. Separate general motivation from attraction/desire directed toward a particular person; stable tendencies from momentary states and long-term trajectories.
2. Distinguish reported desire, subjective arousal, physiological response, behavior, intention, willingness, satisfaction and consent. Neither any internal state nor past agreement establishes current consent; consent must be freely given, specific and revocable. Low desire alone is not a disorder.
3. Examine individual history, actual opportunities, stress, fatigue, health, relationship context, perceived vs enacted responsiveness, values, autonomy and developmental factors without inventing unobserved causes. Hormonal specifics deferred WOM-06; maternal transitions WOM-05.
4. Preserve direction, time scale, competing hypotheses, evidence independence and revision triggers. Population averages can constrain hypotheses, not establish a woman's personal desire. Do not treat responsive desire as hidden desire or a refusal as an obstacle to overcome. No covert testing, pressure or engineered distress.
Synthetic checks (self-authored, unrelated to private records): A. A participant reports affectionate closeness but low general desire; the constructs can coexist and no unique cause is established. B. Daily and annual measures show different variability gaps; compare estimands, sampling and uncertainty before claiming contradiction. C. Laboratory physiological response differs from self-report; respect self-report and explicit choices, do not infer desire or consent. D. An observed desire–workload association may reflect resources, selection, health, appraisal or reverse direction; no identified mediation. External challenges: E2, E3, E5 and E7 counter universal female variability, response sequence, intimacy dependence and postpartum trajectory. Internal reasoning only, no blinded or prospective prediction validation.
