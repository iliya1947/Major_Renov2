# COG-02 — Perception: sensory evidence, representations, and contextual/top-down effects

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Establish a rigorous working model of perception for later psychological reasoning.

The block separates:

- sensory input from perceptual representation;
- perceptual sensitivity from response/decision bias;
- perception from attention;
- perceptual effects from memory, expectation, decision, and report effects;
- unimodal from multisensory processing;
- perceptual representation from conceptual interpretation;
- perceptual evidence from action-selection consequences.

## Core rule

**Perception is inference-constrained processing of sensory information, but not every influence on a perceptual report is a change in perception itself.**

Use:

`physical signal -> sensory transduction -> sensory representation -> perceptual computation/integration -> perceptual representation -> decision/report/action`

with recurrent influences from:

- attention;
- prior experience/context;
- learning;
- action state;
- multisensory information;
- goals and task demands.

The stages are analytical distinctions, not isolated serial boxes.

## 1. Sensory input is not perceptual representation

Physical stimulation first affects sensory receptors and early sensory pathways.

Perception begins when the system computes over that sensory information to construct or transform representations of the external environment.

NIMH RDoC currently defines perception as processes that perform computations on sensory data to:

- construct and transform representations;
- acquire information about the external world;
- generate predictions;
- guide action.

Therefore:

`stimulus present -> exact internal copy`

is invalid.

The sensory signal is only part of the information used to form the perceptual representation.

## 2. Perception is modality-specific and cross-modal

Perception includes multiple modality families:

- vision;
- audition;
- olfaction;
- somatosensation;
- proprioception;
- vestibular perception;
- taste/chemosensation;
- multisensory perception.

Different modalities solve different ecological and computational problems.

Do not assume a result in visual perception automatically generalizes to audition, pain, olfaction, or vestibular perception.

Current cross-modal reviews show that the degree and form of cognitive influence differs across sensory modalities.

## 3. Perceptual representations are transformations, not raw copies

Perceptual systems compress, combine, normalize, segment, compare, and infer from sensory input.

Representations can encode properties such as:

- location;
- orientation;
- motion;
- shape;
- depth;
- pitch;
- source;
- intensity;
- temporal structure;
- object identity;
- material properties;
- multisensory event structure.

The representation used for one task may not be the same representation used for another.

Example:

- detecting whether a sound occurred;
- identifying its source;
- locating it;
- estimating its emotional salience

are different inferential targets despite sharing auditory input.

## 4. Perception is context-sensitive

The same local sensory input can be interpreted differently depending on surrounding spatial, temporal, or multisensory context.

Relevant context includes:

- preceding stimuli;
- surrounding stimuli;
- scene structure;
- temporal regularity;
- learned environmental statistics;
- cross-modal cues.

Recent work emphasizes that perception unfolds in structured spatiotemporal environments rather than as isolated processing of single stimuli.

Canonical rule:

**context effect != measurement artefact by default; but its locus must be established.**

## 5. Bottom-up and top-down are useful but incomplete labels

### Bottom-up influence

Influence driven primarily by current sensory evidence and stimulus structure.

### Top-down influence

Influence from prior knowledge, expectation, goals, memory, context, or other higher-level states.

This distinction is useful but should not be treated as a clean anatomical or temporal binary.

Recurrent sensory processing can mix:

- feedforward information;
- feedback;
- lateral interactions;
- task-dependent modulation.

Use bottom-up/top-down as functional descriptions unless a study directly identifies the underlying mechanism.

## 6. Cognitive penetrability remains a live boundary question

The question is not simply whether cognition correlates with perceptual reports.

The stronger claim is whether cognitive states alter **perceptual processing/experience itself**, rather than:

- attention;
- memory;
- decision criterion;
- response selection;
- report;
- post-perceptual judgment.

A 2024 cross-sensory review concludes that evidence for cognitive penetrability varies by modality and mechanism rather than supporting one universal yes/no answer.

Therefore:

`expectation changes response -> perception changed`

is invalid without ruling out downstream alternatives.

## 7. Priors and perceptual inference

Perceptual systems can use regularities from prior experience when sensory information is noisy or ambiguous.

A useful generic decomposition is:

- **sensory likelihood/evidence** — information carried by the current signal;
- **prior information** — expectations or learned environmental statistics;
- **posterior/decision variable** — their combination in a model of the task.

Bayesian models are powerful formal tools for describing some perceptual behavior.

But:

**Bayesian fit != proof that the brain literally implements Bayes' rule at the algorithmic level.**

Current 2026 reviews explicitly note that predictive-processing/predictive-coding frameworks have variable definitions and inconsistent empirical support at some mechanistic levels.

Use Bayesian/predictive models as candidate computational accounts, not universal doctrine.

## 8. Explicit and implicit priors can behave differently

Recent perceptual-inference experiments show that explicitly communicated priors and implicitly learned environmental priors can receive different weights and may be combined with sensory evidence using different computations.

Therefore:

`prior information = one generic top-down signal`

is too coarse.

For a perceptual task, distinguish:

- learned statistical prior;
- explicit instruction;
- semantic expectation;
- reward expectation;
- task rule;
- recent trial history.

These can act at different stages.

## 9. Attention can change perception without being perception

Attention prioritizes selected information under competition/capacity limits.

It can alter:

- effective sensitivity;
- spatial/temporal priority;
- feature processing;
- perceptual precision;
- access to downstream decision systems.

Visual temporal attention, for example, selectively prioritizes particular time points and can alter perceptual performance.

However:

`attention affected perception task -> perceptual representation itself necessarily changed`

does not automatically follow.

The locus may be:

- early sensory gain;
- perceptual representation;
- readout;
- decision;
- report.

## 10. Perceptual sensitivity and decision criterion must be separated

Observed accuracy is not a pure perceptual measure.

In detection/discrimination tasks, performance can reflect at least:

1. **sensitivity/discriminability** — how well sensory states can be distinguished;
2. **response criterion/bias** — how evidence is translated into a categorical response.

Signal Detection Theory is a core tool for separating these components under its assumptions.

Example:

A high hit rate can arise because:

- sensitivity is high;
- criterion is liberal;
- or both.

Therefore:

`more "yes" responses -> better perception`

is invalid.

Recent methodological work continues to show that perceptual threshold estimates can be distorted if criterion differences are ignored.

## 11. Signal Detection Theory has assumptions

Classical equal-variance SDT is useful but not universally exact.

Potential issues include:

- unequal signal/noise variance;
- nonstationary criterion;
- sequential dependence;
- confidence-scale assumptions;
- multiple latent decision variables.

Current work shows that conventional d' can be biased under unequal-variance conditions.

Therefore SDT estimates should be interpreted as model-dependent measures, not direct readouts of a single neural sensitivity.

## 12. Perceptual decision-making is downstream of sensory encoding

Many tasks labeled "perceptual" require:

`sensory representation -> evidence accumulation/readout -> decision rule -> motor/report response`.

A performance change can arise at any of these stages.

For example, rule switching can reduce the efficiency with which sensory information is used for decisions without necessarily changing the sensory representation itself.

Canonical rule:

**perceptual task performance is not automatically a pure measure of perception.**

## 13. Response time and accuracy reflect different constraints

A person can trade:

- speed for accuracy;
- conservative criterion for liberal criterion;
- evidence accumulation time for faster commitment.

Therefore a change in response time alone does not establish faster perception.

Likewise, unchanged accuracy with faster responses can reflect:

- improved encoding;
- more efficient readout;
- altered threshold;
- motor practice;
- strategy.

Separate sensory, decisional, and motor explanations where possible.

## 14. Perceptual constancy is an inference problem

Perceptual systems often preserve relatively stable object/world properties despite changes in proximal sensory input.

Examples include constancy for:

- size;
- color;
- shape;
- object identity;
- source identity.

This shows why perception cannot be reduced to raw sensory magnitude.

The system uses contextual and structural information to infer stable environmental causes.

## 15. Ambiguity and uncertainty are normal properties of perception

Many sensory signals are compatible with more than one environmental state.

Perceptual systems must resolve uncertainty using:

- signal reliability;
- context;
- prior statistics;
- cross-modal information;
- learned regularities.

Uncertainty can remain.

A confident percept does not imply that the sensory evidence was unambiguous.

## 16. Multisensory integration

Everyday perception often combines information from several modalities.

Potential functions include:

- increasing precision;
- resolving ambiguity;
- estimating common causes;
- spatial localization;
- timing;
- body/self-motion estimation;
- action guidance.

Multisensory processing is not always simple averaging.

The system may need to decide whether cues come from:

- the same event/source;
- different events/sources.

Current neuroscience emphasizes integration and segregation across multiple timescales rather than one fixed multisensory fusion stage.

## 17. Cue reliability matters

When multiple cues inform the same environmental property, their relative reliability can affect how much weight each cue receives.

Normative Bayesian models often describe cue combination as reliability-weighted integration.

But empirical deviations do not automatically indicate irrationality because:

- internal uncertainty may differ from experimenter estimates;
- cues may not be perceived as sharing one cause;
- learning/history may differ;
- task instructions may alter strategy.

## 18. Perception is linked to action

Perceptual representations guide action.

Action also changes the sensory stream.

Examples:

- eye movements;
- head movements;
- locomotion;
- reaching;
- active touch.

Motor commands and predicted sensory consequences can be integrated with sensory signals.

Recent work on self-motion and visuomotor processing shows that perception/action systems use motor and sensory information jointly to distinguish external changes from self-generated sensory consequences.

Therefore perception is often better understood as part of a **perception–action loop** rather than passive observation.

## 19. Self-generated sensory consequences require special handling

Actions alter sensory input.

A system must often distinguish:

- externally generated signal;
- sensory consequence of one's own movement.

Corollary-discharge/efference-copy mechanisms are candidate mechanisms for this distinction in several systems.

Do not infer a stable perceptual bias from an effect that may be caused by unmodeled self-motion or action-related sensory changes.

## 20. Perceptual and conceptual representations are distinct but interacting

A perceptual representation can encode sensory structure without requiring a full conceptual label.

Conceptual knowledge can later influence:

- categorization;
- naming;
- interpretation;
- expectations.

Recent work on action perception demonstrates separable perceptual and conceptual representations while also showing their interaction.

Therefore:

`recognized/named object -> perceptual representation equals concept`

is too strong.

## 21. Social perception adds further inferential layers

Perceiving:

- facial configuration;
- biological motion;
- gaze direction;
- voice/prosody

is not the same as inferring:

- emotion;
- intention;
- personality;
- honesty;
- mental state.

The latter adds social-cognitive inference and is routed primarily to SOC.

Canonical rule:

**sensory/perceptual cue -> social interpretation is an additional inference step.**

## 22. Emotional perception is not purely bottom-up

Current reviews of emotional-stimulus perception emphasize interactions between:

- stimulus features;
- prior knowledge;
- context;
- expectations.

But emotional perception is especially vulnerable to conflating:

- perceptual encoding;
- attention;
- appraisal;
- memory;
- social inference.

Therefore any "top-down emotional perception" claim must specify which stage changed.

## 23. Illusion does not imply broken perception

Perceptual illusions can reveal the assumptions/regularities used by a normally functioning perceptual system.

A systematic illusion can arise because a mechanism that is usually adaptive is applied to an artificial or ambiguous stimulus.

Therefore:

`illusion -> perceptual deficit`

is invalid.

## 24. Hallucination-like report does not isolate perceptual sensitivity

Signal-detection research illustrates why unusual perceptual reports must distinguish:

- sensitivity;
- criterion;
- prior expectation;
- sensory noise;
- attention;
- decision/report processes.

A liberal response criterion can increase false alarms without proving degraded sensory discriminability.

Clinical interpretation is deferred to CLN and PSY-RES-07 constraints.

## 25. Perception and consciousness are not identical

Perceptual processing can occur without a reportable conscious experience, and conscious reports can be influenced by decision/report requirements.

The exact boundary remains theoretically contested.

Therefore:

`measurable perceptual processing -> conscious perception`

is not automatic.

Consciousness is not made a subcomponent of perception in this qualification architecture.

## 26. Measurement hierarchy for perceptual claims

### Level 1 — Raw response rate

Weakest if criterion cannot be separated.

Examples:

- percent "seen";
- percent "present";
- hit rate alone.

### Level 2 — Accuracy / psychometric function

Stronger for stimulus-response relation but can still mix sensitivity, decision, motor, and strategy factors.

### Level 3 — Sensitivity + criterion model

Signal-detection or related models separate discriminability from response bias under stated assumptions.

### Level 4 — Process decomposition

Sequential-sampling, computational, eye-tracking, temporal, neural, or perturbational evidence helps distinguish encoding/readout/decision stages.

### Level 5 — Converging causal/process evidence

Multiple methods support a specified perceptual mechanism and rule out key attentional, decisional, memory, and motor alternatives.

This is a practical evidence ladder, not a universal ranking for every perceptual question.

## 27. Perception Inference Gate

Before interpreting a perceptual result, ask:

### A. Target

1. What perceptual property/process is being inferred?
2. Is the claim about detection, discrimination, estimation, recognition, localization, or subjective appearance?
3. Which modality is involved?

### B. Sensory signal

4. What physical/sensory information was available?
5. How reliable/ambiguous was it?
6. Could peripheral sensory limitations explain performance?

### C. Context and priors

7. What spatial/temporal context was present?
8. What learned prior information was available?
9. Was prior information explicit or implicit?
10. Could recent trial history alter expectations?

### D. Attention

11. Were stimuli equally attended?
12. Could spatial/temporal/feature attention explain the effect?
13. Was attentional allocation measured or manipulated?

### E. Decision/report

14. Does the measure separate sensitivity from criterion?
15. Could response bias explain the observed effect?
16. Could reward/instruction alter criterion?
17. Could motor/report demands explain performance?

### F. Representation locus

18. What evidence places the effect at a perceptual rather than post-perceptual stage?
19. Could memory or conceptual interpretation explain it?
20. Could the effect arise during evidence readout or decision formation?

### G. Multisensory/action context

21. Were other sensory modalities informative?
22. Could cue integration or causal-inference assumptions alter the result?
23. Did self-generated action alter sensory input?
24. Could eye/head/body movement explain part of the effect?

### H. Measurement/model

25. What psychophysical/computational model is being used?
26. What assumptions does it require?
27. Are sensitivity/criterion estimates robust to plausible alternative models?
28. Is task performance being mistaken for a pure process measure?

### I. Generalization

29. Does the effect generalize across stimuli?
30. Does it generalize across contexts/modalities?
31. Is the perceptual claim broader than the sampled task permits?

### J. Conclusion

32. What is the strongest perceptual conclusion supported?
33. What remains attentional/decisional/conceptual rather than perceptual?
34. What result would discriminate the preferred interpretation from its nearest rival?

## 28. High-value failure modes

### Failure A — stimulus = percept

Incorrect.

Correct:

Perception transforms sensory evidence into representations; it is not a literal copy.

### Failure B — expectation changed response = perception changed

Incorrect.

Correct:

Expectation can alter attention, criterion, decision, memory, or report. A perceptual locus needs evidence.

### Failure C — hit rate = sensitivity

Incorrect.

Correct:

Hit rate also depends on response criterion; false alarms are required for SDT-style separation.

### Failure D — d' = pure sensory mechanism

Incorrect.

Correct:

d' is model-dependent and can also reflect how sensory information is read out; model assumptions matter.

### Failure E — perceptual task = pure perception

Incorrect.

Correct:

Tasks can recruit attention, memory, control, decision, motor response, and strategy.

### Failure F — top-down versus bottom-up is a clean binary

Incorrect.

Correct:

Recurrent processing mixes feedforward, feedback, learned, attentional, contextual, and decisional effects.

### Failure G — Bayesian model fit proves Bayesian brain implementation

Incorrect.

Correct:

A computational model can describe behavior without uniquely identifying the neural/algorithmic implementation.

### Failure H — multisensory integration = weighted averaging only

Incorrect.

Correct:

The system also solves source/causal-assignment and temporal integration problems.

### Failure I — concept = percept

Incorrect.

Correct:

Conceptual interpretation and perceptual representation can be distinguishable while interacting.

### Failure J — social interpretation = perception

Incorrect.

Correct:

Inferring emotion, intention, honesty, or mental state adds social-cognitive inference beyond sensory cue processing.

### Failure K — illusion = deficit

Incorrect.

Correct:

Illusions can expose normally adaptive inferential assumptions.

### Failure L — unusual report = sensory deficit

Incorrect.

Correct:

Sensitivity, criterion, priors, attention, and reporting must be separated.

## Canonical working rule

**Identify the sensory evidence, model the perceptual representation, separate attention and priors, separate sensitivity from criterion, locate decision/report contributions, then infer only the perceptual mechanism that survives those alternatives.**

Never:

**perceptual-task response -> direct readout of perception.**

## Current-standard note

As of 2026-11-19:

- NIMH RDoC continues to define perception as computation over sensory data that constructs/transforms representations, predicts the external world, and guides action.
- Current review literature supports substantial contextual, attentional, multisensory, learned, and action-related influences on perceptual behavior.
- The stronger claim of cognitive penetrability remains mechanism- and modality-dependent rather than a settled universal property.
- Predictive-processing/Bayesian frameworks remain important computational approaches, but current 2026 review literature explicitly cautions against treating predictive coding as one fully specified, uniformly validated neural mechanism.

## Scope note

This block establishes a cross-modal perception framework.

Detailed visual, auditory, olfactory, somatosensory, pain, vestibular, interoceptive, and social-perception specializations should be opened only when downstream work requires them.
