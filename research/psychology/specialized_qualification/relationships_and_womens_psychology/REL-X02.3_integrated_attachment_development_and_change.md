## 1. Question and dependencies

E1. Pinquart, Feussner & Ahnert (2013), Meta-analytic evidence for stability in attachments from infancy to early adulthood. https://doi.org/10.1080/14616734.2013.746257 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23210665/ . Indexed publisher and author abstract extracts: 127 papers, pooled stability correlation r=.39 across included time intervals. This is not a single estimate of continuity from infancy to adulthood or of romantic attachment self-reports. Duration, measurement family and population must match the target comparison. Moderator estimates, dependence handling and confidence intervals not independently checked. Underlying studies may include E2/E3/E4; do not count as independent replications of the pooled result.

E2. Waters, Merrick, Treboux, Crowell & Albersheim (2000), Attachment security in infancy and early adulthood: a twenty-year longitudinal study. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00176 ; https://academic.oup.com/chidev/article-abstract/71/3/684/8284122 . Complete publisher abstract directly inspected. Of 60 White middle-class infants assessed at 12 months, 50 received a later Adult Attachment Interview (AAI) with interviewers blind to initial classifications. Binary secure/insecure agreement was 72%, kappa=.44. Negative life-event group: 8/18 changed classifications versus 7/32 without reported events. The event associations are observational and grouped across different experiences. Raw agreement and chance-corrected agreement differ; neither is a forecast for every individual. Small selected sample, attrition, measurement change across ages and limited precision restrict inference.

E3. Lewis, Feiring & Rosenthal (2000), Attachment over time. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00180 ; https://academic.oup.com/chidev/article-abstract/71/3/707/8284150 . Complete publisher abstract directly inspected. 84 White middle-class participants assessed at 12 months with a modified Strange Situation and at 18 years with AAI. No continuity detected in attachment classification; parental divorce was associated with adolescent insecurity. Contrast with E2 despite broadly similar developmental endpoints, not a proof of zero stability or causal divorce effect. Differences in protocol, sample, experience and precision may contribute. Exact coefficients and uncertainty not inspected.

E4. Weinfield, Sroufe & Egeland (2000), Attachment from infancy to early adulthood in a high-risk sample: continuity, discontinuity, and their correlates. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10953936/ . Indexed original abstract extract only; direct open CAPTCHA. No significant overall continuity reported; caregiving adversity, maternal depression and later family functioning differentiated continuity and discontinuity groups. Sample count, estimates and adjustment details not verified. Supporting counterevidence, not proof of a universal high-risk trajectory. The later study of continuity and coherence (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14982680/) uses a high-risk longitudinal sample and may overlap; do not count separately here.

E5. Fraley, Roisman, Booth-LaForce, Owen & Holland (2013), Interpersonal and genetic origins of adult attachment styles: a longitudinal study from infancy to early adulthood. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031435 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3885143/ . Indexed original abstract and manuscript excerpts; direct PMC and PubMed opens blocked. NICHD SECCYD prospective assessments from childhood, with adult attachment self-report at age 18; associations with earlier caregiving environment, developing social competence and best-friend relationship quality. Most investigated temperament and candidate genetic measures showed little association. A candidate polymorphism exception was reported; no replicated genetic mechanism established here. Exact sample counts, coefficients, multiplicity and missingness not independently checked. Earlier caregiving quality and infant attachment classification are distinct predictors; adult self-report is not equivalent to AAI.

E6. Groh et al. (2014), Stability of attachment security from infancy to late adolescence. https://doi.org/10.1111/mono.12113 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13245647/ . Indexed original chapter excerpts report N=857 and multiple early assessments followed by adolescent AAI; weak continuity for some disorganization measures. Used as supporting evidence for repeated developmental measurement and construct differentiation, not a precise effect estimate. Shares NICHD SECCYD with E5; no independent cohort replication claimed. Exact security coefficients and uncertainty not independently verified.

E7. Opie et al. (2021 issue), Early childhood attachment stability and change: a meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.1080/14616734.2020.1800769 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7612040/ . Indexed author/publisher methods and results extracts: 63 included studies and 79 independent samples; evidence of funnel asymmetry via Egger tests for two-category (p=.021) and four-category stability (p=.007). Such tests can also reflect heterogeneity or small-study effects; do not identify the corrected true effect. Early-childhood intervals are not infancy-to-adulthood intervals. Direct PMC open failed; full moderator/bias tables not inspected. Counterweight to assuming pooled published stability is unbiased.

E8. Fraley & Roisman (2019), The development of adult attachment styles: four lessons. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2018.02.008 ; https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/the-development-of-adult-attachment-styles-four-lessons/ . Complete institutional author abstract directly inspected. Narrative synthesis emphasizes weak, measurement-dependent associations of early caregiving with adult attachment, greater apparent developmental malleability earlier in life, and the need to examine proximal and target-specific processes. This is an interpretive review, not independent replication of E5/E6 or a quantified individual forecast.

E9. Genetic moderation of stability in attachment security from early childhood to age 18 years: A replication study (2015). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26389606/ . Indexed original abstract: longitudinal data from over 550 individuals provided no evidence for the proposed OXTR rs53576 moderation of early security and later AAI coherence. This tests a different candidate marker from the exception mentioned in E5; it does not directly refute that specific exception or exclude all genetic contributions. Exact effects and power not checked. Possible NICHD overlap, so not an independent replication of developmental stability. No genetic inference about individuals.

## 3. Integrated model
Three different developmental measurements must remain separate: infant observed attachment organization with a particular caregiver (e.g., Strange Situation), later narrative/coherence measures (AAI), and self-reported adult attachment anxiety/avoidance in general or toward specific targets. These differ in method, target and developmental capacities; agreement is an empirical question. Do not equate infant insecurity, adult AAI classification, self-reported avoidance, attachment-figure function or a current interpersonal action.

Combine the preceding blocks into a candidate multilevel process model:
- distal experiences and earlier individual differences may contribute to learned expectations;
- later relationships, available resources, actual safety, life events and selection may contribute to general and target-specific expectations;
- current demands, appraisals and support opportunities influence transient experience and action;
- experiences and actions may feed back into expectations and relationships over longer intervals.
These are plausible links, not identified causal mechanisms. Stable components, contextual offsets, transient states, measurement error and gradual revision can coexist. Distinguish within-person fluctuation, recovery to baseline, sustained change, rank-order stability and cross-age measurement correspondence. A persistent component inferred from adult repeated measures is not proof of an immutable childhood origin.

Competing models for an observed relationship-specific pattern:
1. Ordinary preference, opportunity, relationship conditions or valid boundary: no attachment mechanism required.
2. Transient stress-related response: variation around earlier baseline; recovery may occur when the ordinary context changes.
3. Target-specific expectation: pattern differs across relationships and may follow target-specific history.
4. Broader relatively stable orientation: repeated similar experiences across diverse comparable relationships.
5. Durable developmental or relational change: sustained departure from earlier trajectory across relevant measurement windows.
6. Measurement/reporting artifact or coexistence of mechanisms.
Prefer the simplest supported explanation; a more elaborate account needs discriminating evidence. A general developmental association cannot retrospectively identify an individual's childhood experience or present motive.

## 4. Operational reasoning and revision
1. Define actor, relational target, time window, construct and evidence channel. Keep actual relationship type, romantic preference, felt security, support and consent distinct.
2. Establish observed behavior, voluntary experience reports and actual conditions separately. Do not assign questionnaire scores or AAI classifications to an absent person. No inference of childhood history from adult behavior.
3. Compare credible alternatives at the appropriate level. Assess actual relational conditions before attributing actions to attachment-related regulation.
4. Use naturally occurring repeated observations, documented earlier patterns where legitimately available and voluntarily offered descriptions. Specify what would favor recovery versus stable change and target-specific versus general tendencies. If observations fit all rivals equally, leave them unresolved.
5. Evaluate population, sample selection, measurement family, timescale, dependency, bias and temporal/causal limitations. Do not use pooled correlation or raw classification agreement as an individual probability.
6. Separate momentary comfort, ongoing security, support-role function, autonomous preference and commitment. No expectation that support or reducing insecurity guarantees attraction or a mutually desired relationship transition.
7. Respect freely stated choices, boundaries and autonomy. Do not use attachment theory for covert testing, engineered distress or manipulation. Keep clinical assessment outside this scope. Maintain a record of prediction conditions and revise rather than retrofitting.

## 5. Internal synthetic transfer review
Rubric fixed before responses: construct separation, competing explanation, discriminating evidence, inference boundary. Critical errors: unsupported motive/diagnosis, causal inflation, group-to-person determinism, ignored counterevidence, coercion or privacy breach. Self-authored cases unrelated to private material; not independent or held-out.

A. A pooled stability estimate combines several developmental intervals. Response: pooled association differs from a specific infancy-to-adulthood estimate; interval, methods and cohort variation compete. Inspect relevant interval and compatible measurement subgroup; no personal probability.
B. Two cohorts using early observed attachment and later interview assessments give different results. Response: sample and measurement differences, environmental histories and precision may explain divergence. Compare matched protocols and uncertainty, including null evidence. Neither result establishes universal stability or universal change.
C. An adult questionnaire reports high avoidance while an interview indicates coherent narrative organization. Response: target and method differ; context specificity and measurement effects are alternatives. Check instrument scope and actual reported experiences. No hidden diagnosis or childhood reconstruction.
D. A participant becomes temporarily less willing to rely on others during an exhausting project. Response: current behavior and enduring orientation differ; workload, preference and target-specific expectations compete. Compare ordinary post-project recovery and voluntarily described experiences. No attachment-type assignment.
E. Two partners' felt security rises after a shared stressor resolves, but their relationship preferences remain unchanged. Response: security, preference and commitment differ; shared context and mutual support can coexist. Examine ordinary timing and prior baselines. No causal partner effect or implied romantic transition.
F. A candidate-gene interaction is reported and another marker's moderation fails to replicate. Response: distinct markers, outcomes and hypotheses cannot be equated; low power, multiple testing and sample dependence matter. Seek matched replication; no individual genetic or causal attribution.
