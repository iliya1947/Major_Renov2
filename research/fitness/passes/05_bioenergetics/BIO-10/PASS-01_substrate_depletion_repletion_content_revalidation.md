## 1. Qualification purpose / Question Card

A fitness trainer needs a correct depletion/repletion model to avoid:

- treating all energy substrates as one shared “fuel tank”;
- assuming PCr, muscle glycogen, liver glycogen and IMTG recover on the same time scale;
- treating blood glucose as a storage depot analogous to glycogen;
- assuming muscle glycogen depletion is uniform across fibres, muscles and exercise modes;
- assuming low glycogen means glycogen is literally zero;
- assuming performance recovery equals substrate repletion;
- assuming substrate repletion equals complete recovery;
- assuming one recovery duration restores every substrate;
- assuming carbohydrate ingestion affects PCr, glycogen and IMTG in the same way;
- assuming resistance, sprint and endurance sessions deplete glycogen/IMTG identically;
- equating whole-muscle glycogen concentration with every subcellular/fibre glycogen pool;
- using one study's postexercise recovery curve as a universal rule.

Primary trainer-level decisions supported later:

- understand which substrate is likely to remain altered before a repeated session;
- distinguish fast PCr restoration from slower glycogen/lipid restoration;
- interpret why recovery nutrition matters more for some substrate pools than others;
- avoid inferring readiness from one substrate marker;
- understand why repeated-session metabolic state depends on prior task and recovery.

Question type:

- **mechanism + evidence mapping + recovery-state interpretation**.

Target population/context:

- healthy adults and athletes;
- endurance, sprint/HIIT and resistance exercise;
- short-term recovery through multi-day recovery.

Closure criterion for PASS-01:

- all 9 exact Section 10 items covered at content/scope level;
- substrate-specific time courses and nutrition dependencies explicit;
- no content-level blocker preventing independent closure audit.

## 2. Core substrate-store framework

Different exercise fuels/stores have different:

- anatomical compartments;
- storage capacities;
- rates of use;
- resynthesis pathways;
- nutritional dependencies;
- measurement methods;
- recovery kinetics.

Key pools:

### High-energy phosphate
- PCr.

### Carbohydrate
- muscle glycogen;
- liver glycogen;
- circulating glucose.

### Lipid
- intramyocellular / intramuscular triglyceride-lipid pools;
- circulating fatty acids and triglyceride-derived substrates.

Canonical rule:

**substrate depletion/repletion is pool specific; there is no single “energy stores recovered” state.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 3. PCr depletion

PASS at content level.

PCr is rapidly consumed when ATP turnover rises abruptly.

Magnitude of depletion depends on:

- intensity;
- bout duration;
- muscle recruitment;
- starting PCr;
- prior bouts;
- recovery interval;
- pH/metabolic state.

Repeated-sprint evidence PMID **8964751** shows large PCr depletion during a 30-s maximal sprint and incomplete restoration after ~4 min before the second sprint.

Canonical rule:

**PCr depletion is rapid and task dependent; PCr depletion ≠ ATP depletion and ≠ complete fatigue explanation.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 4. PCr repletion

PASS at content level.

PCr resynthesis after exercise is primarily supported by oxidative ATP production.

Current systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **40189235** included **128 studies** and confirms that 31P-MRS-derived PCr recovery kinetics show substantial methodological and biological heterogeneity.

Recovery is affected by:

- oxidative capacity;
- O2 delivery/availability;
- end-exercise pH;
- degree of depletion;
- muscle studied;
- age/health;
- acquisition/modeling method.

Classic sprint study PMID **9241025** found after 3 min:

- ~90% of pre-exercise PCr after one 6-s sprint;
- ~84% after 5 × 6-s repeated sprints.

Canonical rule:

**PCr recovery occurs on a short time scale relative to glycogen, but no universal “fully restored after X minutes” rule is retained.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 5. PCr recovery is not nutritionally analogous to glycogen repletion

PCr resynthesis requires oxidative ATP and available creatine/phosphate chemistry.

Acute carbohydrate intake is not the direct substrate-replacement mechanism for PCr in the way carbohydrate is for glycogen.

Creatine availability can change total creatine/PCr pool and repeated-sprint behavior, but supplementation prescription remains NUT.

Canonical boundary:

**PCr repletion ≠ carbohydrate refeeding process.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 6. Muscle glycogen depletion

PASS at content level.

Muscle glycogen is a major local carbohydrate store.

Exercise-induced glycogen use depends on:

- workload/intensity;
- duration;
- recruitment;
- fibre type;
- training status;
- starting glycogen;
- carbohydrate ingestion;
- environmental state;
- muscle group.

The 2018 glycogen meta-analysis (PMID **29923148**, retained from BIO-07) supports baseline glycogen and exercise intensity as important determinants of glycogen use.

Current resistance-exercise meta-analysis PMID **41420384** found a significant acute fall in vastus lateralis glycogen across **20 studies**, with greater depletion associated with:

- more sets;
- longer session duration;

and substantial heterogeneity across protocols.

Canonical rule:

**muscle glycogen depletion is workload/recruitment specific, not one fixed percentage per workout.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 7. Resistance exercise depletes glycogen, but the amount is variable

The 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **41420384** included:

- 20 studies;
- 180 participants;
- predominantly men;
- resistance-exercise protocols with heterogeneous designs.

Pooled mean change was approximately:

- **−104 mmol/kg dry mass**,

but the prediction interval was wide and crossed zero.

This means:

- the average effect supports glycogen depletion;
- exact depletion for an individual session is highly variable.

Canonical rule:

**resistance training can materially deplete local muscle glycogen, but one universal percentage or mmol value is not retained.**

Confidence: **HIGH average direction / MODERATE exact magnitude**.

---

## 8. Muscle glycogen depletion is spatially heterogeneous

Glycogen is not uniformly used across:

- different muscles;
- fibre types;
- recruited motor units;
- subcellular glycogen compartments.

Classic human work shows intensity/fibre recruitment changes depletion patterns.

Therefore:

**whole-muscle biopsy glycogen concentration ≠ uniform glycogen status of every fibre or subcellular pool.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual / MODERATE-HIGH exact compartmental mapping**.

Detailed subcellular glycogen-fatigue causality remains BIO-11.

---

## 9. Low glycogen does not mean zero glycogen

Exercise termination can occur with measurable glycogen remaining.

Different fibres/muscles can retain substantial glycogen even after exhaustive work.

Therefore the project rejects:

- “bonking = every muscle glycogen granule is gone”;
- “fatigue occurs only when glycogen reaches zero.”

Canonical boundary:

**low muscle glycogen ≠ zero glycogen and ≠ complete fatigue diagnosis.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

Fatigue causality remains BIO-11.

---

## 10. Carbohydrate ingestion during exercise and glycogen sparing

A 2026 meta-analysis (PMID **41363659**) synthesized:

- 31 prolonged-endurance studies;
- 48 unique crossover effect sizes.

Carbohydrate ingestion produced a **small but statistically significant** reduction in net muscle glycogen use.

Estimated absolute sparing was approximately:

- **24 mmol/kg dry mass** over ~100 min,

with uncertainty.

A separate 2026 HIIT crossover study PMID **42552237** found carbohydrate feeding reduced muscle glycogen utilization by ~40% in its specific protocol.

Canonical rule:

**exogenous carbohydrate can reduce muscle glycogen use in some endurance/high-intensity contexts, but glycogen sparing is not a fixed or universal effect size.**

Confidence: **HIGH existence / MODERATE-HIGH magnitude**.

Exact during-exercise fueling remains NUT.

---

## 11. Muscle glycogen repletion

PASS at content level.

Postexercise muscle glycogen synthesis is promoted by:

- prior depletion/contraction;
- glucose availability;
- insulin signaling;
- glycogen synthase activity;
- adequate carbohydrate intake.

Current direct human study PMID **40836481** showed after glycogen-depleting exercise in well-trained cyclists:

- muscle glycogen fell ~64%;
- without carbohydrate it remained low over 12 h;
- with substantial carbohydrate it rose markedly but was still below pre-exercise values at 12 h.

Canonical rule:

**muscle glycogen repletion is carbohydrate dependent when rapid restoration is required, and even large carbohydrate intake does not guarantee complete restoration within a fixed short window.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 12. Muscle glycogen recovery can extend beyond 12 h

In PMID **40836481**, large carbohydrate intake restored:

- liver glycogen rapidly;
- muscle glycogen only partially by 12 h.

Other studies show near-baseline muscle glycogen can be recovered within ~24 h under some protocols when carbohydrate availability is sufficient.

But the time course varies with:

- initial depletion;
- muscle damage;
- exercise mode;
- carbohydrate availability;
- total energy intake;
- training state.

Canonical rule:

**muscle glycogen recovery time is not fixed; 12 h, 24 h or 48 h cannot be universalized across tasks.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 13. Glycogen supercompensation

The project distinguishes:

- **repletion** = restoration toward prior level;
- **supercompensation** = storage above prior/baseline level.

A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **40901614** found glycogen supercompensation after cycling and running followed by 3–5 d of high-carbohydrate intake.

The magnitude differed by mode and was influenced by:

- basal glycogen;
- postexercise glycogen;
- carbohydrate content.

Canonical rule:

**glycogen repletion ≠ glycogen supercompensation.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

Detailed carbohydrate-loading prescription remains NUT.

---

## 14. Liver glycogen

PASS at content level.

Liver glycogen is a distinct carbohydrate store.

Its major exercise role is supporting hepatic glucose output and therefore blood-glucose homeostasis.

During prolonged exercise, hepatic glucose production derives from:

- liver glycogenolysis;
- gluconeogenesis.

As exercise continues and liver glycogen falls, gluconeogenic contribution can become more important.

Canonical rule:

**liver glycogen ≠ muscle glycogen; liver glycogen primarily supports systemic glucose availability rather than local muscle-only storage.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 15. Liver glycogen depletion is task/nutrition dependent

Prolonged exercise can substantially reduce liver glycogen.

Modern 13C-MRS work provides direct non-invasive measurement.

PMID **40836481** found glycogen-depleting cycling reduced:

- muscle glycogen ~64%;
- liver glycogen ~34%

in its protocol.

A 2026 HIIT study PMID **42552237** also directly measured both liver and muscle glycogen and found distinct responses, with preferential muscle-glycogen use in that interval protocol.

Canonical rule:

**muscle and liver glycogen depletion can differ substantially within the same session.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 16. Liver glycogen repletion can be faster than muscle glycogen in some recovery conditions

PMID **40836481** found:

- liver glycogen exceeded pre-exercise concentration within 6 h with carbohydrate feeding;
- muscle glycogen remained substantially below baseline at 12 h.

This is protocol specific and should not be universalized.

It demonstrates that:

**liver and muscle glycogen have distinct recovery kinetics.**

Confidence: **HIGH for non-equivalence / MODERATE-HIGH exact time course beyond this protocol**.

---

## 17. Liver glycogen concentration vs content

A 2025 study PMID **41115061** showed that after a carbohydrate-rich breakfast:

- liver glycogen concentration could change;
- liver volume changed concurrently;
- total liver glycogen content did not necessarily rise.

Canonical measurement boundary:

**liver glycogen concentration ≠ total liver glycogen content when liver volume changes.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 18. Intramuscular triglycerides / intramyocellular lipids

PASS at content level.

Terminology and measurement matter.

Relevant pools include:

- intramyocellular lipid droplets / IMCL;
- intramuscular triglyceride/triacylglycerol (IMTG);
- extramyocellular/intermuscular fat, which should not be conflated with IMCL.

A 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **36656983** included **44 studies** and found cycling exercise caused net IMCL degradation of approximately **23.7%** overall.

But analytical technique materially influenced the estimated change.

Canonical rule:

**IMTG/IMCL can be an exercise substrate, but measured depletion depends strongly on fibre type, exercise mode and measurement method.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 19. IMTG depletion is fibre and exercise-mode dependent

The 2023 meta-analysis PMID **36656983** found larger depletion:

- in type I fibres;
- with immunofluorescence compared with some whole-muscle biochemical approaches.

In contrast, a 2023 severe repeated high-intensity intermittent study PMID **37877795** found:

- no net loss of major intramuscular lipid-droplet pools;
- subcellular remodeling without clear net substrate depletion.

Canonical rule:

**endurance-type IMTG utilization cannot be generalized to every high-intensity intermittent session.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 20. IMTG repletion

IMTG recovery is nutritionally and protocol dependent.

Human prolonged-cycling study PMID **12783774** found:

- ~21% IMTG decline during exercise;
- recovery to baseline within 48 h with a normal-fat recovery diet;
- impaired repletion with a carbohydrate-rich lower-fat recovery diet.

A later elite-triathlete study PMID **31935113** found large type-I fibre IMTG use during prolonged cycling and recovery toward baseline within 24 h.

Canonical rule:

**IMTG recovery has a different nutritional/time-course logic from glycogen recovery.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

Exact dietary fat prescription remains NUT.

---

## 21. High postexercise fat oxidation does not prove IMTG depletion

During recovery from glycogen-depleting exercise, whole-body fat oxidation may be elevated while IMTG content remains stable.

PMID **12651914** found:

- high fat oxidation during 18 h recovery;
- no net IMTG utilization;
- substantial muscle glycogen resynthesis.

Canonical rule:

**whole-body fat oxidation ≠ net IMTG use.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 22. Blood glucose is not a storage pool

PASS at content level.

Blood glucose is a **regulated circulating concentration/flux**, not a large depot analogous to glycogen or triglyceride.

During exercise, blood glucose reflects balance among:

- hepatic glucose output;
- intestinal/exogenous glucose appearance;
- skeletal-muscle uptake;
- uptake by other tissues;
- hormonal regulation.

Canonical rule:

**blood glucose concentration ≠ blood glucose “store.”**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 23. Blood glucose can remain stable despite high turnover

During prolonged exercise:

- muscle glucose uptake can rise;
- hepatic glucose production can rise;
- exogenous carbohydrate can enter the circulation.

Therefore concentration may remain relatively stable even while glucose flux is high.

Classic tracer study PMID **12169451** showed plasma glucose remained near preexercise level during prolonged exercise with carbohydrate feeding despite continuously rising glucose appearance/disposal.

Canonical rule:

**stable blood glucose concentration ≠ low glucose turnover.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 24. Exercise-induced hypoglycemia is possible but not universal

During prolonged exercise without adequate exogenous support, liver glycogen depletion and insufficient hepatic glucose production can contribute to falling blood glucose.

But:

- not every prolonged session produces hypoglycemia;
- blood glucose can remain maintained while fatigue develops;
- carbohydrate ingestion can maintain glucose without eliminating all other limiting factors.

Recent debate/review literature (PMIDs **41562187**, **42822023**) emphasizes that hypoglycemia can be important in prolonged exercise, while also showing that exercise termination can occur with blood glucose maintained by carbohydrate ingestion.

Canonical rule:

**blood glucose is an important regulated variable, but exercise fatigue cannot be universally reduced to hypoglycemia alone.**

Confidence: **HIGH non-single-cause boundary / MODERATE exact role across endurance contexts**.

Detailed fatigue remains BIO-11.

## 25. Recovery time course is substrate specific

PASS at content level.

Approximate ordering:

### PCr
- rapid recovery over minutes;
- strongly state/method dependent.

### Blood glucose concentration
- regulated dynamically over minutes/hours;
- not a depot with a single repletion curve.

### Liver glycogen
- can recover rapidly over hours with carbohydrate under some conditions.

### Muscle glycogen
- recovery usually extends over hours to a day or more depending on depletion/nutrition/task.

### IMTG
- recovery can extend over many hours to 1–2 d and depends on fat availability/exercise context.

Canonical rule:

**“recovered after X hours” must specify which substrate/store is being discussed.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 26. Nutritional dependence of repletion

PASS at content level.

Different pools have different nutrient dependencies.

### PCr
Primarily oxidative ATP-dependent; not directly carbohydrate-refilled.

### Muscle glycogen
Strongly dependent on carbohydrate availability during rapid recovery.

### Liver glycogen
Strongly carbohydrate dependent; carbohydrate type can influence repletion rate.

### IMTG
Recovery can be impaired when dietary fat availability is low.

### Blood glucose
Depends acutely on hepatic/exogenous glucose appearance vs tissue disposal.

Canonical rule:

**recovery nutrition affects substrate pools differently; there is no one nutrient that “refills all energy systems.”**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

Exact dose/timing belongs NUT.

---

## 27. Carbohydrate timing — scope-limited conclusion

For rapid glycogen recovery, early carbohydrate availability can increase short-term glycogen synthesis.

The classic timing study PMID **3132449** found delayed carbohydrate reduced early glycogen-storage rate.

However current evidence shows total 24-h carbohydrate exposure can sometimes produce similar 24-h glycogen despite different early timing.

PMID **39263899** found immediate vs 3-h delayed carbohydrate with matched total 24-h carbohydrate produced similar 24-h muscle glycogen, yet next-day HIIE capacity differed.

Canonical rule:

**early carbohydrate timing can matter when the recovery window is short, but timing is not the only determinant of 24-h glycogen restoration or next-session performance.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

Exact timing prescription remains NUT.

---

## 28. Protein co-ingestion and glycogen — boundary

A 2021 systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **33507402** found:

- carbohydrate improved short-term glycogen resynthesis vs water;
- adding protein to carbohydrate did not significantly improve glycogen resynthesis compared with carbohydrate alone at the studied carbohydrate intakes.

Protein may still serve other recovery functions.

Canonical boundary:

**protein recovery benefit ≠ necessarily faster glycogen resynthesis when carbohydrate availability is already adequate.**

Confidence: **HIGH for pooled glycogen outcome within included studies**.

Detailed nutrition remains NUT.

## 29. Repeated-session implications

PASS at content level.

A second session can start with incomplete restoration of:

- PCr;
- muscle glycogen;
- liver glycogen;
- IMTG;
- other metabolic/neuromuscular variables.

Which pool matters depends on the next task.

Examples:

- short repeated maximal work can be sensitive to PCr restoration;
- prolonged/high-volume work can be sensitive to carbohydrate availability;
- multi-session endurance work can depend on muscle/liver glycogen restoration;
- prolonged endurance lipid availability/IMTG state can also differ.

Canonical rule:

**repeated-session readiness is substrate- and task-specific.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 30. Substrate restoration ≠ performance restoration

A key non-equivalence is supported directly.

PMID **32187403**:

- carbohydrate accelerated muscle glycogen resynthesis;
- did **not** accelerate recovery of prolonged low-frequency force depression after the studied glycogen-depleting + sprint protocol.

PMID **39263899**:

- 24-h muscle glycogen was similar between immediate and delayed carbohydrate conditions;
- next-day HIIE capacity was worse after delayed carbohydrate.

Therefore:

**glycogen recovery and functional recovery are related but non-equivalent.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 31. Complete substrate repletion ≠ complete recovery

Even if a substrate store returns to baseline, other recovery constraints may remain:

- muscle damage;
- excitation-contraction disturbances;
- neural fatigue;
- hydration;
- temperature;
- sleep/stress;
- connective-tissue state.

Likewise, performance can sometimes recover before every substrate has fully normalized.

Canonical rule:

**one recovered substrate marker cannot certify complete readiness.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

Detailed recovery/readiness belongs REC/BIO-11/12/PRO.

---

## 32. Different depletion patterns by training type — endurance

PASS at content level.

Prolonged moderate/high-intensity endurance exercise commonly causes:

- substantial muscle glycogen use;
- substantial liver glycogen use;
- IMTG utilization, especially in trained/type-I fibres;
- increasing reliance on blood/exogenous glucose as duration progresses.

Magnitude depends on intensity, duration and feeding.

Canonical profile:

**endurance exercise can materially draw on multiple carbohydrate/lipid stores over prolonged time.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 33. Different depletion patterns — sprint/HIIT

Sprint/HIIT can cause:

- rapid PCr depletion;
- substantial local muscle glycogen use despite short total exercise duration;
- repeated-bout changes in PCr availability;
- less consistent/no net IMTG use in severe intermittent protocols.

The 2026 13C-MRS HIIT study PMID **42552237** showed preferential muscle-glycogen use.

The 2023 lipid-droplet study PMID **37877795** found no net IMTG depletion after severe repeated high-intensity intermittent exercise.

Canonical profile:

**sprint/HIIT can be PCr- and glycogen-intensive without mirroring prolonged-endurance IMTG depletion.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 34. Different depletion patterns — resistance exercise

Resistance exercise can cause:

- local PCr depletion within sets;
- muscle glycogen depletion across repeated sets;
- fibre-specific substrate use;
- variable IMTG use depending on protocol/population.

Current meta-analysis PMID **41420384** confirms average muscle glycogen depletion but with wide protocol heterogeneity.

Canonical profile:

**resistance exercise depletion is local and set-structure dependent; it cannot be inferred from session duration alone.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

Detailed resistance bioenergetics remains BIO-12.

---

## 35. Mechanical work does not uniquely determine substrate depletion

Different protocols can produce different substrate depletion at similar or very different external work.

Example:

PMID **41830465** reported greater muscle glycogen depletion after a sprint-interval protocol than moderate continuous exercise despite substantially lower total mechanical work in that small male crossover study.

Canonical rule:

**external mechanical work ≠ direct universal predictor of substrate depletion.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 36. Measurement boundaries

### PCr
- biopsy or 31P-MRS;
- MRS recovery estimates are protocol/model dependent.

### Muscle glycogen
- biopsy biochemistry;
- histochemistry/immunofluorescence;
- 13C-MRS;
- whole-muscle average can hide fibre/subcellular heterogeneity.

### Liver glycogen
- 13C-MRS;
- concentration must be distinguished from total content/organ volume.

### IMTG/IMCL
- biochemical biopsy;
- microscopy;
- 1H-MRS;
- methods can produce different apparent effect sizes.

### Blood glucose
- concentration measurement;
- CGM/interstitial glucose;
- tracer kinetics needed to separate appearance vs disposal.

Canonical rule:

**substrate measurement method is part of the interpretation.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

18. substrate repletion ≠ readiness.

## 39. Established content-level findings

### HIGH

1. PCr can deplete rapidly during high-intensity work and resynthesizes primarily through oxidative ATP production.
2. PCr recovery is much faster than glycogen recovery but is not governed by one universal recovery duration.
3. Muscle glycogen depletion depends on intensity, volume, recruitment and starting state.
4. Resistance exercise can substantially deplete local muscle glycogen.
5. Muscle glycogen depletion is heterogeneous across fibres/muscles.
6. Low muscle glycogen does not mean all glycogen is absent.
7. Carbohydrate availability materially affects rapid muscle glycogen repletion.
8. Large carbohydrate intake does not guarantee complete muscle glycogen restoration within 12 h.
