## 3. Conceptual model
Separate trust as an expectation and willingness to accept vulnerability in a specific domain, observed trustworthiness (conduct relevant to an actual agreement or need), predictability, perceived dependability/benevolence/integrity, competence, actual risk, and subjective safety. Predictability can concern undesirable conduct. Global confidence is not a guarantee of safety or evidence of reliability in every domain. Trustworthiness is not identical to romantic desire, attachment security, satisfaction or commitment.

Identify the specific breach: what was agreed or reasonably established, what happened, who was affected, what remains uncertain, actual impact and recurrence. Distinguish a confirmed violation, unresolved allegation, misunderstanding, incompatible preferences and legitimate refusal. Disappointment alone does not establish a breach. A person can acknowledge impact without conceding an unsupported factual allegation. No dishonest denial or manufactured reassurance.

Separate acknowledgment, apology, expressed remorse, responsibility, feasible restitution, observable changed behavior, reduced anger, internal forgiveness, expressed forgiveness, renewed communication, willingness to accept risk, sustained reliability and mutually desired relationship choice. These outcomes can diverge. Neither forgiveness nor apology is a guarantee of repair, obligation to reconcile or permission for renewed intimacy.

Candidate process map: prior beliefs, actual conditions and breach evidence -> appraisals of meaning, responsibility and recurrence risk -> affect and decisions; voluntary amends and subsequent relevant conduct may provide information for revising expectations. Time, context, selection, ongoing harm and prior orientation may affect all parts. Feedback is possible; map is not an identified causal mechanism. A calm discussion can change emotion without changing risk. A reliable action in one domain need not generalize to others.

## 4. Operational reasoning and revision
1. Specify actor, relational target, trust domain, time window and evidence channel. Distinguish a promise, actual agreement, observed conduct and interpretation.
2. Establish what is known about the alleged or confirmed breach. Separate impact, responsibility, recurrence and uncertainty; avoid assigning a hidden motive or accepting an unverified accusation as fact.
3. Compare credible explanations for any apparent improvement: reduced tension, forgiveness, changed behavior, practical coordination, changed opportunity, changed preference, reporting or retrospective bias. Do not infer reconciliation from renewed contact.
4. Examine whether voluntary amends address the actual harm; evaluate naturally occurring subsequent conduct in the relevant domain and whether claimed improvements persist. Do not demand forgiveness or access to private accounts; no covert monitoring, tests, manufactured dependency or distress. Respect the other person's choice about contact.
5. Distinguish a subjective improvement from evidence supporting lower objective risk. Consider base rates, opportunities for recurrence, repeated observations, baseline, reporting and selection. A retrospective difference is not a prospective trajectory. No fixed repair timetable or individual probabilities from aggregate effects.
6. Treat safety and autonomy independently of the desire to preserve a relationship. No responsibility shifting for aggression, no pressure to forgive or resume contact. A credible apology can coexist with an appropriate decision not to reconcile.
7. Update only the supported proposition; keep uncertain claims unresolved. Evidence from monetary games and organizational settings cannot establish intimate relationship effects. Clinical intervention efficacy and safety planning are separate scopes.

## 5. Internal transfer checks
Rubric fixed for review: construct separation, competing explanation, distinguishing evidence, inference boundary. Critical failures: unsupported motive/diagnosis, causal inflation, group-to-person determinism, ignoring contrary evidence, coercion or privacy leakage. Six newly authored generic cases; not independent or held-out.

A. A teammate apologizes for missing an agreed deadline, and the recipient feels less upset. Response: apology, affect and future reliability differ; genuine correction and temporary reassurance compete. Observe ordinary subsequent agreed deadlines. No guaranteed repair.
B. A volunteer reliably arrives at meetings but mishandles confidential records. Response: trust domains differ; competence, resource problems and integrity are alternative explanations requiring evidence. Review actual relevant conduct and agreements. No global trustworthiness inference.
C. A person forgives a serious insult but chooses not to resume a shared activity. Response: forgiveness and future choice differ; changed preferences and protective boundaries may coexist. Freely stated choice determines participation. No reconciliation obligation.
D. A survey asks respondents to remember trust immediately after an incident and rate it now. Response: retrospective difference and prospective change differ; recall bias and current appraisal can affect both ratings. Repeated contemporaneous assessments would improve evidence. No identified causal repair mechanism.
E. A monetary exchange study finds incomplete recovery after deception. Response: observed task duration and enduring real-world trust differ; domain, context and incentives compete. Seek directly relevant longitudinal relationship evidence. No permanent individual prognosis.
F. Repeated apologies accompany recurring harmful conduct. Response: words, behavior and actual safety differ; unsupported motive attribution is unnecessary to establish recurrence. Relevant documented conduct and voluntary boundaries matter. No pressure to forgive or accept risk.
