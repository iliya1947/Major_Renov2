## 3. Integrated construct map
Attribution is an explanation offered for an event or action, not direct access to the process that caused it. Separate observed action, opportunity and constraints, intentionality, actor's reported reasons, candidate motivation, disposition, transient state, actual impact, causal attribution and responsibility judgment. A situational explanation does not automatically remove responsibility, and a negative outcome does not by itself demonstrate harmful intent. A trait description is not a causal explanation without additional evidence.

Person–situation inference should compare predictions rather than assign causes by a rigid internal/external dichotomy. Conceptually, diagnosticity depends on how probable the same observation would be under competing explanations. If two hypotheses make the same prediction, the observation cannot distinguish them; a constraint need not make all actions equally likely under all dispositions. Prior beliefs, missing data, selection of episodes and hindsight may distort inference. This is qualitative reasoning, not a fitted Bayesian model or grounds for invented probabilities.

Norm analysis requires the relevant group and setting, actual behavior prevalence, actual approval, perceived prevalence, perceived approval, personal endorsement, explicit agreement and perceived/actual sanctions to be distinguished. Public conformity need not imply private agreement. Multiple reference groups can have conflicting expectations; a reported norm may be mistaken. Do not assume a norm caused an individual's act because the act is consistent with it. Distinguish a freely chosen preference from compliance, resource constraint or social expectation; these can coexist.

Candidate feedback model: prior beliefs, preferences, learned norms and actual circumstances influence appraisal and action; another person's interpretation can influence their response; subsequent experience may revise attributions and expectations. Relationship quality can affect interpretation as well as be associated with later interpretation-related outcomes. This map is a hypothesis space, not an identified causal graph. The actor–observer main effect, self-serving valence pattern, correspondence bias and spontaneous trait inference are distinct constructs.

## 4. Operational reasoning and revision
1. Define actor, target, exact event, context, timescale, outcome and evidence channel. Preserve facts and volunteered reports separately from explanations.
2. Distinguish actions, opportunities, stated reasons, inferred intentions and consequences. Consider personal preferences, actual constraints, transient state, role demands, relevant norms and prior history before attributing behavior to a stable trait.
3. Compare credible explanations using ordinary observations and volunteered clarification. Ask whether they make distinguishable predictions when opportunity, context or norms change. Do not count repeated retellings as independent evidence.
4. Distinguish actual norm from perceived norm and personal endorsement, specifying referent group and whether the expectation is descriptive or injunctive. Do not infer endorsement from conformity or use manufactured norm claims.
5. Evaluate evidence design, task context, comparators, base rates, uncertainty, direction of prediction and measurement. Do not equate absence of a significant association with equivalence, or an association with cause. Preserve contrasting findings rather than imposing a universal attribution-error theory.
6. Prefer the narrowest supported claim, and specify conditions that could weaken it. No unsupported motive attribution, diagnostic labels or numerical personal probabilities. Respect freely expressed choices and safety. Do not exploit social expectations or fabricate consensus to pressure another person.
7. Keep perspective taking, empathic accuracy and criterion validity open for the next block; no inference of accurate mental-state access from the plausibility of an explanation.

## 5. Internal transfer checks
Rubric before evaluation: construct separation, credible competing explanation, discriminating evidence, inference boundary. Critical errors: deterministic transfer, unsupported motive/diagnosis, causal inflation, disregard of contrary evidence, coercion or privacy breach. Six neutral self-authored cases unrelated to private material.

A. A speaker reads an assigned position in a debate. Response: expressed argument and endorsed belief differ; role constraint and personal preference may coexist. Compare freely chosen positions in comparable contexts. No definite private belief inferred.
B. A survey finds average self–other causal attribution differences near zero but opposing effects by outcome valence. Response: perspective main effect and valence interaction differ; measurement and self-evaluation processes compete. Inspect outcome-specific comparisons and uncertainty. No universal asymmetry or universal absence of bias.
C. A volunteer misses a meeting after a transport interruption. Response: action, opportunity, intention and responsibility differ; transport, scheduling and preference may contribute. Check agreed arrangements and naturally comparable occasions. No stable unreliability inferred from one event.
D. Two members of a club each believe most others approve a rule, but an anonymous voluntary survey indicates otherwise. Response: perceived approval and measured approval differ; uncertainty, selection and public conformity matter. Compare representative reports and actual behavior. No motive inferred for any member.
E. A field experiment shows opposite changes in a behavior for low and high baseline groups. Response: baseline-dependent outcome and average treatment contrast differ; regression to mean, design and comparison conditions matter. Inspect assigned comparisons and uncertainty. No universal norm effect or interpersonal influence prescription.
F. A panel model produces different prospective findings when causal and responsibility attributions are combined. Response: constructs and model estimands differ; common causes and measurement structure may contribute. Compare prespecified models, measurement validity and later observations. No identified causal direction.
