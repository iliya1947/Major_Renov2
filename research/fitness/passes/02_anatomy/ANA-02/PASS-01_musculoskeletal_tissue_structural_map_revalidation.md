## 2. Whole-system structural model

The useful project model is not a single linear rope.

A simplified path can be written:

`contractile muscle → intramuscular connective-tissue network → aponeurosis/tendon → MTJ/enthesis interfaces → bone → joint tissues → adjacent skeletal segment`

But actual force transfer is distributed through a **3D connective-tissue and skeletal network**.

Therefore:

- muscle fibers do not transmit force only through one distal tendon tip;
- tendon/aponeurosis geometry varies;
- bone, articular surfaces, capsule, ligaments and fibrocartilaginous structures jointly shape joint loading;
- local tissue interfaces matter because material properties change across them.

Project rule:

**Use the chain as a navigation map, not as a literal single serial cable.**

## 3. Bone

Bone is living mineralized connective tissue.

Structural components relevant here:

1. cortical/compact bone;
2. trabecular/cancellous bone;
3. periosteum;
4. endosteum;
5. marrow spaces where present;
6. vascular and neural channels;
7. subchondral/articular interfaces where relevant.

### Cortical bone

Dense external architecture providing substantial structural support and resistance to bending/torsional loads.

### Trabecular bone

Porous internal lattice with high surface area and load-oriented architecture, prominent in vertebrae and near joint ends.

Project rule:

**cancellous/trabecular bone is not “soft bone”; it is mineralized bone with different architecture.**

### Periosteum

Vascularized and innervated external connective-tissue covering of bone except at articular surfaces and certain attachment regions.

### Endosteum

Cellular lining of internal bone surfaces including marrow cavity, trabeculae and internal canals.

Detailed remodeling and mechanobiology → PHY.

## 4. Articular cartilage

Articular cartilage of most synovial joints is specialized **hyaline cartilage** covering articulating bone surfaces.

Core structural properties:

1. extracellular matrix rich in type II collagen and proteoglycan-based components;
2. sparse chondrocytes;
3. no intrinsic vascular network in healthy articular cartilage;
4. no intrinsic innervation;
5. zonal organization through depth;
6. integration with calcified cartilage / subchondral bone interface.

Current 2026 interface literature explicitly treats the joint as a multi-tissue organ and articular cartilage as a tissue with intrinsic zonal interfaces.

Project role:

- provides a low-friction articulating surface;
- distributes compressive/contact loads;
- works together with synovial fluid, subchondral bone and joint geometry.

Do not reduce it to a generic “cushion”.

### Nutrition

Because healthy articular cartilage is avascular, nutrient/metabolite exchange depends substantially on the synovial environment and diffusion.

Detailed cartilage mechanobiology → PHY/MEC.

---

## 5. Cartilage classes

### Hyaline cartilage

Relevant to:
- articular surfaces;
- growth plates in immature skeleton;
- costal and airway structures.

### Fibrocartilage

Collagen-rich tissue adapted to combined tensile/compressive demands.

Relevant to:
- menisci;
- intervertebral discs;
- selected articular discs;
- transitional regions such as some entheses.

### Elastic cartilage

Elastic-fiber-rich tissue such as external ear/epiglottis.

Minimal direct strength-training relevance.

## 6. Synovial joint capsule

A synovial joint has an enclosing capsular architecture.

For project use distinguish:

### Fibrous capsule

Dense connective-tissue outer component attached around joint margins.

Functions structurally:
- encloses joint space;
- contributes passive restraint;
- limits/guides translation and rotation depending on joint/position;
- blends with or is reinforced by ligaments in some regions.

### Synovium

Specialized vascular connective-tissue interface facing the joint cavity.

Modern literature supports treating the synovium as structurally/functionally distinctive rather than casually equating it with the fibrous capsule.

Project terminology:

**joint capsule** may be used broadly for the enclosing capsular unit, but when structural precision matters distinguish **fibrous capsule** from **synovial membrane/synovium**.

---

## 7. Synovial membrane

Synovium lines the non-articular internal surfaces of diarthrodial joint spaces and is also associated with bursae and tendon sheaths.

Healthy synovium has:

1. intimal/lining layer;
2. subintimal/sublining layer;
3. rich vascular/stromal organization compared with articular cartilage.

Important correction preserved from legacy ANA-02:

**normal synovium does not cover the articular cartilage surface itself.**

It interfaces with synovial fluid and supplies components of the joint fluid environment.

---

## 8. Synovial fluid

Fluid occupying the synovial cavity.

Structural-functional roles relevant here:

1. lubrication;
2. facilitation of low-friction articulation;
3. nutrient/metabolite transport for avascular articular cartilage;
4. integration with cartilage/synovium joint interfaces.

Detailed fluid rheology and load-dependent lubrication → PHY/MEC.

---

## 9. Ligament

Ligaments are specialized collagen-rich connective tissues that connect/stabilize skeletal structures across joints and contribute to restraint/guidance of motion.

Project functions:

1. passive restraint;
2. joint stability;
3. guidance/constraint of motion;
4. sensory/proprioceptive contribution in at least some ligaments.

Current literature supports that ligament structure is specialized for complex loading and is not identical to tendon tissue.

Project rule:

**“ligament = rope from bone to bone” is only a mnemonic, not a sufficient anatomical model.**

Ligaments may be:
- capsular;
- extracapsular;
- intracapsular;
- cord-like;
- sheet-like.

Adaptation and injury → PHY/MED.

---

## 10. Tendon

Tendon is specialized connective tissue transmitting muscle-generated force toward skeletal attachment.

Current tendon literature emphasizes hierarchical extracellular-matrix architecture dominated by collagen organization.

Useful structural layers include:

1. collagen fibrillar hierarchy;
2. endotenon/endotenonium between bundles;
3. epitenon/epitenonium at tendon surface;
4. paratenon/peritendon in selected tendons;
5. synovial sheath where present;
6. tendon proper.

Project functions:

- force transmission;
- energy storage/return in some tendons;
- spatially connecting muscle belly to distant attachment;
- participating in the muscle–tendon unit.

Tendon is not a detachable inert cord at the end of muscle.

Detailed stiffness/adaptation → PHY/MEC.

---

## 11. Aponeurosis

Aponeurosis = broad sheet-like tendinous connective structure.

Useful distinctions:

- tendon often cord-like;
- aponeurosis often sheet-like and receives muscle fibers over a broad area;
- both can participate in the same force-transmission continuum.

Examples later matter in abdominal wall and pennate muscles.

Do not treat “aponeurosis” as unrelated to tendon tissue merely because geometry differs.

---

## 12. Intramuscular connective tissue

Core layers:

1. **endomysium** — around individual muscle fibers;
2. **perimysium** — around fascicles;
3. **epimysium** — surrounding whole muscle.

These networks integrate structurally with tendinous/aponeurotic tissues.

Project implication:

**force transmission is not exclusively longitudinal fiber-tip → tendon transmission.**

Detailed lateral force transmission and mechanics → MEC/PHY.

---

## 13. Myotendinous junction (MTJ)

MTJ = specialized interface between muscle and tendon.

Current 2024–2026 reviews describe it as a mechanically critical interface transferring contractile force from muscle into tendon.

Key structural principle:

- interdigitated/folded architecture increases interface area and helps manage stress concentration.

Critical distinction:

- **MTJ = muscle ↔ tendon interface**
- **enthesis = tendon/ligament/connective tissue ↔ bone interface**

Do not use these terms interchangeably.

---

## 14. Enthesis

Enthesis = attachment region where tendon, ligament or related connective tissue attaches to bone.

Current anatomy distinguishes at least:

1. fibrous entheses;
2. fibrocartilaginous entheses.

### Fibrocartilaginous enthesis

Classically shows a graded transition:

1. tendon/ligament;
2. uncalcified fibrocartilage;
3. calcified fibrocartilage;
4. bone.

This is not a simple abrupt glue line.

Current 2026 Journal of Anatomy review emphasizes that macro- and micro-architecture distribute stress across the compliant-soft-tissue → rigid-bone transition.

---

## 15. Enthesis organ

Current 2026 review strongly supports the **enthesis organ** concept for appropriate sites.

The functional unit can include neighboring structures such as:

- enthesis;
- sesamoid/periosteal fibrocartilage;
- bursa;
- fat pad;
- retinaculum.

Project implication:

**some insertions should be understood as a local stress-dissipation system rather than one isolated attachment point.**

Do not universalize every insertion as an identical enthesis organ; apply site-specific anatomy later.

---

## 16. Bursa

Bursa = small synovial-lined fluid-containing sac positioned where adjacent tissues must glide and local compression/friction would otherwise be greater.

Common interfaces:
- tendon ↔ bone;
- skin ↔ bone;
- muscle/tendon ↔ neighboring tissue.

Project role:
- permits gliding;
- reduces local friction.

Critical boundary:

**presence of a bursa is normal anatomy; it does not imply bursitis.**

---

## 17. Tendon sheath

Synovial tendon sheath = tubular synovial gliding structure surrounding tendons in selected constrained paths.

Common examples:
- wrist/hand;
- ankle/foot.

Distinguish from bursa:

- bursa = localized sac-like gliding interface;
- tendon sheath = elongated/tubular structure around a tendon path.

---

## 18. Retinaculum

Retinaculum = localized fibrous thickening retaining tendons near the skeleton.

Structural consequences:
- limits bowstringing;
- organizes tendon path;
- changes local line of pull/contact mechanics.

Detailed pulley mechanics → regional anatomy/MEC.

---

## 19. Meniscus

Menisci are intra-articular fibrocartilaginous structures, especially prominent in the knee.

Current 2025–2026 literature supports roles in:

1. load distribution;
2. contact mechanics;
3. joint stability;
4. shock attenuation;
5. interaction with capsuloligamentous structures.

Project rule:

**do not describe meniscus only as a “shock absorber”.**

Its circumferential/radial fiber architecture is part of load-transfer mechanics and will be handled in knee/MEC topics.

---

## 20. Articular disc

Articular disc = fibrocartilaginous intra-articular structure found in selected joints.

Relevant examples:
- temporomandibular joint;
- sternoclavicular joint;
- distal radioulnar complex.

For current purpose, distinguish category from meniscus and defer joint-specific morphology.

---

## 21. Labrum

Labrum = fibrocartilaginous rim around the margin of a socket-like joint.

Key examples:
- glenoid labrum;
- acetabular labrum.

Structural role:
- increases effective socket depth/contact geometry;
- contributes to sealing/load distribution;
- interfaces with capsule/ligament attachments.

Project rule:

**labrum ≠ meniscus.**

Regional detail → shoulder/hip topics.

## 22. Fascia — current terminology conflict

This topic requires explicit uncertainty.

### What is established

Anatomical literature consistently recognizes named fibrous connective-tissue sheets/sheaths that:

- compartmentalize;
- connect;
- invest muscles/structures;
- form intermuscular septa;
- provide attachment surfaces;
- influence local tissue gliding.

### What is disputed

Recent literature does **not** show one uncontested definition of a body-wide “fascial system”.

2025:
- Stecco et al. proposed a broad anatomical “fascial system” model.

2025:
- another Clinical Anatomy review argued for a narrower traditional fascia concept centered on fibrous membranes.

2026:
- Sharkey explicitly argued that fascia should **not** be classified as an anatomical “system” under established nomenclature principles.

### Project rule

Do not settle this terminology dispute by assertion.

Use named structures:

- thoracolumbar fascia;
- crural fascia;
- brachial fascia;
- intermuscular septum;
- rectus sheath;
- specific aponeurosis;

and describe the exact anatomy/function being claimed.

Do **not** infer whole-body causal “fascial chains” merely from tissue continuity.

If a later claim depends on long-range fascial force transmission, it requires its own biomechanics/evidence review.

---

## 23. Intermuscular septa / deep fascial sheets

Intermuscular septa are fibrous partitions extending between compartment boundaries and often toward bone.

Roles:
- compartment organization;
- separation of muscle groups;
- attachment surface;
- relation to neurovascular pathways.

These are concrete named anatomical structures and can be discussed without adopting a broad “fascial system” ontology.

---

## 24. Tissue vascularity and innervation — structural rule

Avoid slogans.

### Broad patterns

- bone is vascular and innervated;
- periosteum is particularly richly innervated;
- healthy articular cartilage is avascular and aneural;
- synovium is vascular and innervated/stromal;
- tendon and ligament vascularity varies by region;
- fibrocartilage vascularity varies by tissue and zone;
- bursae/synovial tissues have their own vascularized lining architecture.

Project prohibitions:

1. “tendons have no blood supply” — false simplification.
2. “cartilage hurts because cartilage has pain fibers” — incorrect for healthy articular cartilage.
3. “poor vascularity = no adaptation” — physiology claim, not anatomy.

---

## 25. Tissue anisotropy

Many musculoskeletal connective tissues have direction-dependent structure because collagen/fiber architecture is organized.

This is an anatomical fact relevant to later mechanics.

But:

**anisotropic structure ≠ a complete mechanical model.**

Stress–strain behavior, viscoelasticity, strain rate and tissue-specific loading → MEC/PHY.

## 26. Canonical structural distinctions

Project standard:

1. **bone** — mineralized connective tissue / skeletal structural substrate;
2. **articular cartilage** — avascular hyaline cartilage on articulating surfaces;
3. **fibrous capsule** — dense connective enclosure of synovial joint;
4. **synovium** — specialized vascular lining/interface of non-articular joint cavity surfaces;
5. **ligament** — collagen-rich skeletal stabilizing/restraint structure;
6. **tendon** — muscle-force-transmitting connective tissue;
7. **aponeurosis** — sheet-like tendinous structure;
8. **MTJ** — muscle–tendon interface;
9. **enthesis** — tendon/ligament/connective-tissue–bone attachment;
10. **bursa** — synovial-lined gliding sac;
11. **tendon sheath** — tubular synovial gliding structure around selected tendon paths;
12. **retinaculum** — fibrous tendon-retaining thickening;
13. **meniscus/disc** — intra-articular fibrocartilaginous structure;
14. **labrum** — fibrocartilaginous socket rim;
15. **fascia** — named fibrous connective-tissue layer/sheet; define exact structure instead of assuming one universal fascial ontology.

## 27. Anatomy vs physiology vs biomechanics vs pathology boundary

### Anatomy answers

- what structure exists;
- where it is;
- what tissues compose/interface with it;
- what it connects;
- gross/histological organizational relationships.

### Physiology answers

- adaptation/remodeling;
- metabolism;
- cellular signaling;
- healing biology;
- mechanotransduction.

### Biomechanics answers

- forces;
- stress/strain;
- stiffness;
- moment arms;
- material behavior;
- contact mechanics;
- load sharing.

### Medicine answers

- injury;
- pain;
- inflammation;
- degeneration;
- diagnosis;
- treatment.

Project rule:

**anatomical presence or loading does not imply pathology.**

Examples:
- bursa ≠ bursitis;
- enthesis ≠ enthesitis;
- tendon loading ≠ tendinopathy;
- cartilage loading ≠ cartilage damage.

## 28. What changed versus legacy ANA-02

### Confirmed

The legacy structural distinctions remain broadly valid:

- cortical vs trabecular bone;
- articular cartilage;
- fibrous capsule / synovium;
- tendon vs ligament;
- MTJ vs enthesis;
- enthesis organ;
- bursa vs tendon sheath;
- retinaculum;
- meniscus/disc vs labrum;
- intramuscular connective-tissue hierarchy.

### Strengthened / updated

1. 2026 enthesis review directly supports graded attachment architecture and enthesis-organ concept.
2. 2024–2026 MTJ literature reinforces MTJ as a specialized muscle–tendon interface, distinct from enthesis.
3. 2026 tendon literature reinforces hierarchical ECM organization rather than inert “cord” imagery.
4. 2026 cartilage-interface literature supports the joint as an integrated multi-tissue organ and healthy articular cartilage as nonvascular.
5. 2025–2026 meniscus literature reinforces load distribution/contact/stability roles beyond “shock absorption”.
6. Fascia terminology is now explicitly marked **CONTESTED** rather than treated as one settled broad classification.

### Corrected emphasis

The legacy file used a broad fascia taxonomy from an NCBI review that itself notes definitional expansion. Current 2025–2026 literature shows active disagreement about whether fascia constitutes a body-wide anatomical “system”.

Therefore the canonical project rule becomes:

**name the specific fascia/structure; do not assume “fascial system” as settled anatomical ontology.**

## 29. Established findings

### HIGH

1. Bone contains cortical and trabecular architectures plus periosteal/endosteal interfaces.
2. Healthy articular cartilage is specialized avascular hyaline cartilage integrated with synovial fluid and subchondral bone.
3. Fibrous capsule and synovium are structurally distinct components/interfaces of a synovial joint.
4. Tendon, ligament, MTJ and enthesis are distinct structures.
5. Fibrocartilaginous entheses contain graded transitional architecture.
6. Enthesis-organ concept is established for relevant attachment sites.
7. Tendons have hierarchical ECM organization and specialized gliding coverings/sheaths depending on location.
8. Menisci are fibrocartilaginous load-distributing/stabilizing structures, not merely shock absorbers.
9. Bursae, tendon sheaths and retinacula are structurally distinct gliding/retaining structures.
10. Named fascial sheets/septa are real anatomical structures.

### MODERATE / CONTESTED TERMINOLOGY

1. The broad ontology and boundaries of a body-wide “fascial system” remain disputed in current anatomical literature.
2. Exact fascia classification should therefore be source- and structure-specific.
