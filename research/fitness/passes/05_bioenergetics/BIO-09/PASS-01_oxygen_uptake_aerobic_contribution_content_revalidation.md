## 1. Qualification purpose / Question Card

A fitness trainer needs a correct VO2/aerobic-contribution model to avoid:

- treating pulmonary VO2 as a direct measure of muscle ATP production;
- calling oxygen deficit “oxygen missing from the body”;
- assuming oxidative metabolism begins only after VO2 catches up;
- assuming one fixed time constant applies to every person/intensity;
- assuming steady state exists at every constant workload;
- treating VO2max and VO2peak as automatically interchangeable;
- treating failure to show a classic VO2 plateau as proof that maximal effort was not reached;
- assuming high-intensity exercise is aerobically silent;
- treating EPOC as repayment of one simple “oxygen debt”;
- equating EPOC with PCr resynthesis alone;
- assuming high post-exercise VO2 implies a large chronic fat-loss effect;
- assuming between-set recovery is metabolically inactive;
- equating VO2max with cardiac output alone;
- equating VO2max with mitochondrial capacity alone.

Primary trainer-level decisions supported later:

- interpret oxygen uptake during exercise transitions;
- understand why oxidative ATP contribution lags ATP demand without being “off”;
- distinguish steady-state vs non-steady-state exercise;
- interpret VO2max/VO2peak terminology cautiously;
- understand aerobic contribution during high-intensity and intermittent work;
- interpret recovery oxygen uptake without “oxygen debt” simplification;
- separate O2 delivery, extraction and mitochondrial utilization.

Question type:

- primarily **mechanism + evidence mapping + measurement interpretation**.

Target population/context:

- healthy adults and athletes;
- continuous and intermittent exercise;
- maximal/submaximal exercise;
- recovery between efforts.

## 2. VO2 — what it represents

PASS at content level.

VO2 is the **rate of oxygen consumption**.

At whole-body level during exercise, pulmonary gas exchange is commonly used to estimate systemic VO2.

By the Fick principle:

`VO2 = Q × (CaO2 − CvO2)`

where:

- Q = cardiac output;
- CaO2 = arterial O2 content;
- CvO2 = mixed venous O2 content.

PMID **38444762** restates the Fick formulation and emphasizes that VO2 is a **flow rate**, not simply a concentration or amount.

PMID **37429332** describes pulmonary VO2 as dynamically linked to cellular O2 consumption through integrated:

- cardiovascular;
- pulmonary;
- hematologic;
- muscular/metabolic systems.

Canonical rule:

**VO2 is an integrated oxygen-consumption flux; pulmonary VO2 is not a direct one-to-one measurement of local muscle mitochondrial ATP production.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 3. Pulmonary VO2 vs muscle VO2

Pulmonary VO2 is influenced by:

- lung gas stores;
- circulation/transit delay;
- cardiac output;
- arterial O2 content;
- active muscle O2 extraction/utilization;
- non-exercising tissue contribution.

During steady-state whole-body exercise, pulmonary VO2 is a useful approximation of systemic oxidative metabolic demand.

During rapid transitions:

- lung/circulation dynamics create delays;
- breath-by-breath algorithms matter;
- pulmonary and local muscle kinetics are related but not perfectly identical instant by instant.

PMID **37983192** showed that even the breath-by-breath gas-exchange algorithm alters estimated VO2 kinetic parameters.

Canonical rule:

**pulmonary VO2 kinetics are a useful whole-body proxy for oxidative metabolism, but local muscle VO2 and pulmonary VO2 are not identical instantaneous signals.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 4. Oxygen deficit

PASS at content level.

At exercise onset, ATP demand rises faster than pulmonary oxidative O2 uptake reaches its new requirement.

The resulting **oxygen deficit** is an estimate of the gap between:

- the O2/oxidative energy requirement that would correspond to the target workload under the model;
- the accumulated measured oxygen uptake during the transition.

That gap is met by greater non-oxidative contribution and use of stored high-energy phosphates while oxidative metabolism accelerates.

Canonical rule:

**oxygen deficit is an indirect energetic construct describing delayed oxidative adjustment, not a literal deficit of oxygen molecules inside the whole body.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 5. Oxygen deficit ≠ anaerobic energy measured directly

Oxygen deficit / accumulated oxygen deficit methods require assumptions about:

- O2 demand at the target workload;
- extrapolation of submaximal VO2-work relationships;
- efficiency;
- exercise mode;
- pacing;
- non-steady-state VO2;
- recovery/metabolic accounting.

BIO-08 closure already established that:

- MAOD has relatively strong validity/reliability evidence among available methods;
- no gold standard directly measures all anaerobic ATP flux in vivo.

PMID **39369141** further shows that alternative AOD estimates can agree under some conditions but diverge under more severe hypoxia.

Canonical rule:

**oxygen deficit is a method-derived estimate of non-oxidative energy provision, not direct measurement of “anaerobic ATP.”**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 6. VO2 on-kinetics

PASS at content level.

After a step increase in work rate, pulmonary VO2 generally rises toward the new metabolic requirement.

Useful applied phases:

### Phase I / cardiodynamic response
Early pulmonary response influenced strongly by increased pulmonary blood flow and gas stores.

### Phase II / primary component
The major exponential-like rise associated more closely with working-muscle oxidative metabolism.

### Slow component
At sufficiently high constant work rates, VO2 may continue to rise beyond the primary-component prediction.

The classic monoexponential model remains useful for many moderate-intensity transitions, but current work shows model choice and intensity can alter parameter interpretation.

PMIDs **36135954**, **37983192**, **42103246**.

Canonical rule:

**VO2 kinetics describe the time-dependent adjustment of oxidative O2 uptake after a workload transition; they are model- and intensity-dependent rather than one universal fixed curve.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 7. Faster VO2 kinetics

Faster primary VO2 kinetics generally mean:

- oxidative ATP supply rises more rapidly after onset;
- oxygen deficit/non-oxidative supplementation needed during the transition is smaller, all else equal.

But faster kinetics do not automatically mean:

- higher VO2max;
- better performance in every task;
- greater mitochondrial content alone;
- greater O2 delivery alone.

A 2024 randomized training study PMID **39636436** found training could speed VO2 kinetics without a simple intensity-domain-specific relationship or direct correlation with performance indices.

Canonical rule:

**VO2-kinetic speed and VO2max are related aerobic characteristics but non-equivalent constructs.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 8. Exercise-intensity domains and steady state

PASS at content level.

For trainer-level use, distinguish:

### Moderate domain
A stable VO2 steady state is generally attainable.

### Heavy domain
A VO2 slow component can develop, but a delayed steady state can still be attainable below the heavy-severe boundary.

### Severe domain
VO2 continues to rise and a true metabolic steady state is not normally attained before exhaustion; VO2 may approach VO2max depending on intensity/duration.

The 2022 DALE model paper PMID **35482330** explicitly predicts:

- steady-state VO2 in moderate and heavy domains;
- no steady-state VO2 in severe-domain exercise.

Recent work on maximal metabolic steady state and CP/MLSS continues to support the practical distinction between sustainable steady-state and non-steady-state exercise (PMIDs **39196486**, **40124604**).

Canonical rule:

**constant workload ≠ guaranteed physiological steady state.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 9. VO2 slow component

The VO2 slow component is a delayed rise in VO2 during exercise above the moderate domain.

It is associated with:

- higher energetic cost over time;
- changes in muscle efficiency/recruitment;
- fatigue-related changes;
- severe/heavy exercise behavior.

Mechanism remains actively debated.

Current publications include competing interpretations about the relationship between the slow component and glycolytic flux:

- PMID **42435219** reported an association with estimated glycolytic contribution;
- PMID **42616103** argues the slow component is more consistent with declining rather than rising glycolytic rate;
- PMID **39466978** and PMID **37775591** emphasize unresolved mechanistic complexity.

Canonical rule:

**the VO2 slow component is a robust physiological phenomenon, but no single mechanistic explanation is retained as settled.**

Confidence: **HIGH phenomenon / MODERATE mechanism**.

---

## 10. Steady state

PASS at content level.

“Steady state” means a variable such as VO2 reaches a sufficiently stable level under a constant external workload.

It does not mean:

- all metabolic variables are perfectly constant;
- lactate, temperature, hormones, substrate use and fatigue are identical across time;
- exercise can continue indefinitely.

At workloads above maximal metabolic steady state:

- VO2 and other metabolic disturbances progressively increase;
- exercise tolerance becomes finite.

Canonical rule:

**VO2 steady state is a bounded physiological condition, not complete whole-body metabolic stasis.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 11. VO2max

PASS at content level.

VO2max is the maximal rate of whole-body oxygen uptake attainable during large-muscle exercise under the test conditions.

It is an integrated outcome of the oxygen cascade:

1. ventilation/gas exchange;
2. pulmonary diffusion;
3. cardiac output;
4. arterial O2 content;
5. muscle perfusion/diffusion;
6. O2 extraction;
7. mitochondrial utilization.

PMID **37429332** identifies VO2max as the standard expression of aerobic capacity.

Canonical rule:

**VO2max is an integrated systems-level capacity, not a direct isolated measure of cardiac function or mitochondrial function.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 12. VO2max vs VO2peak

PASS at content level.

Operational distinction:

### VO2peak
The highest VO2 value observed during a specific test.

### VO2max
The highest attainable VO2 when evidence is sufficient that a maximal physiological ceiling has been reached/confirmed under the test conditions.

A classic VO2 plateau can support VO2max identification but is not always observed.

Current studies show verification-phase interpretation is also protocol dependent:

- PMID **38238521** found verification added little value in well-trained adults under its protocols;
- PMID **41707053** found verification outcomes depend on workload and can create misleading apparent confirmation;
- PMID **33386984** found verification at appropriate workloads could help confirm values across fitness levels.

Therefore:

**VO2peak and VO2max should not be treated as automatically identical, but absence of a classic plateau alone does not prove that a valid maximal value was not reached.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual boundary / MODERATE-HIGH verification details**.

Exact testing/verification protocol remains TST.

## 13. VO2max is protocol/context dependent

Measured VO2max/peak can depend on:

- exercise mode;
- ramp/increment duration;
- familiarization;
- participant effort/tolerance;
- measurement system;
- averaging interval;
- verification strategy;
- environmental conditions;
- fatigue state.

Therefore:

**a reported VO2max/VO2peak is a measured test outcome under specified conditions, not a context-free immutable property.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 14. Aerobic contribution to high-intensity exercise

PASS at content level.

High-intensity/maximal exercise is not “aerobic off.”

The 2026 systematic review PMID **41965479** found:

- substantial aerobic contribution even during short maximal exercise;
- aerobic share increases as maximal exercise duration increases;
- modeled equal aerobic/anaerobic contribution around 78.6 s in the pooled dataset.

BIO-08 already established that this 78.6 s estimate is:

- pooled;
- method dependent;
- not a universal physiological threshold.

Canonical rule:

**high-intensity exercise can be anaerobic-dominant while oxidative ATP production is already active and increasing.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 15. Aerobic contribution can rise within repeated high-intensity work

Repeated high-intensity bouts can show:

- elevated baseline VO2 before subsequent work;
- faster/altered VO2 response;
- greater integrated oxidative contribution;
- reduced relative anaerobic contribution.

Classic intermittent exercise study PMID **15199225** showed active recovery increased aerobic energy yield in later high-intensity bouts through faster VO2 kinetics and higher VO2peak.

2025 retrospective HIIT analysis PMID **40724066** found VO2 can remain markedly elevated during recovery intervals, with mean VO2 similar between work and recovery across the pooled protocols studied.

Canonical rule:

**inter-bout recovery can sustain oxidative flux and materially influence the aerobic contribution of the next effort.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**, protocol dependent.

## 16. EPOC — definition

PASS at content level.

EPOC = **excess postexercise oxygen consumption**:

the elevation in oxygen consumption above the relevant resting/baseline level after exercise.

EPOC can include contributions from:

- PCr resynthesis;
- restoration of O2 stores;
- elevated circulation/ventilation;
- thermoregulatory cost;
- ion-gradient restoration;
- substrate cycling/oxidation;
- lactate oxidation/gluconeogenic handling;
- sympathetic/catecholamine effects;
- other recovery processes.

Canonical rule:

**EPOC is a multi-process postexercise oxygen-consumption response, not repayment of one single oxygen debt.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 17. EPOC ≠ PCr resynthesis

BIO-08 established that PCr resynthesis is oxidative and occurs during recovery.

But EPOC may persist beyond rapid PCr restoration and reflects additional processes.

Therefore:

**PCr resynthesis contributes to early recovery oxygen demand but does not equal total EPOC.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 18. EPOC magnitude and duration

EPOC depends on exercise characteristics.

The 2021 systematic review PMID **32656951** included **22 studies** comparing high-intensity interval/sprint exercise with moderate continuous exercise and generally found:

- higher short-duration EPOC after HIIE/SIE than MICE;
- higher long-duration EPOC after HIIE vs MICE in available studies;
- substantial methodological heterogeneity;
- need for standardized EPOC measurement.

Classic controlled work also shows intensity and duration affect EPOC magnitude (PMIDs **1861633**, **3558208**, **2384417**).

Recent 2024 older-adult isocaloric studies show modality/intensity effects can differ by protocol and population (PMIDs **38513180**, **36638413**).

Canonical rule:

**EPOC is dose/protocol dependent; no universal fixed percentage or duration is retained.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 19. EPOC and energy expenditure

EPOC adds to total postexercise energy expenditure.

But its practical size is context dependent.

Classic well-trained-subject data PMID **2384417** found 8-h EPOC represented a small fraction of total exercise + recovery O2 cost across tested conditions.

A randomized crossover study PMID **20175430** concluded that postexercise fat oxidation changes may be small relative to oxidation during the exercise bout.

Therefore:

**EPOC exists and can be larger after harder exercise, but it should not be marketed as a universally large calorie-burning effect.**

Confidence: **HIGH anti-overclaim boundary**.

Body-composition prescription remains NUT/PRO.

## 20. Aerobic system during between-set recovery

PASS at content level.

Between bouts/sets, oxidative metabolism supports restoration including:

- PCr resynthesis;
- ATP-dependent homeostatic processes;
- continued O2 consumption;
- substrate oxidation/redistribution.

The metabolic state during recovery affects the next bout.

Relevant evidence:

- PCr recovery studies from BIO-08;
- PMID **40724066** shows VO2 can remain high during recovery from short HIIT intervals;
- PMID **36253649** shows recovery duration and pre-exercise metabolic state can alter subsequent VO2 on-kinetics.

Canonical rule:

**recovery intervals are periods of active oxidative metabolism and can shape the energetic profile of the next work bout.**

Confidence: **HIGH concept / MODERATE-HIGH exact interval behavior**.

---

## 21. Aerobic recovery ≠ complete recovery

Elevated oxidative metabolism can support recovery while:

- PCr remains incompletely restored;
- pH remains altered;
- ionic state remains altered;
- glycogen is not restored;
- neuromuscular fatigue persists.

Therefore:

**high aerobic recovery flux ≠ complete recovery readiness.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

Detailed fatigue/recovery ownership remains BIO-10/11/12/REC.

## 22. Cardiovascular O2 delivery vs mitochondrial utilization

PASS at content level.

Whole-body VO2 is constrained by the integrated oxygen cascade.

Fick:

`VO2 = cardiac output × arteriovenous O2 difference`.

Conceptually separate:

### Convective O2 delivery
- cardiac output;
- arterial O2 content;
- blood volume/hemoglobin;
- pulmonary gas exchange.

### Peripheral O2 distribution/extraction
- muscle perfusion;
- capillary diffusion;
- microvascular distribution;
- arteriovenous extraction.

### Mitochondrial utilization
- mitochondrial content;
- respiratory capacity;
- oxidative enzymes;
- ADP/substrate control.

Canonical rule:

**O2 delivery, O2 extraction and mitochondrial utilization are linked but non-equivalent constraints.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 23. What limits VO2max depends on state/population

It is incorrect to state universally:

- “VO2max is limited only by the heart”;
- “VO2max is limited only by mitochondria.”

PMID **37429332** notes that young fit individuals are commonly limited primarily by O2-delivery capacity, while pathology can impose limitations at multiple steps.

2026 review PMID **41845944** emphasizes systemic O2 delivery as a primary limitation in elite endurance athletes while also retaining strong peripheral extraction/capillary/mitochondrial contributions.

Human training study PMID **38048175** found endurance training could shift the limitation of muscle VO2max in normoxia from utilization capacity toward O2 transport capacity.

A 2025 training study PMID **40047443** found VO2max improvements after higher-intensity training were supported predominantly by central adaptations in that cohort, without proving a universal central-only model.

Canonical rule:

**the rate-limiting step in the oxygen cascade is state-, population-, environment- and training-dependent.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 24. VO2 kinetics limitation ≠ VO2max limitation

A person may have:

- high VO2max but relatively slower kinetics;
- faster kinetics without a proportionate increase in VO2max.

A 2025 human study PMID **41458383** found VO2max correlated with VO2-kinetic speed across fitness levels, but O2-delivery kinetics were not the limiting correlate of VO2 kinetics; intracellular utilization appeared more influential in that cohort.

Canonical rule:

**determinants of VO2 kinetics and determinants of VO2max overlap but are not identical.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 25. Practical trainer-level framework

When interpreting oxygen uptake, ask:

1. Is this pulmonary whole-body VO2 or local muscle oxygenation/VO2?
2. Is the exercise onset, steady state, severe non-steady state, or recovery?
3. What is the intensity domain?
4. Is the measure instantaneous, breath-averaged, or integrated?
5. Is VO2max verified or only VO2peak observed?
6. Is the task continuous or intermittent?
7. Are we discussing O2 delivery, extraction, or mitochondrial utilization?
8. Is the “oxygen deficit” directly measured or model estimated?
9. Is EPOC being expressed relative to a measured resting baseline?
10. Are method/protocol/population limitations explicit?

## 29. Evidence-ledger candidates

1. VO2 is an integrated oxygen-consumption flux governed by cardiac output and arteriovenous O2 difference; pulmonary VO2 is not direct local muscle ATP production.
2. Oxygen deficit estimates the transient gap between modeled oxidative requirement and measured oxygen uptake; it is not direct anaerobic ATP measurement.
3. VO2 on-kinetics describe time-dependent oxidative adjustment and vary with intensity, initial metabolic state and analysis method.
4. Moderate exercise generally reaches steady state, heavy exercise can reach delayed steady state, while severe exercise normally does not achieve metabolic steady state before exhaustion.
5. VO2max is an integrated oxygen-cascade capacity; VO2peak is the highest observed test value and should not automatically be equated with confirmed VO2max.
6. High-intensity exercise can be anaerobic dominated while aerobic contribution is already substantial and rises with duration.
7. EPOC is a multi-process postexercise elevation in oxygen consumption whose magnitude/duration depend on exercise intensity, duration, mode and measurement method.
8. Oxidative metabolism remains active during between-bout recovery and supports PCr resynthesis/restoration; elevated recovery VO2 does not equal complete recovery.
9. O2 delivery, peripheral extraction and mitochondrial utilization are distinct but interacting determinants of exercise VO2.
10. The limiting step for VO2max and the limiting step for VO2 kinetics can differ and are context/training-state dependent.
