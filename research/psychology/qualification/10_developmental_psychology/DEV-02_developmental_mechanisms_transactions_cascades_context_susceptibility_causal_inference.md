# DEV-02 — Developmental mechanisms and trajectories: transactions, cascades, context, cumulative exposure, susceptibility, and causal inference

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public developmental-science evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

DEV-01 established that chronological age is not a mechanism, sensitive periods require system/exposure/outcome specificity, and developmental measurement/design can itself distort trajectories.

DEV-02 addresses the next dependency:

**How do children and environments influence one another over time, how should family/peer/school/community contexts be integrated, when are developmental cascades or cumulative-risk claims justified, and how should differential susceptibility, equifinality, multifinality, and causal inference be handled without treating early experience as destiny?**

High-risk errors to prevent:
- "parenting causes the child outcome";
- "child behavior is only a response to parenting";
- "family effect = parent-driven effect";
- "peer similarity = peer influence";
- "school belonging causes better outcomes because it predicts them";
- "one context dominates development";
- "developmental cascade = causal chain";
- "cross-lagged path = causal direction";
- "earlier risk = stronger risk";
- "more ACE types = known mechanism";
- "cumulative risk count = exposure dose";
- "timing effect = sensitive period";
- "protective factor = protective mechanism";
- "moderator = mechanism";
- "interaction p-value = differential susceptibility";
- "sensitive child = fixed personality type";
- "differential susceptibility always beats additive models";
- "vantage sensitivity = differential susceptibility";
- "diathesis–stress = differential susceptibility";
- "same early risk = same adult outcome";
- "different early risks = different outcomes";
- "high-risk trajectory class = natural developmental type";
- "family/peer/school correlations prove cross-domain cascade";
- "within-person association = environmental causation";
- "controlling baseline solves all confounding";
- "longitudinal = causal";
- "early intervention effect proves the hypothesized developmental mechanism".

## Core rule

**Development is transactional, multilevel, and path-dependent: characteristics of the developing person influence environments they encounter and evoke, while family, peers, schools, communities, and historical conditions influence subsequent states and opportunities. Developmental pathways are therefore probabilistic. Cumulative exposure, timing, cascades, and susceptibility are competing/overlapping hypotheses that require explicit operationalization and causal identification rather than narrative plausibility.**

Canonical reasoning:

\`define person process + environmental process + timescale -> establish temporal ordering and repeated measures -> separate selection/evocation from environmental exposure -> distinguish additive, cumulative, timing-specific, interactive, and cascade hypotheses -> model family/peer/school/community levels -> test reciprocal paths and competing confounding -> verify measurement and trajectory robustness -> infer only the narrowest developmental mechanism supported\`.

## 1. Transactional development

Transactional development means:

**the developing person and environment influence one another over time**.

Possible directions include:
- caregiver -> child;
- child -> caregiver;
- peer group -> child;
- child -> peer selection;
- school -> behavior;
- behavior -> school belonging/response.

Transaction is not merely "both correlate."

It requires temporally ordered reciprocal influence hypotheses.

## 2. Person is an active developmental participant

Children and adolescents:
- select environments;
- evoke responses;
- interpret experiences;
- modify interactions;
- choose peers;
- seek or avoid contexts.

Development is not a one-way environment→person process.

## 3. Environment is also active

Environments:
- structure opportunities;
- provide reinforcement;
- impose constraints;
- communicate norms;
- allocate resources;
- respond to behavior.

Person activity does not imply environmental effects are weak.

## 4. Reciprocal influence can occur on different timescales

A child behavior can alter caregiver response:
- within minutes;
- over weeks;
- across years.

The resulting caregiver behavior can then influence later child behavior.

Sampling must match the hypothesized cycle.

Annual waves may miss fast transactions.

## 5. Cross-lagged association is not automatically transaction

A model showing:
- parenting T1 -> child behavior T2;
- child behavior T1 -> parenting T2

is consistent with transaction.

It can also reflect:
- stable unmeasured family factors;
- measurement bias;
- time-varying confounding;
- shared reporter effects.

Transactional interpretation requires more than two significant paths.

## 6. 2025 parenting–externalizing longitudinal evidence

A large prospective cohort:
- N=1,287;
- four waves;
- ages approximately 1–6

found:
- higher observed parenting quality predicted lower later externalizing behavior at one interval;
- higher child externalizing predicted lower later parenting quality at subsequent intervals.

This supports both parent-driven and child-driven pathways in that sample.

It does not establish a complete causal loop.

## 7. Transaction direction can change with development

The strength/direction of reciprocal paths can vary with:
- age;
- autonomy;
- school transition;
- family stress;
- measurement method.

A developmental transaction should not be assumed time-invariant.

## 8. Reporter effects can mimic asymmetry

Parent-reported:
- parenting;
- child behavior

can share method variance.

Observer/teacher/child reports may produce different path estimates.

Method must be considered before declaring one direction stronger.

## 9. Parent effects and child effects can be similar in magnitude

Earlier meta-analytic evidence on externalizing found:
- child-driven effects on later parental functioning around r=.087;
- parent-driven effects around r=.075.

This is a calibration example:
**child effects are not negligible simply because parenting is the focal predictor.**

## 10. Family is not one variable

Family context can include:
- caregiver sensitivity;
- warmth;
- hostility;
- discipline;
- conflict;
- coparenting;
- socioeconomic resources;
- household chaos;
- sibling relationships.

These constructs are not interchangeable.

## 11. Caregiver sensitivity

Caregiver sensitivity generally concerns:
- noticing child signals;
- interpreting them appropriately;
- responding contingently and promptly.

It is one developmental process, not a global "good parenting" score.

## 12. 2026 caregiver-sensitivity umbrella review

An umbrella review synthesized 17 meta-analyses.

Meta-umbrella estimates for caregiver sensitivity included approximately:
- attachment security: r=.25;
- cognition: r=.23;
- language: r=.26;
- socioemotional problems: r=-.07.

Heterogeneity was high.

Canonical implication:

**caregiver sensitivity has reproducible developmental associations, but effect magnitude varies by outcome and does not imply deterministic parent causation.**

## 13. Family association ≠ unique parent mechanism

Caregiver sensitivity can covary with:
- parental education;
- mental health;
- stress;
- household resources;
- child temperament.

Observed prediction may include:
- direct effects;
- correlated environments;
- selection;
- reciprocal child influence.

## 14. Sibling comparison can isolate some family-level confounding

Sibling designs can compare children sharing parts of:
- family;
- neighborhood;
- genetics.

They can help separate:
- absolute family environment;
- child-specific differential treatment.

But siblings are not identical exposures.

## 15. Parental differential treatment

A 2024 meta-analysis:
- 2,890 effect sizes;
- 88 sources;
- 43 samples;
- N=26,451

found:
- less-favored treatment related to more internalizing/externalizing behavior;
- larger between-sibling treatment differences also related to more problems.

This supports child-specific family-context relevance.

It still does not uniquely identify direction of causality.

## 16. Differential treatment can be child-evoked

Parents may respond differently because siblings differ in:
- temperament;
- behavior;
- needs.

Thus:
**different parenting -> different child outcome**
and
**different child characteristics -> different parenting**
can both operate.

## 17. Intergenerational continuity is modest

A 2025 multilevel meta-analysis of parenting across generations found:
- 408 effect sizes;
- 24 datasets;
- 51 articles;
- average intergenerational stability around r=.12.

Therefore:
**parenting patterns can transmit across generations, but continuity is far from destiny**.

## 18. Intergenerational similarity has multiple pathways

Possible contributors:
- learned parenting scripts;
- shared social context;
- genetics;
- socioeconomic continuity;
- partner selection;
- cultural norms.

An intergenerational correlation does not identify one mechanism.

## 19. Peer development includes selection and influence

Peer similarity can arise because:
1. people select similar peers;
2. peers influence one another;
3. both experience common environments.

Therefore:
**peer similarity ≠ peer influence**.

## 20. Peer selection

Selection means a young person is more likely to:
- choose;
- retain;
- be accepted by

peers with certain characteristics.

Selection precedes or co-occurs with socialization.

## 21. Peer influence/socialization

Peer influence means exposure to peer:
- behavior;
- norms;
- feedback;
- presence

changes later behavior/attitudes.

Need baseline behavior and credible peer-exposure measurement.

## 22. Longitudinal peer influence is small on average

A meta-analysis of 60 independent studies:
- 233 effects;
- externalizing, internalizing, and academic behaviors;
- baseline behavior controlled;
- peers measured by external informants

found an average cross-lagged peer-influence coefficient around:
- β=.08.

This supports real but modest longitudinal peer influence.

## 23. Experimental peer influence can be larger proximally

A 2026 preregistered meta-analysis:
- 79 independent reports;
- >9,500 adolescents

found immediate experimental peer-influence effects around:
- g=.47;
- high heterogeneity (I²=85%).

Peer-norm manipulations produced stronger effects than mere presence/feedback in moderator models.

## 24. Experimental and longitudinal peer effects answer different questions

Experimental studies often estimate:
- immediate response to manipulated peer cues.

Longitudinal studies estimate:
- naturalistic change over longer periods.

Larger experimental effect ≠ larger real-world developmental socialization.

Timescale and exposure differ.

## 25. Peer influence is not inherently harmful

Peers can influence:
- risk-taking;
- prosocial behavior;
- academic engagement;
- norms.

The 2026 experimental literature is dominated by risk-taking outcomes.

Do not generalize one behavior domain to all peer influence.

## 26. School context is developmental context

School supplies:
- peer ecology;
- adult relationships;
- norms;
- cognitive demands;
- institutional structure;
- opportunity.

School effects are not reducible to classroom instruction.

## 27. School belonging

School belonging concerns perceived:
- acceptance;
- inclusion;
- support;
- respect

within school.

It differs from:
- school engagement;
- school climate;
- academic commitment.

Jingle/jangle risk is substantial.

## 28. 2026 school-belonging meta-analysis

A systematic review/meta-analysis included:
- 53 articles cross-sectionally;
- longitudinal subset k=17, N=29,944.

School belonging and externalizing behavior showed:
- longitudinal overall r≈-.16;
- belonging -> later externalizing r≈-.17;
- externalizing -> later belonging r≈-.16.

This is compatible with reciprocal development.

It does not prove bidirectional causation.

## 29. School belonging can be partly child-evoked

Behavior problems can alter:
- teacher responses;
- peer acceptance;
- disciplinary exposure.

Thus lower belonging can be both:
- predictor;
- consequence.

## 30. Family, peer, and school systems interact

A child can experience:
- supportive family + adverse school;
- adverse family + supportive peers;
- supportive school + peer rejection.

No single context should be assumed dominant without direct evidence.

## 31. 2026 ABCD social-context review

A review of 64 high-impact ABCD studies emphasized:
- family;
- peer;
- school;
- community;
- identity/cultural

contexts across neurocognitive, mental, physical-health, and substance-use outcomes.

Family conflict and school disengagement appeared as recurrent risk correlates across multiple outcomes.

The review supports multi-level developmental ecology, not a ranking of universal causal importance.

## 32. Multi-context trajectories

A 2026 study of:
- N=8,168 adolescents;
- three waves

modeled joint family, school, and community connection trajectories.

Different joint trajectories related to:
- well-being;
- peer support;
- participation.

Latent classes summarize patterns.

They are not natural developmental types.

## 33. Latent trajectory class ≠ developmental essence

Class solutions depend on:
- number of waves;
- indicators;
- model specification;
- entropy;
- sample.

Use trajectory classes descriptively/predictively.

Do not reify them into fixed child types.

## 34. Developmental cascades

A developmental cascade occurs when change in one domain contributes to later change in another domain, potentially propagating over time.

Example scaffold:
\`motor skill -> exploration -> social interaction -> language opportunity\`.

Each arrow requires evidence.

## 35. Cascade ≠ sequence of correlations

To claim a cascade:
- temporal ordering must be plausible;
- alternative pathways must be modeled;
- mediating links need support;
- confounding must be addressed.

A three-wave regression chain is not automatically causal.

## 36. 2025 cascade review

The 2025 review emphasizes:
- cross-domain development;
- multiple timescales;
- whole-child integration;
- causal evidence as a high bar.

It recommends combining:
- controlled experiments;
- naturalistic observation;
- dense process measurement.

## 37. Cascades can operate across timescales

A moment-to-moment process can accumulate into a long-term pathway.

Example:
- repeated successful exploration moments;
- more learning opportunities;
- later skill differences.

But a long-term association cannot reveal the microprocess without intermediate measurement.

## 38. Timescale mismatch can create false cascade stories

If:
- mediator operates daily;
- exposure/outcome measured annually

then the proposed process may be undersampled.

Developmental mechanism requires timing resolution compatible with theory.

## 39. Cascade direction can be reciprocal

Developmental systems often contain feedback:
\`A -> B -> A -> C\`.

Forcing a strictly linear one-direction cascade may misrepresent the process.

## 40. Cascade intervention logic

If A causally influences B and B influences C:
- intervening on A could affect C through B.

But intervention on A that changes C does not automatically prove B mediated the effect.

Mechanism requires mediation evidence.

## 41. Cumulative risk

Cumulative-risk approaches aggregate:
- number of adversities;
- risk indicators;
- chronic stressors.

They test whether greater total exposure predicts poorer outcomes.

They are useful for:
- parsimonious risk burden;
- dose-like prediction.

## 42. ACE/risk count loses exposure structure

A simple count often ignores:
- type;
- severity;
- chronicity;
- developmental timing;
- dependence between exposures.

Two people with count=3 may have very different developmental exposures.

Count ≠ mechanistic dose.

## 43. Dimensional adversity approaches

Dimensional models distinguish features such as:
- threat;
- deprivation;
- unpredictability;
- caregiver disruption.

They can generate mechanism-specific hypotheses.

But dimensional taxonomies are also models and require validation.

## 44. Cumulative and dimensional approaches are complementary

Cumulative count asks:
**how much broad risk burden?**

Dimensional approach asks:
**which features/processes of adversity?**

One does not automatically invalidate the other.

## 45. Timing, duration, recurrence, and type compete

A developmental outcome can relate to:
- age at first exposure;
- duration;
- recurrence;
- total number of exposures;
- specific exposure type.

These variables can be highly correlated.

Sensitive-period claims require separating them.

## 46. 2024 structured life-course adversity study

A longitudinal study:
- N=4,210;
- adversity measured at ages 1, 3, 5, 9

compared:
- cumulative type;
- duration/timing;
- specificity hypotheses.

Exposure to more adversity types for longer periods best predicted adolescent psychopathology overall.

Some exposure-specific/timing-specific effects also appeared.

## 47. Cumulative evidence does not imply simple additivity

A cumulative association can arise because:
- risks cluster;
- duration increases;
- severe contexts produce multiple indicators.

Risk count can be a useful predictor while remaining mechanistically ambiguous.

## 48. Early exposure ≠ necessarily strongest exposure

In the N=4,210 structured life-course study:
- adversity at age 9 was often a stronger predictor than earlier exposure;
- neglect at age 3 showed a specific association with later internalizing symptoms.

This directly blocks:
**earlier adversity is always more consequential**.

## 49. Cumulative risk and sensitive period are competing hypotheses

A strong developmental design should compare:
- total burden;
- recency;
- duration;
- sensitive timing;
- specific exposure.

Selecting one story after significance testing invites overinterpretation.

## 50. Differential susceptibility

Differential susceptibility proposes that some individuals may show greater responsiveness to:
- adverse environments;
and
- supportive environments.

The predicted pattern is "for better and for worse."

## 51. Diathesis–stress

Diathesis–stress predicts greater vulnerability primarily under:
- adverse conditions.

It does not require disproportionate benefit in supportive environments.

## 52. Vantage sensitivity

Vantage sensitivity predicts greater benefit from:
- positive/supportive environments

without necessarily greater harm from adverse environments.

These models are distinct.

## 53. Environmental sensitivity ≠ one model

A "sensitive" person could fit:
- diathesis–stress;
- vantage sensitivity;
- differential susceptibility;
- no interaction/additive effects.

Need to test the shape of the interaction.

## 54. Interaction significance is insufficient

A significant Person × Environment interaction does not show differential susceptibility by itself.

Need:
- environment spanning adverse to supportive range;
- predicted crossover/regions of significance;
- appropriate scaling;
- robustness;
- replication.

## 55. Susceptibility should not be inferred from median split

Dichotomizing:
- temperament;
- sensitivity scores;
- environment

can create unstable interaction patterns.

Prefer continuous models.

## 56. 2025 four-cohort IPD test

A pooled individual-participant study used:
- >30,000 children;
- four prospective cohorts;
- 16 infant-characteristic × parenting × outcome combinations;
- competitive-confirmatory model comparison.

Results:
- additive/no-interaction models were most common;
- diathesis-stress appeared in a minority;
- vantage sensitivity appeared in a minority;
- differential susceptibility was absent in primary pooled analyses.

## 57. Differential susceptibility is not a default developmental law

The >30,000-child analysis shows:
**developmental moderation hypotheses must compete against simpler additive models**.

A theory can be plausible without being the best model for a given outcome.

## 58. Interaction replication is difficult

Person × Environment interactions are vulnerable to:
- low power;
- measurement error;
- range restriction;
- scale dependence;
- multiple testing;
- sample-specific context.

Replication across cohorts/outcomes is especially important.

## 59. Sensitivity marker ≠ fixed child type

Possible sensitivity indicators include:
- temperament;
- physiological reactivity;
- sensory processing sensitivity;
- genetic/polygenic measures.

No marker should be treated as:
- universal;
- deterministic;
- context-free type.

## 60. Susceptibility can be outcome-specific

A child could be more environmentally responsive for:
- internalizing symptoms

but not:
- cognition.

Sensitivity should not be generalized across developmental domains without evidence.

## 61. Susceptibility can be environment-specific

Responsivity to:
- parenting

does not establish responsivity to:
- peers;
- school;
- socioeconomic adversity.

Environment specificity matters.

## 62. Protective factor

A protective factor is associated with:
- better outcome under risk/adversity.

Examples can include:
- caregiver support;
- school belonging;
- supportive peer relationship.

A protective factor is not automatically the causal mechanism.

## 63. Promotive versus protective

A **promotive** factor predicts better outcome broadly.

A **protective** factor specifically reduces the impact of risk/adversity (interaction/buffering logic).

These should be distinguished.

## 64. Buffering interaction requires adequate scale and range

An apparent buffering effect can depend on:
- outcome scale;
- nonlinear floor/ceiling;
- range of risk.

Interaction interpretation requires visualization and robust modeling.

## 65. Equifinality

Equifinality:
**different developmental pathways can lead to a similar outcome**.

Example:
similar externalizing outcome may arise through different combinations of:
- temperament;
- peer environment;
- family conflict;
- school disengagement.

Same outcome ≠ same mechanism.

## 66. Multifinality

Multifinality:
**similar early exposure can lead to different later outcomes**.

One adversity can be followed by:
- no persistent impairment;
- internalizing;
- externalizing;
- academic difficulty;
- multiple outcomes.

Early risk is probabilistic.

## 67. Equifinality/multifinality are inference constraints

These principles prevent:
- backward diagnosis from one adult outcome;
- deterministic forecasting from one childhood exposure.

They do not themselves specify mechanisms.

## 68. Risk factor ≠ trajectory

A risk factor changes probability.

It does not specify:
- onset;
- timing;
- persistence;
- individual path.

Trajectory requires repeated outcome measurement.

## 69. Risk accumulation can be non-linear

Possible patterns:
- linear addition;
- threshold;
- saturation;
- synergy;
- diminishing returns.

A summed score assumes a functional form that should be tested.

## 70. Protective contexts can compensate without "erasing" risk

A supportive context can:
- lower probability;
- change opportunity;
- alter interpretation;
- improve resources.

It does not imply prior exposure had no effect.

Developmental compensation and causal erasure are different concepts.

## 71. Context effects can be proximal or distal

Proximal:
- caregiver interaction;
- peer feedback;
- classroom.

Distal:
- neighborhood;
- policy;
- economic conditions;
- culture.

Distal effects often operate through intermediate processes.

But multilevel mediation must be tested, not assumed.

## 72. Ecological level is not causal priority

A factor at a "higher" ecological level is not automatically:
- stronger;
- more fundamental.

Levels describe organization.

Causal importance is empirical.

## 73. Shared environment can create correlated developmental paths

Children in one:
- family;
- classroom;
- school;
- neighborhood

share exposures.

Ignoring clustering can:
- understate uncertainty;
- conflate individual and context effects.

Multilevel models may be needed.

## 74. Contextual effect requires contextual variation

To estimate school-level effects, researchers need:
- multiple schools;
- sufficient school-level variability;
- appropriate clustering.

Individual perceived school belonging is not identical to school-level climate.

## 75. Compositional effect versus contextual effect

A school can appear high-risk because it contains more high-risk students.

A contextual effect asks:
**does the school environment predict outcome beyond individual composition?**

Do not conflate.

## 76. Family socioeconomic conditions are bundled exposures

Socioeconomic disadvantage can involve:
- material scarcity;
- housing instability;
- neighborhood exposure;
- parental stress;
- school resources.

A single SES index does not identify mechanism.

## 77. Context can be selected

Families select:
- neighborhoods;
- schools

within constraints.

Adolescents select:
- peers;
- activities.

Context–outcome association can therefore contain selection.

## 78. Constraint can limit selection

Selection is not complete freedom.

Choices are constrained by:
- income;
- geography;
- policy;
- discrimination;
- opportunity.

Developmental models should represent both agency and structural constraint.

## 79. Genetic confounding can matter

Parent/child traits can influence:
- parenting behavior;
- child outcomes.

Shared genetics can contribute to parent–child association.

Behavioral-genetic designs can help.

They do not make environmental processes irrelevant.

## 80. Gene–environment correlation

Broad forms:
- passive;
- evocative;
- active.

These describe ways genetic differences can correlate with environments.

They do not establish:
- genetic determinism;
- immutability.

## 81. Gene × Environment interaction requires the same skepticism as other interactions

G×E findings are vulnerable to:
- multiple testing;
- population stratification;
- measurement error;
- replication failure.

A candidate genotype should not be treated as a "plasticity gene."

## 82. Polygenic scores are population- and ancestry-sensitive

PGS can predict some developmental traits/environments.

Limitations:
- portability;
- ancestry;
- indirect genetic effects;
- socioeconomic confounding.

PGS ≠ biological destiny.

Detailed genetics belongs downstream/NEU where mechanistically relevant.

## 83. Randomized interventions strengthen environmental causality

If parenting support is randomized and child outcomes change, this strengthens:
**modifiable environmental influence**.

It still may not identify:
- exact mediator;
- developmental cascade;
- susceptibility subgroup.

## 84. Intervention moderation requires stronger evidence than subgroup significance

To claim "sensitive children benefit more":
- interaction should be preregistered;
- power adequate;
- subgroup pattern replicated;
- baseline scaling appropriate.

Post hoc responder groups are weak evidence.

## 85. Natural experiments

Policy/school/calendar changes can create quasi-random exposure differences.

Useful designs:
- regression discontinuity;
- difference-in-differences;
- instrumental variables.

Each depends on strong identifying assumptions.

## 86. Fixed effects

Within-person/family fixed-effects models can remove stable unobserved confounds.

They do not remove:
- time-varying confounding;
- reverse causation;
- measurement error.

Fixed effects ≠ randomized experiment.

## 87. Controlling baseline is useful but not sufficient

Baseline adjustment can:
- reduce pre-existing differences.

It can also introduce bias when:
- baseline is measured with error;
- exposure affects baseline;
- conditioning creates collider structures.

Causal diagrams help define adjustment sets.

## 88. Cross-lagged models need interpretation discipline

Traditional CLPM mixes:
- stable between-person differences;
- within-person deviations.

RI-CLPM and related models can separate some components.

No cross-lagged model automatically identifies causality.

## 89. Within-person temporal precedence is stronger but incomplete evidence

If:
- parenting is worse than usual;
- child behavior later worsens relative to usual

this narrows some confounding.

Still possible:
- unmeasured time-varying family stress;
- reverse/faster dynamics;
- common causes.

## 90. Mediation needs temporal ordering

For:
\`context -> mediator -> developmental outcome\`

need:
- context measured before mediator change;
- mediator before outcome change;
- adequate interval;
- confounder handling.

Same-wave mediation is weak developmental evidence.

## 91. Cumulative mediation can span multiple timescales

A distal context may alter:
- daily interactions;
which alter:
- skill practice;
which alters:
- long-run outcome.

Testing this requires nested timescale data.

Single-timescale models can miss the mechanism.

## 93. Failure modes

A. Parenting = one-way cause: **invalid simplification**.
B. Child behavior = passive outcome: **invalid**.

## 94. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- development is transactional rather than purely parent/environment driven;
- child characteristics can shape later parenting/environmental responses;
- peer similarity requires separation of selection and socialization;
- family, peer, school, and community contexts are noninterchangeable levels;
- developmental cascades require evidence for each temporally ordered link;
- cumulative risk, timing, duration, recurrence, and adversity type are distinct exposure hypotheses;
- sensitive-period claims should compete with cumulative/recency models;
- diathesis–stress, vantage sensitivity, and differential susceptibility are distinct;
- additive models often fit developmental data better than interaction models;
- equifinality and multifinality block deterministic developmental inference;
- longitudinal/cross-lagged/within-person associations do not automatically establish causality.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- caregiver sensitivity predicts multiple developmental outcomes on average;
- parent-driven and child-driven effects can coexist;
- naturalistic longitudinal peer influence is small but reproducible on average;
- experimental peer cues can produce moderate immediate behavior changes;
- school belonging and externalizing behavior show bidirectional longitudinal associations;
- cumulative breadth/duration of adversity can outperform simple sensitive-period models in some cohorts;
- some child/environment interactions fit diathesis–stress or vantage patterns;
- multilevel social contexts jointly relate to adolescent trajectories.

### Not established as universal
- one dominant family mechanism;
- one peer-influence magnitude;
- one school protective mechanism;
- one causal developmental cascade;
- one adversity dimension/timing rule;
- one "highly susceptible child" class;
- one differential-susceptibility marker;
- one early exposure that determines later outcome;
- one trajectory class that describes a natural developmental kind.

## Canonical operating rule

**Define the developing-person process and environmental process -> measure both repeatedly on a timescale compatible with their reciprocal dynamics -> separate exposure from selection/evocation -> distinguish family, peer, school, and broader context levels -> compare additive, cumulative, timing-specific, cascade, and susceptibility hypotheses -> treat equifinality/multifinality as default uncertainty constraints -> use causal designs/models appropriate to the link being claimed -> state only the narrowest developmental pathway supported.**

Never:
**parent = cause; child = passive outcome; peer similarity = influence; school association = context cause; cascade = causality; risk count = mechanism; early = strongest; interaction = susceptibility; susceptibility = child type; longitudinal = causal; early risk = destiny.**
