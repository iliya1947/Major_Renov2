## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a neuroplasticity framework useful for interpreting motor learning and resistance training without:

- equating any performance improvement with neural plasticity;
- equating a TMS/fMRI/MRI marker with learning itself;
- assuming use-dependent plasticity means durable skill;
- treating specificity as “constant practice is always best”;
- treating repetition as a universal dose-response rule with a fixed threshold;
- treating “salience matters” as a validated universal motivational prescription;
- equating consolidation with sleep-only effects;
- treating offline gain as required evidence of consolidation;
- treating retention as permanent storage;
- treating interference as universally harmful;
- treating relearning/savings as the same thing as overt retention;
- importing clinical neurorehabilitation principles or doses directly into healthy fitness.

### Primary downstream context

Healthy adult novice/intermediate resistance trainees learning and retaining exercise technique while also developing strength.

### Critical outcomes

- delayed retention;
- transfer/generalization;
- rate of relearning;
- task-specific performance;
- neural adaptation when directly measured;
- independence from temporary acquisition effects.

## 2. What “neuroplasticity” means in this project

Neuroplasticity is used as an umbrella term for experience-related changes in nervous-system structure/function across multiple levels, including:

- synaptic efficacy;
- intracortical inhibition/excitation;
- corticospinal/descending pathway excitability;
- network recruitment/connectivity;
- representational organization;
- structural MRI measures;
- spinal/motoneuronal adaptations.

Project boundary:

**neuroplasticity is not one biomarker and is not synonymous with motor learning.**

A behavioral skill can improve without a detectable change in the selected neural marker, and a neural marker can change without proving durable skill learning.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 3. Experience-dependent plasticity

The classic rehabilitation synthesis by Kleim & Jones (PMID **18230848**) framed experience-dependent plasticity as the neural basis of learning in the intact brain and relearning after brain damage, organizing principles such as:

- use it or lose it;
- use it and improve it;
- specificity;
- repetition matters;
- intensity matters;
- time matters;
- salience matters;
- age matters;
- transference/generalization;
- interference.

Important scope boundary:

This 2008 paper is explicitly a review of neuroscience principles **for rehabilitation after brain damage**. It is foundational conceptual framing, not a validated dosing manual for healthy lifting technique.

A 2026 systematic review of rapid structural plasticity (PMID **42105826**) found measurable short-interval structural MRI changes (<2 h) across motor/spatial/perceptual/language/stress/exercise/pharmacological manipulations.

However:

- MRI signal change does not identify one cellular mechanism;
- rapid structural marker change is not proof of durable skill;
- interventions and imaging methods were heterogeneous.

Canonical rule:

**experience can induce measurable neural change, but the behavioral meaning of a neural marker must be established rather than assumed.**

Confidence: **HIGH for experience-dependent neural change / MODERATE for interpreting any single marker**.

## 4. Use-dependent changes

Use-dependent plasticity refers to changes biased by repeated use of a movement/action pattern, even without requiring explicit task-error minimization or reward.

Classic human evidence:

### Classen et al., 1998 — PMID 9463469

- repeated thumb movements in a trained direction;
- after minutes of practice, TMS-evoked thumb movement direction shifted toward the practiced direction;
- effect persisted for minutes and then decayed.

### Bütefisch et al., 2000 — PMID 10716702

- use-dependent motor-cortex plasticity was reduced by NMDA-receptor blockade and by increased GABA-A modulation;
- this supported a synaptic-plasticity contribution in that paradigm.

Project boundary:

**use-dependent bias ≠ complete motor learning architecture.**

Repeated use can:

- stabilize a useful pattern;
- bias action selection/representation;
- also reinforce a poor or overly narrow solution.

Confidence: **HIGH for existence / HIGH against equivalence to all motor learning**.

## 5. Behavioral learning can dissociate from neurophysiological markers

This is closure-critical.

2026 lower-limb study PMID **42089992**:

- n = 14 healthy participants;
- two bouts of knee-extensor visuomotor force-tracking separated by 60 min;
- force steadiness improved;
- MEP amplitude, SICI and spinal excitability did not significantly change;
- only a delayed silent-period change was found.

Therefore:

`behavioral improvement`
≠
`detectable corticospinal-excitability change`.

The inverse inference is also unsafe:

`neural marker change`
≠
`durable skill learning`.

Canonical rule:

**behavioral learning and candidate neuroplastic markers are related but non-equivalent outcomes.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 6. Resistance training itself produces neural adaptations

Direct healthy-adult RT evidence prevents an overly narrow “neuroplasticity = skill practice only” model.

2026 systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **42460308**:

- 11 resistance-training studies;
- 244 healthy adults;
- pooled corticospinal-excitability increase:
  - SMD ≈ 0.59;
  - 95% CI ≈ 0.10–1.08;
  - I² ≈ 69%;
- pooled motor-performance increase:
  - SMD ≈ 0.40;
  - 95% CI ≈ 0.02–0.79;
  - I² ≈ 65%.

Interpretation requires caution because:

- TMS measures and protocols differed;
- RT tasks/doses differed;
- heterogeneity was substantial;
- corticospinal excitability is not the whole neural adaptation.

2026 RCT PMID **41845576** adds pathway-specific evidence:

- n = 30;
- 3 weeks of metronome-paced vs self-paced dumbbell RT vs control;
- both RT conditions increased strength;
- self-paced training produced larger StartReact/ICAR changes and RFD changes;
- metronome-paced training produced larger corticospinal-excitability and SICI changes.

Canonical rule:

**resistance training can induce central neural adaptation, and the pattern may be training-specific; no single neural marker explains strength gain.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 7. Specificity

Plasticity and learning are often specific to:

- practiced task;
- sensory context;
- force/velocity/tempo;
- movement direction;
- effector;
- feedback structure;
- neural pathway demands.

Direct current RT example:

PMID **41845576** showed different pacing constraints produced distinct descending-pathway adaptations despite both groups improving strength.

Long-term motor-memory study PMID **43003929**:

- thousands of adaptation trials across weeks;
- retention and spontaneous recovery increased with extended training;
- angular generalization **narrowed** with prolonged training.

This is an important specificity/generalization trade-off:

**more training can strengthen the trained memory while tuning it more narrowly to the trained task.**

However specificity does not mean:

`constant practice > variable practice`

universally.

Registered report PMID **41351896**:

- n = 80;
- constant vs variable bimanual shuffleboard practice;
- no significant 24-h retention difference;
- Bayesian analysis supported the null for the preregistered comparison.

Canonical rule:

**plasticity is constrained by what is practiced, but the optimal balance between specificity and variability depends on the desired retention/transfer target.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 8. Repetition

Repetition provides repeated exposure for:

- refining task estimates;
- reinforcing successful solutions;
- use-dependent bias;
- consolidating task representations.

But:

`more repetitions`
≠
`more useful plasticity`
without limit.

Evidence against a universal threshold/dose:

### Classic upper-limb use-dependent plasticity

PMID 9463469 found TMS-representation shifts after as little as 5–30 min in some participants.

### 2026 lower-limb study

PMID 42089992 found behavioral improvement after repeated training but no significant change in most corticospinal/spinal markers.

### 2026 structural-learning study PMID 41795357

- blocked repetition of perturbations facilitated early subsequent adaptation;
- strongest effect occurred in the eight-repetition condition;
- exploratory 24-h retention subgroup showed no lasting effect.

Therefore:

**repetition matters, but there is no universal repetition count or neural-plasticity threshold transferable across tasks, muscles, markers and learners.**

Confidence: **HIGH against universal dose threshold**.

---

## 9. Repetition quality matters

Repeated execution can reinforce:

- successful task solutions;
- task-irrelevant habits;
- inaccurate task representations;
- cue-dependent patterns.

Upstream NEU-18 remains binding.

Therefore:

**repetition is not automatically beneficial merely because a movement is repeated.**

A useful repetition should preserve sufficient:

- task relevance;
- information;
- acceptable error;
- state quality.

Exact practice volume/frequency belongs NEU-13 / PRO.

Status: **PRACTICAL_SYNTHESIS**.

## 10. Salience

“Salience matters” is one of the classic Kleim/Jones rehabilitation principles.

However “salience” is not one standardized scalar in healthy motor-learning research.

Potential operationalizations include:

- reward;
- task relevance;
- meaningful goal;
- attention capture;
- motivational value.

### Reward evidence

PMID **34345810**:

- 90 healthy participants;
- reward improved motor skill learning beyond performance reinforcement alone;
- benefit persisted on the next day and selectively potentiated task-relevant reinforcement adjustments.

PMID **40139803**:

- 65 healthy participants;
- monetary reward accelerated pinch-grip skill learning;
- reward altered early corticospinal-output variability;
- most other TMS measures did not differ between groups.

PMID **41730946** provides an important counterweight:

- forcing ~80% success produced less reward-based motor learning than ~50% success in a brief drawing task;
- maximizing positive outcomes/motivation did not maximize learning.

Canonical rule:

**motivational/reward salience can modulate motor learning, but “make it more salient” is not a universal dose rule and maximum success/reward is not necessarily maximum learning.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 11. Consolidation

For this project, consolidation refers to post-acquisition processes through which a newly formed motor memory becomes more stable and/or changes in expression over time.

It can involve:

- offline stabilization;
- offline performance change;
- resistance to interference;
- systems/network reorganization.

Consolidation is **not defined by improvement during sleep**.

2025 ALE meta-analysis PMID **40800901**:

- 38 motor-task imaging studies;
- identified overlapping and distinct activation patterns for online vs offline motor learning;
- supports biologically distinguishable but overlapping online/offline processes.

2026 fMRI study PMID **42990842** further suggests distributed/hippocampal-putaminal-sensorimotor reactivation-related processes during wakeful rest in multi-sequence learning.

But consolidation is not guaranteed to produce an observable “offline gain.”

2025 study PMID **41145718** found that replacing brief inter-trial rest with engaging tasks did not significantly alter micro-offline gains, challenging the claim that such gains necessarily represent rest-dependent consolidation.

Canonical rule:

**consolidation is a memory-stabilization process, not synonymous with any one offline performance phenomenon.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 12. Sleep and consolidation

Sleep can affect memory and performance, but motor consolidation is task/component dependent.

2026 study PMID **42648788**:

- 48 healthy adults;
- one night total sleep deprivation vs normal sleep before motor sequence acquisition;
- sleep deprivation reduced movement-time gains during acquisition;
- spatial accuracy was spared;
- delayed 24-h and 7-day consolidation remained preserved;
- BDNF and corticospinal-excitability measures did not explain the behavioral difference.

Therefore:

**sleep/recovery matter, but “one poor night destroys motor consolidation” is not a valid universal rule.**

Exact sleep prescription belongs REC.

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH against simplistic sleep-only consolidation claims**.

## 13. Retention

Retention is delayed expression of previously acquired skill after a period without practice or with reduced exposure.

Retention can decay.

2025 meta-analysis PMID **40455501**:

- 1,344 effect sizes;
- 457 reports;
- skill decay increased with longer periods of nonuse;
- approximate loss of half the initial acquisition gain:
  - ~6.5 months for accuracy measures;
  - ~13 months for speed measures;
  - ~11 months for mixed measures;
- decay was moderated by task type, complexity, intermittent performance opportunities and instructions.

These are broad procedural-skill averages, not lift-specific expiry dates.

Canonical rule:

**retention is graded and task dependent; nonuse can produce decay, but there is no universal “skill expires after X days” threshold.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 14. Retention ≠ unchanged performance

Retention does not require perfectly unchanged performance.

Observed retention can be affected by:

- warm-up;
- context;
- fatigue;
- test familiarity;
- speed–accuracy trade-off;
- relearning during the test itself.

The 2025 procedural-retention meta-analysis explicitly emphasizes separating retention performance from relearning effects.

Project rule:

**retention testing should minimize contamination by substantial new practice/relearning.**

Exact measurement design belongs TST.

## 15. Interference

Interference means that learning/practicing one task changes acquisition, consolidation or expression of another memory.

It can be:

- retrograde — later learning disrupts earlier memory;
- anterograde — earlier learning impairs later learning;
- online/resource competition;
- context-/similarity-dependent.

Interference is **not universally present** whenever two skills are practiced close together.

2025/2026 ballistic study PMID **41270933**:

- 45 participants;
- learned an isometric ballistic wrist-flexion task;
- subsequent isometric accuracy task impaired later ballistic retention;
- dynamic accuracy task using the same muscles/direction did not;
- interference therefore depended on similarity including contraction mode.

2026 study PMID **41263401** found online declarative-memory interference affecting fast/slow processes of force-field adaptation under specific concurrent conditions.

Canonical rule:

**interference depends on overlap in task representation, resources and context; “practice B after A always harms A” is false.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 16. Interference can coexist with beneficial variability/contextual interference

Terminology trap:

**memory interference** is not the same construct as **contextual interference** from random/interleaved practice.

Contextual-interference literature can show poorer acquisition but better delayed retention/transfer.

Therefore:

- “interference” can describe detrimental memory competition;
- “contextual interference” describes a practice-schedule effect that can improve learning in some tasks.

Do not infer one from the other.

Confidence: **HIGH taxonomy**.

---

## 17. Relearning / savings

Relearning refers to learning again after prior exposure.

Savings means reacquisition is faster/better than initial acquisition.

It can reveal a latent memory even when overt performance appears returned toward baseline.

2026 fMRI study PMID **41805913**:

- two-day sensorimotor adaptation;
- savings was associated with reinstatement of cortical manifold structure formed during initial learning;
- effect was linked particularly to default-mode-network dynamics.

2026 review PMID **42139814** emphasizes that motor memories can coexist, be contextually selected and updated rather than simply overwritten.

2026 long-term study PMID **43003929** found extended training increased retention and spontaneous recovery while tuning generalization.

Canonical rule:

**savings/relearning is evidence of prior learning history, but it is not equivalent to overt retention strength or broad transfer.**

Confidence: **HIGH for behavioral distinction / MODERATE for exact neural mechanism**.

---

## 18. Relearning can cross information modalities

2026 study PMID **41968588**:

- visual feedback supported initial reaching adaptation;
- auditory feedback alone initially failed;
- after visual learning, subsequent auditory adaptation became possible;
- authors interpreted this as cross-modal transfer from retained learned direction.

This reinforces:

**prior learning can change the learning landscape for later practice, even when the surface feedback context changes.**

It does not imply unrestricted far transfer.

Confidence: **MODERATE**.

## 19. Neuroplasticity markers are not direct coaching targets

Examples:

- MEP amplitude;
- SICI;
- silent period;
- fMRI activation;
- cortical thickness;
- diffusion MRI;
- StartReact;
- ICAR;
- BDNF.

These can be useful scientific measurements.

But a coach should not assume:

`larger neural change = better learning`.

The 2026 lower-limb study demonstrates behavioral learning without most expected marker changes.

The 2026 RT tempo study shows different marker profiles can accompany similar strength improvement.

Canonical rule:

**train/test the behavioral outcome first; use neural markers to investigate mechanism when valid, not as automatic optimization targets.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 20. Limits of transferring clinical neurorehabilitation principles to healthy fitness

This item is closure-critical.

The classic Kleim/Jones principles were explicitly formulated to guide rehabilitation after **brain damage**.

Clinical neurorehabilitation differs from healthy fitness in:

- damaged vs intact nervous systems;
- spontaneous recovery/remapping after injury;
- abnormal inhibition/excitability;
- impairment-driven goals;
- compensatory vs restorative mechanisms;
- therapy dose constraints;
- patient fatigue/comorbidity;
- functional independence endpoints.

Current rehabilitation literature also highlights transfer problems.

2026 review PMID **42996797** emphasizes that improvement in a trained digital/rehabilitation task does not guarantee real-world functional transfer and that transfer must be deliberately tested.

2026 SCI task-specific-training scoping review PMID **43020864** found inconsistent reporting of key training principles, limiting causal inference about which “neuroplasticity principles” actually drive outcomes.

Therefore:

**clinical neuroplasticity principles are useful conceptual constraints, not direct healthy-fitness prescriptions.**

Do not import:

- repetition counts;
- intensity thresholds;
- salience rules;
- therapy schedules;
- neurostimulation mechanisms

as validated gym prescriptions without healthy/task-specific evidence.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 21. Clinical “principles of plasticity” are not ten independent laws

The Kleim/Jones list is a conceptual synthesis.

The principles interact.

Examples:

- repetition without task specificity may reinforce the wrong solution;
- specificity can improve trained performance while narrowing transfer;
- salience/reward can accelerate learning but excessive success can reduce information;
- intensity/repetition can interact with fatigue/homeostatic limits;
- interference depends on similarity and timing.

Canonical rule:

**treat the classic principles as interacting design considerations, not independent monotonic laws.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 22. Practical strength-skill synthesis

For later resistance-exercise coaching:

1. repeated task exposure is necessary but not a fixed-dose prescription;
2. make practice specific to the performance/transfer target;
3. do not over-specialize when broader transfer is required;
4. use reward/goal relevance when it improves engagement/information, not merely to maximize success percentage;
5. protect retention by testing after delays, not only immediately;
6. expect some interference when closely overlapping memories/tasks compete, but test rather than assume it;
7. exploit relearning/savings after breaks rather than treating temporary decay as total loss;
8. distinguish neural adaptation evidence from behavioral skill evidence;
9. do not use clinical rehab “plasticity principles” as numerical healthy-fitness rules.

Status:

**PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not a universal neuroplasticity algorithm.

## 24. Measurement boundaries

### Neuroplasticity
Experience-related nervous-system change at a defined level/measure.

### Motor learning
Relatively durable capability change inferred from retention/transfer/relearning.

### Use-dependent change
Bias/change produced through repeated use of a movement/action pattern.

### Consolidation
Post-acquisition stabilization/reorganization of memory.

### Retention
Delayed expression of prior learning.

### Interference
Competition/change between memories/tasks.

### Savings
Faster/better relearning after previous exposure.

### Generalization
Expression of learned change across nearby stimuli/contexts.

### Transfer
Performance benefit in a distinct untrained task/context.

### Neural marker
Measured proxy of one neural property, not plasticity “itself.”

## 25. Direct resistance-exercise boundary

Current direct evidence supports:

- chronic RT can alter corticospinal and other descending-pathway measures;
- neural adaptations can be protocol specific;
- strength improvement can coexist with different neurophysiological profiles.

Current evidence does **not** establish:

- one optimal “neuroplasticity” RT dose;
- one TMS marker that should be maximized;
- that larger corticospinal excitability gains cause larger strength gains;
- that a neural adaptation measured after RT equals technique learning;
- that clinical rehabilitation repetition/intensity thresholds apply to healthy lifters.

Exact RT application belongs NEU-22 / PRO / TST.

## 28. Evidence-ledger candidates

1. Experience-dependent neural plasticity is real but no single neural marker defines it.
2. Use-dependent plasticity can arise from repeated movement and is not equivalent to durable motor learning.
3. Behavioral improvement can occur without detectable change in common corticospinal markers.
4. Resistance training can modify central excitability, with substantial protocol/marker heterogeneity.
5. Training specificity can produce pathway-specific neural adaptation and may narrow generalization with extensive practice.
6. Repetition matters but no universal repetition/plasticity threshold exists.
7. Reward/salience can facilitate learning, but more reward/success is not monotonically better.
8. Consolidation is broader than sleep-dependent offline gain.
9. Procedural skill retention decays with nonuse but at task-dependent rates.
10. Motor interference depends on similarity/context and is not universally induced by close practice.
11. Savings/relearning can persist despite weak overt retention and has distinct neural correlates.
12. Clinical neurorehabilitation plasticity principles are conceptual constraints, not validated healthy-fitness dose prescriptions.
13. Neural markers should not be used as direct coaching optimization targets without behavioral validation.
