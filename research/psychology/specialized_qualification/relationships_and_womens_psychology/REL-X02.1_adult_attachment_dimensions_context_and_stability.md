E1. Fraley, Hudson, Heffernan & Segal (2015), Are adult attachment styles categorical or dimensional? https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000027 ; https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/are-adult-attachment-styles-categorical-or-dimensional-a-taxometr/ . Complete institutional author abstract directly inspected. Taxometric analyses in two samples favored continuous representations over discrete categories, both globally and for specific relationships. This supports dimensional operationalization, not a proof that all attachment measures, populations or theoretical mechanisms are continuous. Sample counts, model comparisons and uncertainty not independently checked.

E2. Fraley, Heffernan, Vicary & Brumbaugh (2011), Experiences in Close Relationships–Relationship Structures questionnaire. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022898 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21443364/ . Indexed original author abstract and directly opened author's instrument documentation https://labs.psychology.illinois.edu/~rcfraley/measures/relstructures.htm (overview, scoring and updated global/target instructions). ECR-RS has separate anxiety and avoidance measures for different relational targets. The documentation reports approximately .65 30-day retest reliability for romantic relationships (including breakups) and .80 for parental relationships; these are context-specific estimates, not immutable properties of a person or scale. The author subsequently added separately administered general ratings rather than only averaging four target-specific scores. A computed global mean mechanically overlaps target scores; independent global ratings permit examining their associations without this particular mathematical coupling. Different targets, versions and response anchors must be recorded. Original psychometric tables, reliability uncertainty and population norms not independently checked. No individual scoring from third-party reports.

E3. Roisman et al. (2007), The Adult Attachment Interview and self-reports of attachment style: an empirical rapprochement. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.4.678 ; https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/the-adult-attachment-interview-and-self-reports-of-attachment-sty/ . Institutional author abstract and indexed original abstract inspected. Meta-analytic Study 1 (N=961) reported mean r=.09 between interview-based security and self-reported attachment dimensions; Study 2 (N=160) also investigated overlap with updated measures. Weak convergence challenges treating these instruments as interchangeable indicators of the same individual attribute. Different targets, narrative versus self-report methods and construct content may contribute. Neither measure automatically reveals an unconscious true motive; no diagnostic superiority inferred. Full analysis and precision not inspected.

E4. Fraley, Vicary, Brumbaugh & Roisman (2011), Patterns of stability in adult attachment: an empirical test of two models of continuity and change. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21707199/ ; https://digitalcommons.iwu.edu/psych_scholarship/19/ . Institutional author abstract directly inspected. Two intensive longitudinal studies: 203 people assessed daily for 30 days and 388 weekly for one year. Patterns favored a prototype model (a persistent component alongside shorter-term deviations) over the alternative studied. Measured Big Five traits did not explain the observed stability. This is comparative model fit, not proof of an immutable childhood prototype or absence of durable individual change. Exact parameter estimates, model assumptions and attrition not independently verified.

E5. Fraley, Gillath & Deboeck (2021), Do life events lead to enduring changes in adult attachment styles? https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000326 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32790474/ . Indexed author abstract extracts; direct PubMed CAPTCHA. Multiwave naturalistic sample exceeding 4,000. Approximately half of examined event categories were associated with immediate changes; around one quarter with enduring mean changes relative to pre-event trajectories. These proportions refer to studied event categories, not probabilities for people. Many deviations diminished over time. Counterevidence to both fixed attachment and automatic permanent change after salient events. Selection, concurrent events and prior trends limit causal interpretation. Exact event estimates and heterogeneity not verified.

E6. Girme et al. (2018), The ebbs and flows of attachment: Within-person variation in attachment undermine secure individuals' relationship wellbeing across time. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000115 ; https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/the-ebbs-and-flows-of-attachment-within-person-variation-in-attac/ . Direct institutional author abstract. Study 1 N=409; longitudinal Study 2 N=324 individuals and Study 3 N=171 couples. Within-person security fluctuations occurred widely; larger fluctuations were associated with worsening relationship well-being, especially among participants with lower initial anxiety or avoidance. Thus relatively low baseline insecurity does not imply temporal invariance. Observational prediction, not proof that fluctuations caused later distress; feedback, common stressors, measurement error and scale bounds may contribute. Exact effect sizes, moderators and missingness not inspected.

E7. Testing the canalization hypothesis of attachment theory: Examining within-subject variation in attachment security (online 2023). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38059981/ . Indexed author abstract; direct open blocked. Longitudinal data from 1,741 adults, 3–24 surveys (mean 6.79, median retest interval 35 days). Newer relationships were associated with greater fluctuations in partner-specific anxiety, including responses to attachment-relevant events. Authors describe partial support for canalization. Do not extend the anxiety finding to every avoidance outcome or infer duration itself causes security. Selection of surviving relationships, age, experiences and sampling can confound duration comparisons. Exact avoidance findings, coefficients and precision not independently verified.

E8. Changes in global and relationship-specific attachment working models (2022). https://doi.org/10.1177/02654075211051408 . Indexed publisher methods and abstract extracts; full HTML open failed. Longitudinal data on 4,904 adults; growth models compare global and specific target ratings and distinguish longer-term trends from deviations. Reported covariation of models over time. This supports investigating interdependence without assuming either full independence or identity of representations; no direction of transmission identified. Exact target-specific estimates and precision not verified. Possible sample overlap with other large online longitudinal attachment studies (E5/E7) is unresolved; do not count them as independent replications.

E9. Hudson, Fraley, Brumbaugh & Vicary (2014), Coregulation in romantic partners' attachment styles: a longitudinal investigation. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167214528989 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24743602/ . Indexed author abstract and publisher description confirm repeated partner-specific self-reports in couples. Used here as evidence of an appropriate dyadic study design, not a verified effect or causal claim; detailed outcomes and estimates remain to be inspected in REL-X02.2.

## 3. Construct map and operational model
Attachment-related anxiety concerns insecurity about availability, care or abandonment. Avoidance concerns discomfort with reliance, disclosure or closeness. Low scores on both are often termed relatively secure, but directly measured momentary felt security is a distinct endpoint. Neither dimension is a psychiatric diagnosis, an exhaustive personality descriptor or a measure of romantic interest, ethical character, love or consent. Do not confuse attachment orientation with attachment-figure function (safe haven, secure base, proximity seeking), which requires separate examination.

Distinguish global self-reported orientation, domain/target-specific orientation, momentary state, observed action and response to a genuinely supportive, unavailable or unsafe context. A person can be generally comfortable with closeness yet reluctant in a particular relationship; an action such as seeking reassurance or declining contact can have multiple functions.

Illustrative decomposition of a measured dimension: y(i,r,t) = global_i + relationship_offset(i,r) + time_varying_state(i,r,t) + error(i,r,t). This is a conceptual accounting model, not a fitted causal model. Components may interact and evolve; global ratings need not equal the arithmetic mean of specific ratings. Separate average, within-person fluctuation, recovery, and longer-term change. Reliability, rank-order stability and stability of individual absolute scores are distinct. A single observed score or behavioral episode cannot identify the components.

## 4. Practical inference and revision
1. Identify the exact target and time frame, including whether the question concerns orientation, a momentary state, actual attachment function, or another relational construct.
2. Record evidence channel and actual context. Voluntary self-report, trained interview coding, observed behavior and another person's impressions are different measurements; do not score an absent individual from correspondence or proxy interpretation.
3. Compare at least two realistic alternatives for a behavior: ordinary preference or boundary, contextual overload, accurate appraisal of another's behavior, temporary state, relationship-specific expectations, enduring tendency. These may coexist. Actual relational safety and reciprocity matter independently of attachment.
4. Seek naturally occurring repeated observations in comparable contexts and voluntary descriptions of experience. If the pattern is specific to one relationship, do not globalize it. If conditions change, assess deviations and recovery rather than postulating a permanent change immediately. Consider reference shifts, time-varying confounding and measurement error.
5. State the hypothesis at the supported level and its revision condition. No inferred childhood cause from current behavior, rigid attachment-type assignment, unsupported numerical individual score or confidence in uncalibrated forecasts.
6. An explicit choice or boundary remains actionable regardless of hypothetical attachment dynamics. No interpretation of refusal as hidden affection, covert testing, engineered insecurity or pressure.
7. Do not treat every relationship change as a regulatory response. Distinguish preferences, opportunities, emotional states and mutual decisions under REL-X01 and IND-03. Broader dyadic regulatory evidence is a subsequent task.

## 5. Internal synthetic checks
Rubric set before answering: construct separation, competing explanation, distinguishing evidence, inference boundary. Critical failures: deterministic type labels, unsupported diagnosis or motive, causal inflation, ignoring disconfirmation, coercion or private-data transfer. Self-authored neutral examples, not held-out.

A. A participant reports ease relying on friends but discomfort relying on a particular colleague. Response: distinguish target-specific expectations and global tendencies; role norms, prior experience and ordinary preferences compete. Voluntary ratings across targets and repeated comparable contexts could discriminate. No global type or diagnosis.
B. An interview and romantic self-report yield apparently conflicting results. Response: target, narrative method and questionnaire constructs differ; method variance and genuine specificity are alternatives. Check validated scoring and scope rather than forcing agreement. No assertion that either reveals hidden truth.
C. A survey score shifts after relocation, then returns near its earlier trend. Response: transient deviation is different from enduring change; adaptation, measurement and concurrent circumstances compete. Repeated comparable assessments and pre-event trajectory clarify. No proof of a single cause.
D. A participant with low average anxiety shows substantial fluctuations. Response: average and variability differ; actual relationship instability, situational states and error may contribute. Examine repeated contextual data and measurement precision. No contradiction or fixed type.
E. A sample of newer couples shows more anxiety fluctuation. Response: duration association is not individual trajectory or causation; survivorship, age, context and assessment frequency matter. Longitudinal comparison and appropriate controls could weaken alternatives. No universal timeline.
F. Someone declines a close relationship but remains socially outgoing. Response: a freely expressed preference and generalized sociability are distinct from attachment avoidance; incompatibility, circumstances and independent priorities remain possible. Respect the choice; no hidden desire inferred.
