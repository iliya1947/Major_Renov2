# STR-04 — Stress, coping, and resilience integration/sufficiency audit

Status: **COMPLETE_FOR_CURRENT_SCOPE — 20/20 PASS**
**Domain decision: STR sequence COMPLETE_FOR_CURRENT_SCOPE (STR-01…04).**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public stress/coping/resilience science and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## 1. Purpose and inputs

This block integrates and audits:

1. **STR-01** — stressor, appraisal, acute/chronic stress, stress response, allostasis, coping, resilience, and broad measurement boundaries;
2. **STR-02** — HPA/ANS/immune physiology, cortisol/HRV/sAA/EDA, reactivity, recovery, habituation, biomarker and wearable validity;
3. **STR-03** — coping targets, controllability, flexibility, social support/resources, resilience definitions, trajectories, and psychometrics.

The goal is not to claim that stress science is complete.

The goal is to decide whether the current foundation is sufficient to prevent major false inferences in generic psychological reasoning.

## 2. Integrated causal scaffold

A practical analytic sequence is:

\`stressor/exposure -> appraisal + control/resources -> psychological/biological response -> coping actions -> short-term outcome -> recovery/adaptation -> cumulative consequences\`

This is a **reasoning scaffold**, not a fixed biological chain.

Feedback loops are expected:
- coping can change the stressor;
- response can change appraisal;
- support can change resources;
- stress history can alter future responses;
- repeated exposure can produce habituation or sensitization;
- adaptation can change later vulnerability.

No stage should be used as a synonym for another.

## 3. Core distinction: stressor is not stress response

A stressor is an exposure, demand, condition, or anticipated challenge.

Stress responses can include:
- subjective distress;
- vigilance;
- affective change;
- autonomic activation;
- HPA activation;
- inflammatory change;
- cognitive/behavioral changes.

The same stressor can produce different response profiles across people and times.

The same physiological profile can arise from non-stress causes.

Therefore:

**stressor exposure ≠ subjective stress ≠ physiological response ≠ downstream harm**.

## 4. Stress is not one latent intensity variable

STR-01 and STR-02 jointly reject a universal "stress amount" inferred from one channel.

Potential channels:
- perceived stress;
- negative affect;
- heart rate;
- blood pressure;
- HRV;
- cortisol;
- sAA/catecholamine proxies;
- EDA;
- inflammatory markers;
- behavior;
- recovery trajectory.

Cross-channel correlations can be weak because systems have different kinetics, sensitivity, and confounds.

Divergence can be scientifically meaningful.

## 5. Negative affect, arousal, and stress remain distinct

AFF showed that:
- negative valence is not a stress definition;
- arousal is not an emotion category.

STR adds:
- stressor appraisal can alter affect and arousal;
- stress can occur with modest negative affect;
- arousal can occur without stress.

Thus:

**negative emotion -> stress**
and
**high arousal -> stress**

are both invalid unique inferences.

## 6. Acute stress response is not pathology by definition

Acute stress responses can:
- mobilize energy;
- alter attention;
- increase vigilance;
- change action selection;
- influence memory.

Whether the response is adaptive depends on:
- context;
- magnitude;
- timing;
- recovery;
- stress history;
- task demands.

A strong acute response is not automatically harmful.

A weak response is not automatically resilient.

## 7. Acute, repeated acute, and chronic stress

These terms describe different temporal/exposure structures.

- **Acute:** time-limited challenge/episode.
- **Repeated acute:** multiple separated episodes.
- **Chronic:** prolonged/persistent/recurrent exposure or burden across longer time.

Severity and duration are different dimensions.

A severe short event is not "chronic" because it is intense.

A modest recurring demand can be chronic.

## 8. Chronic stress must specify what is chronic

STR-01 identified major measurement inconsistency.

"Chronic stress" can refer to:
- prolonged stressor exposure;
- sustained perceived stress;
- persistent biological dysregulation;
- downstream burnout or health burden.

These are not interchangeable.

Canonical rule:

**Name the chronic exposure/process/outcome rather than using chronic stress as a catch-all label.**

## 9. Appraisal is explanatory but not total

Appraisal variables include:
- threat/challenge;
- control;
- predictability;
- uncertainty;
- coping resources;
- relevance.

These can explain part of response variability.

But appraisal is not:
- the whole stress response;
- always conscious;
- the only determinant.

Physical state, learning history, environment, social conditions, and prior stress exposure also matter.

## 10. Challenge/threat is conditional

Challenge/threat models compare perceived demands and resources.

The 2025 updated meta-analysis found better performance on average under challenge than threat states, with small effects.

This supports a meaningful framework, not a universal binary.

A stressor may contain both challenge and threat features.

Challenge is not automatically healthy; threat is not automatically pathological.

## 11. Control must be decomposed

Control may mean:
- preventing exposure;
- stopping it;
- reducing severity;
- choosing timing;
- choosing behavior;
- changing outcome probability.

Perceived control and objective control can differ.

High perceived control can be inaccurate.
Low perceived control can be realistic.

Coping judgments must specify which kind of control exists.

## 12. Physiological systems have different clocks

STR-02 establishes:

- ANS/cardiovascular changes can occur rapidly;
- sAA/catecholamine-related measures can respond rapidly;
- cortisol commonly peaks later;
- inflammatory changes may emerge later still.

Therefore the same clock time can correspond to:
- autonomic recovery;
- ongoing HPA rise;
- delayed immune change.

"Post-stressor" is not one physiological phase.

## 13. Cortisol is not stress itself

Cortisol is influenced by:
- circadian phase;
- awakening;
- sleep;
- food/metabolic state;
- exercise;
- illness;
- medication;
- hormonal context;
- stressor timing.

CAR, diurnal slope/output, acute reactivity, and recovery are distinct constructs.

A single cortisol value cannot identify psychological stress intensity.

## 14. Low reactivity is not resilience

STR-02 incorporated 2026 meta-analytic evidence showing small blunted cardiac/hemodynamic stress responses in depression, without consistent overall blunting of HRV or neuroendocrine reactivity.

Thus:

**smaller physiological response -> better adaptation**

is false as a universal rule.

Blunting may reflect:
- habituation;
- chronic exposure;
- disengagement;
- high baseline;
- health/medication effects;
- psychopathology-associated physiology.

## 15. Habituation is not resilience

Habituation means reduced response to repeated exposure under specific conditions.

It differs from:
- within-episode recovery;
- long-term resilience;
- low baseline;
- adaptive coping.

STR-02's repeated-TSST synthesis showed stronger habituation in HPA than in ANS/immune responses.

A system can habituate while another does not.

## 16. Recovery is not simply return to the exact old baseline

Recovery can mean:
- decline after peak;
- return to a reference band;
- restoration of function.

Adaptation can include a new stable configuration.

Therefore:
**not returning exactly to prestressor baseline**
does not automatically imply failed adaptation.

The reference state must be justified.

## 17. Homeostasis and allostasis are compatible

Homeostasis:
- regulation of variables within viable ranges.

Allostasis:
- anticipatory/reactive change that maintains function under demand.

Allostasis does not mean "stress physiology" in general.

Stress mediators can be adaptive when:
- appropriately mobilized;
- proportionate;
- efficiently terminated.

## 18. Allostatic load is not an objective stress meter

Allostatic-load indices vary in:
- biomarker number;
- physiological systems;
- thresholds/scoring;
- medication handling;
- disease overlap.

Recent 2026 reviews show substantial heterogeneity and overlap with cardiometabolic multimorbidity.

Thus a high AL composite can indicate multisystem burden without proving:
- psychological stress cause;
- one exposure history;
- one mechanism.

## 19. Coping is broader than emotion regulation

AFF-02 and STR-03 overlap on:
- reappraisal;
- acceptance;
- avoidance;
- suppression.

But coping can target:
- the external stressor;
- resources;
- practical consequences;
- meaning;
- emotion.

The frameworks overlap but are not identical.

Taxonomy labels alone do not identify different mechanisms.

## 20. No coping family has a universal adaptive sign

Problem-focused coping can fail when action is impossible or dangerous.

Emotion-focused coping can be useful when:
- external control is low;
- immediate action is impossible;
- emotional regulation supports later decisions.

Avoidance can:
- protect from genuine danger;
- reduce overload;
- prevent escalation;

or:
- maintain fear;
- block corrective learning;
- postpone solvable problems.

**Function, context, timing, and outcome determine interpretation.**

## 21. Coping flexibility is not "more coping"

STR-03 distinguishes:
- repertoire size;
- switching;
- variability;
- strategy abandonment;
- strategy–situation fit.

The 2025 meta-analysis of 89 studies (N=102,304) found coping flexibility associated with adjustment at approximately r=.28 with significant heterogeneity.

This is evidence for association, not proof of a universal flexibility mechanism.

## 22. Psychological flexibility is not coping flexibility

A 2026 meta-analysis of psychological flexibility found moderate associations with lower depression, anxiety, stress/burnout and higher resilience, but:
- only 8 studies entered quantitative meta-analysis;
- heterogeneity for several outcomes was very high;
- the evidence was predominantly correlational.

Psychological flexibility is therefore not automatically equivalent to:
- coping repertoire;
- strategy–situation fit;
- emotion-regulation flexibility.

Different flexibility constructs require separate operationalization.

## 23. Strategy–situation fit is a functional target

A useful coping question is:

**Given the current controllability, resources, goals, and stressor, did the selected tactic improve the intended outcome?**

This is more informative than:
- number of strategies;
- switching frequency;
- trait flexibility score.

Even strategy fit remains outcome-specific and must be measured.

## 24. Coping frequency is not coping effectiveness

Frequent strategy use may reflect:
- effective preference;
- more severe stress exposure;
- repeated failure;
- lack of alternatives;
- habitual responding.

A coping study should control for prior distress and stressor severity.

Daily diary/EMA associations do not automatically identify causal strategy efficacy.

## 25. Social support is robustly associated with adjustment

The 2025 second-order synthesis included:
- 60 meta-analyses;
- >2,700 studies;
- >2.1 million participants.

Overall:
- support–adjustment association r≈.24;
- perceived support was generally stronger than received support;
- adjusted effects were smaller;
- effects varied substantially.

This makes support a strong resilience-relevant correlate/resource.

It does **not** identify one universal causal mechanism.

## 26. Perceived and received support are different

Perceived support:
- expectation/belief that responsive support is available.

Received support:
- assistance actually provided.

Received support can be:
- mismatched;
- unwanted;
- controlling;
- poorly timed.

More support is not always better.

Support type, source, recipient goal, and context matter.

## 27. Resources are multilevel

Relevant resources can exist at:
- individual;
- relational;
- family;
- community;
- institutional;
- material/economic levels.

This prevents moralizing coping as solely personal skill.

Poor adaptation can reflect inadequate or inaccessible resources rather than defective motivation or character.

## 28. Resilience requires adversity

This is the strongest invariant across current resilience methodology.

To infer resilience, specify:
1. adversity/stressor exposure;
2. system/person/population;
3. outcome;
4. time horizon.

Good mental health under low adversity is not evidence of resilience to high adversity.

## 29. Resilience trait, outcome, and process are different

The 2025 systematic review of:
- 193 longitudinal studies;
- 805,660 participants

found only 32% explicitly defined resilience:
- 6% trait;
- 19% outcome;
- 8% process.

Conceptual heterogeneity is therefore a central evidence limitation.

No universal "resilience score" follows.

## 30. Resilience scales often mix constructs

The 2026 review of 83 resilience scales found item content distributed across:
- process-related content;
- five-factor-model trait content;
- environmental factors.

Hence:

**resilience scale score ≠ observed resilient adaptation**.

A reliable scale can still measure a mixed construct.

## 31. New 2026 resilience scales do not solve the conceptual problem automatically

A 2026 Human Resilience Scale study used an 18-item three-factor model (flexibility, sociality, meaning) and tested moderation of stress–mental-health association.

This is a useful psychometric development.

It does not establish:
- those three factors are the unique architecture of resilience;
- scale score equals longitudinal resilient outcome;
- adversity measurement is unnecessary.

Construct validation remains theory-dependent.

## 32. Resilience factors are not mechanisms

A factor predicts better outcome under adversity.

A mechanism explains how it changes the adversity–outcome pathway.

Examples:
- social support = factor/resource;
- increased perceived control = candidate mechanism;
- practical assistance = another candidate mechanism.

Correlation does not identify the mechanism.

## 33. Promotive and protective effects differ

Promotive factor:
- predicts better outcome generally.

Protective factor:
- specifically buffers adversity effects.

A main effect is not automatically a resilience-specific process.

Moderation must also be interpreted carefully because vulnerability and differential-susceptibility patterns can produce interactions.

## 34. Positive appraisal style is promising, not total

The 2025 Nature Communications study found positive appraisal style:
- predicted resilience outcomes prospectively;
- was modifiable in an intervention sample;
- statistically mediated intervention-related resilience improvement.

This strengthens appraisal as a candidate mechanism.

It does not show:
- unrealistic optimism is adaptive;
- appraisal explains all resilience;
- social/material conditions are secondary.

## 35. Trajectory classes are models, not natural types

Trajectory analysis can identify patterns such as:
- stable functioning;
- disruption/recovery;
- delayed impairment;
- chronic difficulty.

But class solutions depend on:
- outcome;
- assessment spacing;
- total follow-up;
- class number;
- exposure measurement;
- model assumptions.

Do not label a person as a fixed "resilience type" from one latent-class solution.

## 36. Background adversity changes resilience classification

A focal event does not equal total exposure.

Unmeasured background stressors can cause:
- apparent resilience misclassification;
- biased protective-factor estimates;
- incorrect trajectory interpretation.

The 2025 longitudinal review and later methodological critique reinforce exposure measurement as a core requirement.

## 37. Daily resilience is a different timescale

Daily resilience studies often measure:
- perceived ability to recover;
- affective recovery after daily stressors.

These microprocesses can be useful.

They cannot automatically be generalized to:
- bereavement;
- chronic adversity;
- severe trauma;
- long-term functional resilience.

Timescale and adversity magnitude matter.

## 38. Resilience can be domain-specific

Possible outcomes:
- mental health;
- work/education;
- relationships;
- physical health;
- cognition.

Good functioning in one domain can coexist with cost in another.

Therefore:

**resilient in domain X at time T**
is more defensible than
**a resilient person**.

## 39. Resilience is not absence of stress response

A resilient trajectory can include:
- strong acute response;
- temporary impairment;
- later recovery;
- support use;
- active coping;
- adaptive learning.

No requirement exists that a resilient individual should show:
- low cortisol;
- low distress;
- high HRV;
- minimal arousal.

Response amplitude and long-term adaptation are separate.

## 40. Trauma is a separate dependency

Stressful event and traumatic exposure are not synonymous.

STR currently supports generic stress/coping/resilience reasoning.

Trauma-specific questions require separate work on:
- exposure definitions;
- post-traumatic trajectories;
- memory/intrusion/avoidance;
- PTSD criteria and differential diagnosis;
- trauma-informed intervention evidence.

These should not be smuggled into generic stress inference.

## 41. Burnout is not stress physiology

Burnout is a work-related construct/syndrome.

It may relate to chronic demands but is not interchangeable with:
- chronic stress exposure;
- cortisol pattern;
- allostatic load;
- resilience failure.

Work/clinical measurement requires its own construct validity.

## 42. Stress history modifies later response

Prior exposure can produce:
- habituation;
- sensitization;
- learning;
- changed appraisal;
- altered coping choices.

Current response must therefore be interpreted relative to stress history when possible.

Same task ≠ same biological meaning across histories.

## 43. Measurement hierarchy

Use the channel that matches the question:

| Question | Relevant measurement |
| --- | --- |
| What stressor occurred? | objective/contextual exposure measure |
| How stressful was it perceived? | appraisal/perceived-stress measure |
| What biological systems responded? | system-specific physiology |
| How fast did the response resolve? | repeated temporal sampling |
| What coping happened? | event-linked behavior/report |
| Was coping effective? | specified outcome after tactic |
| Was support available? | perceived/received/type/source measures |
| Was resilience present? | adversity-qualified longitudinal outcome |
| Is a resource protective? | adversity × factor model or stronger causal design |
| Is a mechanism causal? | temporal/experimental/mediation evidence with alternatives |

No row automatically substitutes for another.

## 45. Empirical tensions that remain open

Open empirical questions include:
- when reduced versus increased stress reactivity is adaptive;
- how coping fit should be operationalized across contexts;
- which support pathways are causal;
- which resilience factors are true mechanisms;
- how adversity should be aggregated across time;
- whether latent resilience trajectories replicate across samples;
- how biological adaptation and psychosocial resilience map onto each other.

These are research uncertainties, not reasons to collapse constructs.

## 46. Red-team inference cases

**Case A — strong subjective stress, flat cortisol.**
Possible valid conclusion: high subjective stress with little measured cortisol reactivity at sampled times. Do not invalidate the subjective report or infer HPA pathology.

**Case B — low heart-rate response to stress task.**
Could reflect low exposure potency, habituation, high baseline, blunting, medication, disengagement, or individual physiology. Not resilience by itself.

**Case C — repeated avoidance.**
Could be adaptive threat protection or maladaptive narrowing. Need actual risk, controllability, timing, and long-term consequence.

**Case D — high coping-flexibility questionnaire score.**
Does not prove event-level strategy–situation fit.

**Case E — high perceived social support.**
Predicts adjustment on average but does not identify received support or the causal pathway.

**Case F — low symptoms after major event.**
Need total/background adversity, baseline functioning, and follow-up before classifying resilient outcome.

**Case G — high resilience scale score.**
May capture personality, process beliefs, or environmental resources; not direct proof of adaptation under adversity.

**Case H — stable low-symptom trajectory.**
Could reflect genuinely resilient functioning or relatively low cumulative exposure.

**Case I — high allostatic load.**
Indicates composite multisystem burden under the chosen index, not a direct measure of psychological-stress history.

## 47. Cross-domain routing

1. **LEA:** learning, threat/reward contingencies, extinction, habituation boundaries.
2. **MOT:** effort, reward, goals, controllability/value.
3. **AFF:** affective appraisal, emotional response and regulation.
4. **STR:** stressor exposure, multilevel stress response, coping, adaptation/resilience.
5. **SOC:** social cognition, attribution, norms, group processes, social influence.
6. **REL:** interpersonal support, conflict, attachment, dyadic regulation.
7. **PER:** stable trait claims and individual differences.
8. **DEV:** developmental stress/resilience differences.
9. **NEU:** neural/HPA/autonomic mechanisms and causal circuitry.
10. **PSYCHOMETRICS:** scale construction, invariance, individual score interpretation.
11. **CLN:** disorders, trauma-related syndromes, diagnostic boundaries.
12. **BHC:** intervention efficacy and behavior change.

This prevents stress from becoming an all-purpose explanation.

## 48. Why SOC is the next core dependency

RESEARCH_ORDER places social cognition after STR.

STR has already established that:
- social support matters;
- appraisal depends partly on social context;
- interpersonal resources alter coping.

But generic stress science does not yet supply sufficient foundations for:
- attribution;
- person perception;
- mentalizing;
- empathy;
- social inference;
- conformity/norms;
- group identity;
- social influence.

These are independent core social-psychology dependencies.

## 49. Integrated Stress/Coping/Resilience Inference Gate — 52 questions

### A. Exposure and appraisal
1. What exact stressor/adversity is present?
2. Is exposure measured objectively, subjectively, or both?
3. What is its duration/frequency?
4. Is total/background exposure measured?
5. Is objective control separated from perceived control?
6. Are predictability and uncertainty specified?
7. Are demands and resources assessed?
8. Could the same exposure have different appraisals?

### B. Response
9. Is subjective stress distinguished from negative affect?
10. Is arousal distinguished from stress?
11. Which physiological system is measured?
12. Does sampling match the system's kinetics?
13. Is baseline valid?
14. Is peak timing measured?
15. Is reactivity separated from recovery?
16. Are cross-system differences preserved?
17. Could non-stress biological factors explain the measure?
18. Is low reactivity being interpreted as resilience?

### C. Repeated/chronic processes
19. Is acute stress separated from repeated acute/chronic exposure?
20. Is within-episode recovery separated from habituation?
21. Could sensitization occur?
22. Is chronic stress defined as exposure, response, burden, or outcome?
23. Is allostatic-load operationalization specified?
24. Is allostatic load being mistaken for direct psychological stress?

### D. Coping
25. What is the coping target?
26. Is problem-focused coping feasible under actual control?
27. Is emotion-focused coping specified beyond the label?
28. Is meaning-focused coping distinct?
29. Is acceptance separated from resignation?
30. Is avoidance evaluated across short and long horizons?
31. Is strategy use separated from effectiveness?
32. Is coping flexibility defined explicitly?
33. Is repertoire breadth separated from strategy–situation fit?
34. Does switching actually improve fit/outcome?
35. Are structural/material resource constraints included?

### E. Social resources
36. Is perceived support separated from received support?
37. Are support source and type specified?
38. Is support matched to recipient goals?
39. Is support a factor/resource or an identified mechanism?
40. Could common-method/cross-sectional design inflate association?

### F. Resilience
41. Is resilience trait, process, or outcome?
42. Is adversity required by the operationalization?
43. What exact outcome/domain is assessed?
44. Over what follow-up period?
45. Are protective and promotive effects separated?
46. Is a factor being called a mechanism without causal evidence?
47. Are trajectory classes being reified as person types?
48. Is background adversity measured?
49. Is questionnaire resilience being substituted for longitudinal resilient outcome?

### G. Inference
50. Could another domain (AFF/MOT/CLN/SOC) better explain the observation?
51. What viable competing stress/coping/resilience mechanism remains?
52. What is the narrowest conclusion supported by exposure, timing, process, and outcome?

## 51. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- stressor, subjective stress, physiology, and health burden are distinct;
- acute response amplitude has no universal adaptive sign;
- system-specific timing is required for physiological interpretation;
- coping value depends on context/control/outcome;
- coping frequency is not efficacy;
- resilience requires adversity and outcome specification;
- resilience factor is not automatically a mechanism;
- resilience questionnaires are not the same as longitudinal resilient outcomes;
- allostatic load is operationally heterogeneous.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- challenge/resource appraisals relate to performance;
- coping flexibility relates to adjustment;
- strategy–situation fit can improve inference;
- social support robustly relates to adjustment;
- positive appraisal style is a promising modifiable resilience factor;
- repeated exposure can produce system-specific habituation.

### Not established as universal
- one biomarker of stress;
- one optimal coping strategy;
- one resilience scale;
- one resilience trajectory taxonomy;
- low physiological reactivity as resilience;
- social support as one causal mechanism;
- positive appraisal as the sole resilience process.

## 52. Sufficiency decision

**STR-01…04 are sufficient for current generic stress/coping/resilience inference.**

Rationale:
1. stressors, appraisals, response channels, and recovery are separated;
2. acute, repeated, and chronic temporal structures are explicit;
3. HPA/ANS/inflammation measurement and biomarker boundaries are covered;
4. allostasis/allostatic-load limits are explicit;
5. coping is contextualized by control, resources, goals, and strategy–situation fit;
6. social support/resource mechanisms remain appropriately qualified;
7. resilience requires adversity-qualified, outcome-specific, longitudinal inference;
8. person-level clinical/trauma claims are blocked;
9. remaining open questions route cleanly to SOC/PER/DEV/NEU/PSYCHOMETRICS/CLN/BHC/REL.

No scientifically necessary **STR-05** is identified for the current generic foundation.

Close STR as **COMPLETE_FOR_CURRENT_SCOPE**, not "stress science solved."

## Canonical operating rule

**Define stressor and cumulative exposure -> assess appraisal/control/resources -> measure the correct response channel at the correct time -> separate reactivity, recovery, habituation, and cumulative burden -> specify coping target and strategy–situation fit -> measure support/resources without assuming mechanism -> define resilience relative to adversity, outcome, and time -> separate factors from mechanisms and scales from observed adaptation -> compare competing explanations -> state the narrowest supported inference.**

Never:
**stressor = response; biomarker = stress; low response = resilience; coping label = adaptive value; flexibility = strategy count; support = mechanism; good outcome = resilience without adversity; questionnaire = resilience outcome; trajectory = person type.**
