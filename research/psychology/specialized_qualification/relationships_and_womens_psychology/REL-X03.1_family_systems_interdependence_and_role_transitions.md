## 2. Evidence ledger

No personal information used in searches.

E1. Rusbult & Van Lange (2003), Interdependence, Interaction, and Relationships. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.54.101601.145059 . Publisher abstract directly inspected. Theory differentiates the structure of interdependent situations from motivational transformation (consideration of longer-term objectives and another person's welfare), and repeated situational adaptation. Conceptual review, not a quantified empirical effect. Shared outcomes do not imply identical goals, preferences or influence. No individual motive inferred from a payoff structure.

E2. McNulty et al. (2021), How both partners' individual differences, stress, and behavior predict change in relationship satisfaction: Extending the VSA model. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2101402118 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8271655/ . Indexed author abstract, design and discussion excerpts; direct PMC access blocked. Pooled ten longitudinal studies, 1,104 different-sex newlywed couples, five to ten waves across two to four years. Own and partner enduring qualities were associated with satisfaction trajectories through measured behavioral exchanges; stress moderated several associations. Behavioral statistical mediation and stress moderation are not identified causal mechanisms. Pooled sample heterogeneity, possible common-method or selection effects, and limited relationship stage constrain transfer. Exact path coefficients, uncertainty and model sensitivity not verified.

E3. Ross, Nguyen, Karney & Bradbury (2022), Three tests of the Vulnerability-Stress-Adaptation Model: Independent prediction, mediation, and generalizability. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.921485 . Publisher abstract, participant/procedure excerpts and discussion directly inspected. 431 newlywed couples recruited in low-income Los Angeles neighborhoods; 388 analyzed after exclusions. Four assessments nine months apart, observational interaction coding and separate self-report. Vulnerabilities, stress and adaptive processes were independently associated with satisfaction, but communication did not statistically mediate the vulnerability or stress associations. Couples who had separated/divorced did not complete later interviews; missing early stress data for many participants and differences in sample and model matter. This is counterevidence to a universal communication mediation pathway, not proof that communication never matters. Sample overlap with E2's pooled cohorts not established; do not count as guaranteed independent replication. Exact coefficients and precision not checked.

E4. Falconier, Jackson, Hilpert & Bodenmann (2015), Dyadic coping and relationship satisfaction: A meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2015.07.002 ; https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Dyadic-coping-and-relationship-satisfaction-A/99511107402346 . Complete institutional author abstract directly inspected. 72 independent samples from 57 reports, 17,856 participants; pooled zero-order association r=.45 between reported dyadic coping and satisfaction. Partner or combined perceptions were more strongly associated than own perceptions. Distinguish joint coping, stress communication, supportive or delegated coping, and negative responses. Reporter effects, shared-method variance, reverse direction and selection remain possible. No causal estimate or individual prediction, and moderator nulls do not establish equivalence. Full-text uncertainty and bias analysis not inspected.

E5. Doss, Rhoades, Stanley & Markman (2009), The effect of the transition to parenthood on relationship quality: an 8-year prospective study. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0013969 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19254107/ . Indexed original abstract and PMC result excerpts. 218 couples assessed across the first eight years of marriage. Parent trajectories showed more abrupt deterioration on several outcomes around first birth; nonparents showed more gradual change. Modeling a transition-related level discontinuity differs from comparing overall slopes. Nonrandom entry into parenthood, concurrent changes, selection and timing limit causal identification. Exact group counts, outcome-specific estimates and precision not independently checked.

E6. Mitnick, Heyman & Slep (2009), Changes in relationship satisfaction across the transition to parenthood: a meta-analysis. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20001143/ . Indexed author abstract: 37 pregnancy-to-parenthood studies and four childless-newlywed comparisons. Nonparents showed declines similar to parents across comparable observation spans. Counterexample to assuming all change is birth-specific. E5 and E6 can differ in estimand (abrupt level change versus aggregate decline), measurement and comparator design; primary-study overlap unresolved. Reuses source previously recorded in WOM-05.3, not new independent evidence. Exact pooled estimates not rechecked.

E7. Division of Household and Childcare Labor and Relationship Conflict Among Low-Income New Parents. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28348454/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5365149/ . Indexed original abstract and methods extracts. Longitudinal interviews with 108 couples. Perceived unfairness of housework changes was associated with conflict, but fairness did not significantly mediate the relationship between task redistribution and later conflict. Differentiate amount of labor, workload, subjective fairness, prior expectations and conflict. Do not generalize housework results to childcare or infer a causal mechanism. Exact model estimates and attrition not checked.

E8. An Actor-Partner interdependence model of parenting difficulties over time in stepfamilies (2022). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35655389/ . Indexed original abstract; direct PubMed blocked. 676 remarried couples, predominantly White and college educated, follow-up after one year. Several actor associations and a limited pattern of partner associations, varying by stepfamily constellation. This supports considering family configuration rather than presuming symmetric cross-spouse paths; exact coefficients, null uncertainty, attrition and moderator tests not verified. No inference about an individual parent or stepfamily.

E9. Conger et al. (1999), Couple resilience to economic pressure. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9972553/ . Indexed original abstract; over 400 married couples followed three years. Family stress model tested links among economic pressure, marital distress and couple interaction; observed problem solving was reported as a moderator of conflict-related distress. Economic resources, perceived pressure and emotional response differ. Observational moderation is not an intervention effect; historical cohort, exact estimates and generalizability not verified.

## 3. Integrated framework
Family systems: distinguish romantic couple, coparenting, parent-child and other family relationships; each subsystem has distinct roles, functions, preferences and outcomes. Shared family context can influence multiple subsystems without a direct causal arrow between them. No assumption that a child's needs and an adult relationship necessarily compete in a fixed zero-sum manner. A parental role can be important while other roles remain valuable. Do not infer neglect, loss of romantic interest or individual blame solely from time allocation.

Interdependence: an outcome can depend on own action, the other person's action, joint decisions and external resources. Dependence is not the same as affection, dedication or voluntary choice; reciprocal dependence does not imply equal power or symmetrical effects. Distinguish material and perceived constraints, goals, opportunities, role requirements, investments, available support, subjective fairness, desired relationship and observed action. Respect rights and autonomy independently of system-level outcomes.

Conceptual dyadic model: Y_A(t+1) = f(Y_A(t), X_A(t), X_B(t), C(t), error_A); analogous equation for B. X_A and X_B are actor-specific observations; C denotes shared circumstances. An actor association links own predictor to own outcome; a partner association links one actor's predictor to the other's outcome conditional on specified model structure. Residual covariance, assortative selection, shared causes and feedback can explain dyadic similarity. A fitted APIM coefficient is not causal by definition. Repeated measures permit separation of stable between-couple differences and within-couple changes only if modeled appropriately. Simultaneous effects, time-lagged effects, transition discontinuities and long-run slopes are distinct estimands.

Competing explanations for change in family interaction:
- changed opportunity/resources with stable valuation;
- changed role priority or preferences;
- shared external stressor or separate actor-specific stress;
- partner interaction or reciprocal feedback;
- selection, ordinary development, preexisting trends or measurement changes.
They may coexist. No systemic theory licenses assigning an unobserved motive or distributing responsibility equally in situations involving coercion or harm.

## 4. Operational inference
1. Identify the actor, subsystem, exact outcome, transition and timescale. Separate individual, dyadic and household-level measures.
2. Establish actual pretransition observations, role obligations, resources and freely stated preferences; do not assume a baseline. Distinguish task allocation from time demands, subjective fairness and goal importance.
3. Compare plausible alternative explanations; identify observations that would discriminate them rather than merely match all models. Natural variation in opportunities can help distinguish constraints from preference, but does not identify unique cause.
4. Separate actor, partner and shared predictors; account for dependence, selection, preexisting trend, attrition and reciprocal direction. Do not multiply one shared event into independent evidence.
5. Distinguish between-person levels, within-person changes, abrupt transitions and gradual slopes; compare estimands before declaring conflicting studies incompatible. Statistical mediation requires additional causal assumptions.
6. Respect autonomous choices and the welfare of each family member. No using children, financial constraints, engineered dependence or distress as leverage. No diagnosis or claim that the system's equilibrium proves its members' preferences.
7. Update the smallest supported claim. Keep unsupported explanations open and individual forecasts qualitative unless validated. Explicitly preserve the possibility of asymmetric effects.

## 5. Internal synthetic checks
Rubric specified before responses: construct separation, competing explanation, discriminating evidence, inference boundary. Critical errors: unsupported diagnosis or motive, causality inflation, deterministic transfer, ignoring counterevidence, coercion or privacy breach. All scenarios newly authored and generic, not held-out.

A. Both partners report lower leisure satisfaction after a rent increase. Response: shared exposure and partner influence differ; separate budget constraints, individual appraisals and mutual interaction. Compare pre-change trajectories and naturally differing circumstances. No causal partner coefficient inferred from synchronous change.
B. A caregiver reduces participation in a valued language class after accepting a new shift schedule. Response: effort, valuation and opportunity differ; stable preference and durable reprioritization may coexist. Compare voluntary accounts and naturally restored access. No role-based personality inference.
C. A study finds communication mediation; another finds independent associations but null mediation. Response: distinguish predictors, statistical mediation and mechanism. Compare populations, time windows and model assumptions, including possible cohort overlap. No universal mediation or proof of zero communication effect.
D. Two households allocate equal task hours but report different fairness ratings. Response: equality and fairness differ; expectations, task complexity and paid work compete. Examine actual workload and voluntarily reported expectations. No universal allocation prescription.
E. Parents and nonparents show comparable four-year declines, but only parents show abrupt change at a transition. Response: cumulative change and discontinuity are distinct; selection and concurrent events compete. Compare pre-event trends, timing and equivalent measures. No contradiction at identical estimand or identified birth causation.
F. One family configuration shows a partner association and another does not. Response: distinguish actor/partner effects, subgroup contrasts and uncertainty. Consider configuration, sample selection and power; compare direct interaction estimates. No assumed universal symmetry or individual prediction.
