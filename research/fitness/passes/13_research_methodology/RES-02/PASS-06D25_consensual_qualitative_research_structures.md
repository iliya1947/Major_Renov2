# 2. Canonical scientific target

CQR is designed to obtain a **rich, inductive, context-sensitive description of participants' experiences, attitudes, beliefs or perceptions**, while using multiple analyst perspectives, team consensus and auditing to construct a systematically checked cross-case account.

A useful canonical question is:

> What do participants report about this phenomenon, how can each case be summarized faithfully, and which patterns are shared or variable across cases?

### Canonical project representation

open-ended but comparable data
+
small/intensive cases
+
within-case domains
+
core ideas
+
team consensus
+
auditor
+
cross-analysis
+
categories/subcategories
+
case-frequency labels.

### Critical rule

CQR is not simply:

> “qualitative study with more than one coder.”

Its analytic architecture is substantially more specific.

---

# 3. Philosophical architecture

Current Hill/Knox guidance characterizes CQR as **predominantly constructivist with some postpositivist elements**.

## Constructivist components

CQR acknowledges that:

- qualitative data are constructed rather than direct access to one objective truth;
- participants may experience multiple legitimate realities;
- researcher perspective affects interpretation;
- context matters.

## Postpositivist components

CQR also deliberately seeks:

- standardized/common foundational interview questions across cases;
- bracketing of researcher biases where possible;
- consensus across analysts;
- external auditing;
- close traceability to raw data;
- some degree of reproducibility/replicability across research teams/samples.

### Project rule

Do not classify CQR as:

- purely phenomenological;
- purely postpositivist;
- purely relativist.

Its distinctive architecture deliberately combines elements of both traditions.

---

# 4. CQR assumes constructed data, not objective transcript truth

Hill & Knox explicitly state that CQR does not assume an objective truth waiting to be directly recovered.

The interview itself is produced through:

- participant;
- interviewer;
- question wording;
- context;
- follow-up probes.

### Critical nuance

CQR nevertheless tries to represent:

> participants' meanings rather than researchers' preferred theoretical interpretation.

This explains its simultaneous use of:

- constructivist co-construction;
- bracketing;
- team consensus;
- auditing.

---

# 5. Inductive orientation

CQR is primarily inductive.

Researchers generally seek to avoid imposing a strong pre-existing coding framework.

The method emphasizes:

- open-ended questions;
- participant language/meaning;
- bottom-up domain/category development;
- repeated return to raw data.

### Critical rule

An interview guide can be theory-informed.

But if all domains/categories are pre-specified from theory and data are only sorted into them, the analysis moves away from canonical inductive CQR.

---

# 6. Open-ended but comparable data collection

Traditional CQR usually uses:

- semi-structured/open-ended interviews;
- broadly consistent foundational questions across participants;
- flexible probing for depth.

Hill & Knox caution that radically changing the foundational protocol between cases can weaken cross-case comparability.

### Important boundary

CQR differs from some grounded-theory designs where:

> later interviews may be substantially redesigned based on emerging theory.

CQR can refine probes, but it generally values a stable common interview core.

---

# 7. Data words/stories rather than measurement scores

Traditional CQR treats:

- words;
- narratives;
- descriptions;

as the primary raw material.

Quantitative variables may exist in the parent study, but CQR itself analyzes qualitative material.

### Rule

A mixed-method study can contain a CQR component.

Store:

parent quantitative/mixed design
+
CQR qualitative component
+
integration logic.

---

# 8. Traditional sample logic

Classic CQR is designed for **small, intensively examined samples**.

Methodological sources commonly recommend approximately:

> 8–15 cases

for traditional in-depth CQR interviews.

### Critical rule

8–15 is a methodological recommendation/heuristic, not a universal validity threshold.

Relevant considerations include:

- study aim;
- sample homogeneity/heterogeneity;
- interview richness;
- subgroup comparisons;
- available analyst capacity;
- stability of categories.

### Current example

The 2026 exercise-science CQR study used 17 participants because representational goals required additional recruitment.

---

# 9. Case rather than utterance is a central organizational unit

Traditional CQR preserves each participant as a **case** during early analysis.

Analysis first asks:

> What is present in this person's account?

Only after domains/core ideas are developed within cases does cross-analysis examine commonalities across cases.

### Critical rule

Do not begin by pooling all transcript fragments across participants as if case provenance were irrelevant.

Within-case integrity precedes cross-case frequency characterization.

---

# 10. Primary research team

Canonical CQR uses a **primary analysis team** rather than one solo analyst.

Common applied structures use:

- approximately 3–5 team members;
- an additional auditor or auditors.

### Why multiple analysts?

The aim is not merely labor division.

Multiple analysts provide:

- different perspectives;
- challenge to individual assumptions;
- richer interpretation;
- protection against one person's blind spots.

### Critical rule

“Three coders” alone does not establish CQR.

The team must actually perform consensual CQR analysis.

---

# 11. Consensus is constitutive

Consensus is a defining CQR operation.

Analysts typically:

1. examine material independently;
2. propose domains/core ideas/categories;
3. discuss disagreements;
4. return to raw data;
5. revise interpretations;
6. reach a shared analytic formulation.

### Scientific function

Consensus is intended to integrate multiple perspectives into an interpretation that all primary analysts can support.

---

# 12. Consensus ≠ participant consensus

This distinction is crucial.

In CQR:

> **research analysts** reach consensus about how to represent the data.

Participants themselves do **not** need to agree with one another.

A category can represent:

- disagreement;
- minority experience;
- contradictory perspectives.

### Rule

Do not interpret “consensual qualitative research” as:

> methodology for finding participant consensus.

---

# 13. Consensus ≠ majority vote

Canonical CQR aims for discussion until the analysis team reaches a mutually acceptable interpretation.

A procedure such as:

> two-thirds vote wins

is an adaptation away from the strongest canonical consensus logic.

### Project rule

Extract:

- full consensus?;
- majority vote?;
- lead-researcher adjudication?;
- unresolved dissent?;
- rotating/internal consensus?;

Do not call them equivalent.

---

# 14. Consensus ≠ intercoder reliability

Traditional intercoder reliability asks:

> How independently similar were coders before reconciliation?

CQR asks:

> What understanding can the analysts construct through discussion and return to the data?

### Critical rule

CQR consensus is not the same as:

- Cohen's kappa;
- percent agreement;
- majority coding;
- codebook reliability testing.

Intercoder reliability may be added, but it is not the defining CQR validity logic.

---

# 15. CQR is an important exception to D12's cross-family rule

PASS-06D12 correctly established:

> consensus coding / multiple coders are not universal qualitative quality requirements.

CQR is a **methodology-specific exception**.

Here:

- team consensus;
- auditor challenge;

are central to the methodology itself.

### Project implication

The classifier must route quality criteria by methodology.

What would be inappropriate as mandatory validation in reflexive TA may be constitutive in CQR.

---

# 16. Biases and expectations

CQR explicitly asks research-team members to examine their:

- backgrounds;
- values;
- prior experiences;
- assumptions;
- expectations;
- relationships to the phenomenon.

Earlier CQR literature distinguished:

- expectations about likely findings;
- personal biases that could shape interpretation.

Later guidance places stronger emphasis on researcher biases/positions and transparent reflexive discussion.

### Rule

Record what the authors actually identify.

Do not force every paper into one historical terminology version.

---

# 17. Bracketing in CQR

CQR generally uses **bracketing** differently from descriptive phenomenological epoché.

Its practical goal is:

> become aware of researcher assumptions and set them aside as much as possible while interpreting participant data.

### Critical boundary

This does not imply:

> researcher influence is fully removed.

Current CQR explicitly acknowledges constructed data and continuing researcher influence.

### Project wording

Prefer:

> CQR-style bias awareness/bracketing

rather than:

> complete elimination of subjectivity.

---

# 18. Team diversity is an analytic resource, not automatic validity

CQR values multiple perspectives.

Team diversity can include:

- discipline;
- identity;
- lived experience;
- clinical expertise;
- methodological experience.

### Critical rule

Diverse demographics alone do not prove strong CQR.

The paper should show:

- how different perspectives entered analysis;
- how disagreement was resolved;
- how assumptions were examined.

---

# 19. Stage 1 — domains

A **domain** is a broad topic area used to organize participant material.

Examples in exercise-science recruitment:

- research participation;
- researcher behavior/decisions;
- safety.

### Procedure

Analysts identify or refine domains and then assign portions of each case to relevant domains.

### Critical rule

Domain ≠ final theme.

It is primarily an organizing structure for within-case analysis.

---

# 20. Domain coding can be iterative

Initial domains may be:

- revised;
- split;
- merged;
- renamed;

as additional cases are examined.

Earlier cases should be reconsidered when the domain system changes.

### Rule

A codebook frozen after one transcript without rechecking earlier material is weaker alignment with CQR's iterative logic.

---

# 21. Stage 2 — core ideas

**Core ideas** are concise summaries of the essential meaning of a participant's material within a domain.

They aim to:

- remove redundancy;
- preserve context;
- remain close to participant meaning;
- reduce long transcript passages into comparable analytic units.

### Critical rule

Core idea ≠ abstract theoretical interpretation.

It should summarize the case faithfully enough that the raw data can still be traced.

---

# 22. Core idea abstraction is intermediate

A strong core idea is:

- shorter than the raw transcript;
- more precise than a verbatim quote;
- less abstract than a high-level cross-case category.

### Project extraction

Record whether the study actually used:

- core ideas;
- summaries;
- direct category coding without core ideas.

This distinction helps identify traditional CQR vs CQR-M/adaptations.

---

# 23. Stage 3 — cross-analysis

After within-case domains/core ideas are developed, CQR compares material **across cases**.

Cross-analysis identifies:

- common categories;
- subcategories;
- meaningful variation;
- shared patterns.

### Canonical movement

case
→ domain
→ core idea
→ compare core ideas across cases
→ categories/subcategories.

### Critical rule

Cross-analysis is not merely:

> count existing codes.

The category structure is itself developed through consensual cross-case interpretation.

---

# 24. Category ≠ domain

A domain is a broad topic area.

A category/subcategory is a pattern identified within a domain across cases.

### Example

Domain:
> Safety.

Possible categories:
- political safety;
- physical safety;
- psychosocial safety.

### Rule

Do not flatten the hierarchy when extracting CQR findings.

---

# 25. Auditor role

The auditor is distinct from the primary consensus team.

Classic/current CQR gives the auditor a critical-review role across:

- domains;
- core ideas;
- cross-analysis.

The auditor can ask:

- Was raw material placed in the correct domain?
- Was important material omitted?
- Does the core idea faithfully summarize the participant?
- Does category organization make conceptual sense?
- Is there a better alternative organization?

### Critical rule

Auditing is not a ceremonial signature.

It should create meaningful challenge to the primary team's interpretation.

---

# 26. External vs internal auditor

CQR applications use:

- external auditors;
- internal rotating auditors;
- multiple auditors.

The 2005 update found variation in practice.

A particularly strong recommendation is:

> external auditing is desirable for the cross-analysis because it reduces team groupthink.

### Project rule

Record auditor independence.

Do not treat:

> team member checked another team member's coding

as identical to an external audit.

---

# 27. Auditor does not simply overrule the team

The primary team considers auditor feedback and decides how to revise the analysis.

A strong audit process involves:

- critique;
- response;
- revision;
- possible re-audit.

### Critical rule

Auditor ≠ final omniscient judge.

The quality function is independent challenge + analytic accountability.

---

# 28. Raw-data return is central

CQR repeatedly returns interpretations to original data.

This protects against:

- elegant but unsupported categories;
- consensus drifting away from participant words;
- auditor/team assumptions overriding evidence.

### Rule

Consensus alone is insufficient.

A team can agree and still be wrong about its own data if it does not maintain traceability.

---

# 29. Frequency labels are part of CQR cross-analysis

After categories are formed, CQR characterizes how many **cases** contain each category.

Typical labels include:

- **general**;
- **typical**;
- **variant**;
- sometimes **rare**.

Current/common convention:

- general = all or all but one case;
- typical = more than half of cases up to general;
- variant = at least several/two cases but less than half;
- rare = low-count categories, especially useful in larger samples.

### Critical rule

Exact numerical thresholds depend on:

- sample size;
- CQR version/convention;
- authors' declared rule.

Extract the actual thresholds.

---

# 30. Frequency unit is the case, not mention count

If one participant discusses a category 20 times, that usually still contributes:

> one case to the category frequency.

### Critical rule

CQR category frequency is not:

- quote count;
- word count;
- number of mentions;
- event count.

It is generally:

> number of cases represented in the category.

---

# 31. General / typical / variant ≠ prevalence

This is a major inferential boundary.

Suppose a CQR category is “general” in 14/15 participants.

This supports:

> nearly all cases in this qualitative sample contained the category.

It does **not** establish:

> approximately 93% of the target population has this experience.

### Why not?

Traditional CQR samples are usually:

- small;
- purposive/criterion-based;
- volunteer;
- context-specific;
- not probability samples.

### Project rule

Never translate CQR frequency labels into population prevalence.

---

# 32. Frequency labels ≠ statistical inference

CQR frequency descriptors can help distinguish:

- broadly shared;
- moderately shared;
- less common;

patterns **within the sample**.

They do not provide:

- confidence intervals;
- sampling error;
- hypothesis tests;
- population probabilities.

### Rule

If authors compare CQR frequencies across subgroups, treat this as exploratory qualitative comparison unless a separate quantitative sampling/inference design supports stronger claims.

---

# 33. Rare ≠ unimportant

A low-frequency category can be:

- clinically important;
- ethically important;
- theoretically important;
- a signal of overlooked experience.

### Critical rule

Frequency is not the same as:

- importance;
- severity;
- causal influence.

CQR frequency labels describe sample representativeness, not value.

---

# 34. Sample homogeneity and subgroup logic

Traditional CQR often works best when cases are sufficiently comparable for meaningful cross-analysis.

If the sample contains distinct subgroups, analysts may need to:

- conduct subgroup cross-analysis;
- ensure enough cases per subgroup;
- avoid averaging incompatible contexts.

### Critical rule

A heterogeneous sample does not automatically invalidate CQR.

But cross-case frequency claims need a coherent comparison set.

---

# 35. Saturation is not CQR's sole defining adequacy criterion

Some applied CQR papers also use:

- data saturation;
- thematic saturation.

The 2026 exercise-science paper explicitly used both a CQR sample recommendation and saturation goals.

### Project rule

Do not make generic saturation a universal CQR requirement.

CQR adequacy can additionally be evaluated through:

- intensive case analysis;
- category stability;
- sample coherence;
- cross-case representativeness;
- raw-data traceability.

---

# 36. Stability check history

Early CQR guidance suggested holding out cases to evaluate category stability.

The 2005 update indicated that the formal stability check could be eliminated if other evidence of trustworthiness is presented.

### Project rule

Absence of a held-out stability check is not automatically a CQR quality failure.

Extract the version of guidance being followed.

---

# 37. Member checking boundary

Participant review can be used in CQR.

Examples:

- transcript review;
- clarification;
- review of findings;
- testimonial validity.

The 2005 update discussed participant review as a useful option/recommendation.

### Critical rule

Member checking is **not as constitutive as**:

- analyst consensus;
- auditing;
- domains/core ideas/cross-analysis.

A CQR study should not be rejected solely because no participant “validated” final categories if other methodological integrity is strong.

Consistent with PASS-06D12.

---

# 38. Transcript checking ≠ findings validation

Participant checking may concern:

1. transcription accuracy;
2. factual correction;
3. interpretation/findings;
4. new reflections.

These are different purposes.

### Project rule

Do not record:

> member checking = yes

without specifying what was checked.

# 40. Original CQR evaluation criteria

The 1997 paper proposed criteria including:

- trustworthiness of the method;
- coherence of results;
- representativeness to the sample;
- testimonial validity;
- applicability;
- replicability across samples.

### Project interpretation

These should be read within CQR's own constructivist/postpositivist hybrid framework.

“Replicability” here should not be silently interpreted as:

> identical qualitative results must appear in every new sample.

# 41. CQR-M — purpose

**CQR-M** = Consensual Qualitative Research–Modified for **simple/brief qualitative data**, often with larger samples.

Typical data:

- short written answers;
- open-ended survey responses;
- relatively straightforward narratives.

### Why the modification exists

Traditional CQR is labor-intensive because every interview is analyzed deeply through domains/core ideas and cross-analysis.

That is often impractical for hundreds of short responses.

---

# 42. CQR-M architecture

CQR-M retains key elements such as:

- inductive coding;
- multiple judges;
- independent initial analysis;
- consensus;
- auditor;
- domains/categories/subcategories;
- frequency summaries.

But it commonly simplifies:

- depth of within-case contextual analysis;
- core-idea construction.

In many applications, data are placed directly into categories/subcategories because responses are already short and simple.

### Critical rule

Do not demand full traditional core-idea procedures from CQR-M when the variant explicitly omits them.

---

# 43. CQR-M sample/data boundary

CQR-M is suitable when:

- sample is much larger than traditional CQR;
- responses are brief/simple enough for scalable analysis.

### Critical rule

Large N alone does not justify CQR-M.

If each participant supplies long, complex interviews, simply calling the study CQR-M does not solve the analytic workload/context problem.

---

# 44. CQR-M frequency counts still do not equal prevalence

CQR-M may include hundreds of participants and percentages.

That can increase descriptive breadth.

But unless sampling is probability-based and target-population inference is justified:

> category percentages still describe the recruited sample.

### Project rule

Large qualitative sample ≠ representative prevalence study automatically.

---

# 45. CQR-C — purpose

**CQR-C** adapts CQR for **case-study/process research**, especially psychotherapy cases.

Its scientific target is often:

> how a known complex phenomenon unfolds over one case or a small number of cases.

Possible evidence:

- recorded sessions;
- interviews;
- process measures;
- events/episodes;
- therapist/client material.

### Critical rule

CQR-C is not traditional cross-case CQR with N=1.

The analytic architecture shifts toward intensive process/event analysis within case.

# 47. CQR-C frequency labels may be inappropriate

Traditional general/typical/variant labels depend on cross-case counts.

In a single-case CQR-C study, the more relevant units may be:

- events;
- sessions;
- episodes;
- process sequences.

### Project rule

Do not mechanically apply participant-frequency labels to CQR-C.

Extract the actual unit:

- person;
- session;
- event;
- case.

# 48. CQR vs phenomenology

CQR often studies:

- experiences;
- beliefs;
- internal meaning.

That does not make it phenomenology.

## Phenomenology

Scientific target:
- lived-experience structure/essence/meaning according to a phenomenological tradition.

## CQR

Scientific target:
- systematic within/cross-case description of participant meanings using consensual team/audit architecture.

### Critical rule

“Lived experiences” in a CQR paper title ≠ phenomenological methodology.

A study can explicitly combine:

phenomenological parent framing
+
CQR analytic procedures.

Store both when authors clearly do so.

---

# 49. CQR vs generic thematic analysis

## Thematic analysis

Can be conducted:

- solo or team;
- reflexively or with codebooks;
- without auditor;
- without core ideas;
- without case-frequency labels.

## CQR

Requires a much more specific architecture:

- case-based domains/core ideas;
- consensus team;
- auditor;
- cross-analysis;
- sample-frequency characterization.

### Rule

Consensus TA ≠ CQR automatically.

---

# 50. CQR vs qualitative content analysis

Qualitative content analysis can:

- categorize text;
- use multiple coders;
- count categories.

But CQR additionally requires its specific:

- within-case core-idea logic;
- consensus;
- auditing;
- cross-analysis.

### Rule

Category frequency + multiple coders does not establish CQR.

---

# 51. CQR vs Framework Method

Both can organize data systematically across cases.

But Framework Method often uses:

- matrix/charting by case and theme;
- deductive + inductive framework;
- applied team analysis.

CQR uses:

- domains;
- core ideas;
- consensus;
- auditor;
- cross-analysis;
- frequency labels.

### Rule

Matrix-based team analysis ≠ CQR unless Hill-derived architecture is actually implemented.

---

# 52. CQR vs generic consensus coding

A qualitative team may discuss disagreements until consensus.

That is not enough for CQR.

### Full CQR candidate requires most of:

- Hill lineage;
- open-ended comparable data;
- domains;
- core ideas;
- cross-analysis;
- auditor;
- case-frequency labels;
- consensus.

### Project rule

Use:

> CQR-informed / adapted

when only selected procedures are borrowed.

---

# 53. CQR vs Delphi / consensus method

CQR reaches consensus among **analysts about interpretation of participant data**.

Delphi seeks structured consensus/opinion among a panel on:

- priorities;
- standards;
- forecasts;
- recommendations.

### Critical rule

Analyst consensus ≠ participant/expert consensus research.

CQR remains a qualitative interpretation methodology, not a Delphi-type consensus design.

---

# 54. CQR and theory

Traditional CQR often aims primarily for rich description/patterning rather than formal theory generation.

It can support:

- conceptual interpretation;
- practice implications;
- theory refinement.

But it does not require:

- core category;
- theoretical sampling;
- theoretical saturation;
- substantive theory.

### Rule

CQR ≠ grounded theory merely because it is inductive.

---

# 55. Claims CQR can support

CQR can strongly support contextual claims about:

- participant experiences;
- beliefs;
- attitudes;
- perceptions;
- barriers/facilitators;
- common/variable patterns within a sample;
- cross-case contextual differences;
- practice-relevant categories.

Its frequency architecture allows carefully worded statements such as:

> this category was general/typical/variant in this sample.

---

# 56. Claims CQR cannot establish by itself

CQR alone does not establish:

- population prevalence;
- incidence;
- population proportions;
- treatment effect;
- causal mediation;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism.

### Critical rule

“General category” is not a population-generalizable percentage.

# 66. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| CQR vs “multiple coders” | CQR has domains/core ideas/cross-analysis/auditor architecture |
| consensus among analysts vs consensus among participants | different target |
| full consensus vs majority vote | canonical CQR seeks mutual analytic agreement |
| consensus vs intercoder reliability | constructed agreement vs independent reliability statistic |
| team diversity vs automatic rigor | use of differences matters |
| biases acknowledged vs bias eliminated | CQR acknowledges continuing researcher influence |
| CQR bracketing vs phenomenological epoché | different philosophical/analytic function |
| domain vs final category | domain is broad within-case organizer |
| core idea vs theory | concise data-near summary |
| cross-analysis vs code counting | categories are built across core ideas/cases |
| auditor vs second coder | auditor independently critiques team analysis |
| audit vs ceremonial approval | must challenge fidelity/organization |
| case frequency vs mention frequency | one case usually counts once/category |
| general/typical/variant vs prevalence | qualitative sample descriptors |
| frequency vs importance | rare categories can be important |
| 8–15 recommendation vs hard validity threshold | methodological heuristic |
| saturation vs universal CQR requirement | applied adjunct, not sole core architecture |
| member checking vs mandatory CQR validity | useful but less constitutive than consensus/audit |
| transcript checking vs analytic validation | different functions |
| CQR-M vs traditional CQR | brief/simple larger-sample variant |
| CQR-M vs large-N qualitative automatically | data simplicity + architecture required |
| CQR-C vs ordinary CQR with N=1 | case/process architecture differs |
| CQR-C vs parent case study | preserve both layers |
| CQR vs phenomenology | experience target alone does not create phenomenology |
| CQR vs thematic analysis | CQR has a specific team/case/audit architecture |
| CQR vs content analysis | category counting alone is insufficient |
| CQR vs Framework Method | matrices/framework ≠ CQR core ideas/cross-analysis |
| CQR vs Delphi | analyst interpretation consensus ≠ expert-panel consensus |
| CQR vs grounded theory | no requirement for substantive theory/theoretical sampling |
| CQR frequency difference vs statistical group difference | no sampling-error inference by default |
| “general category” vs universal experience | general only within analyzed cases |

# 69. Status after PASS-06D25

## Established

1. CQR is a distinct Hill-derived qualitative methodology rather than generic team coding.
2. Its scientific target is intensive, inductive within-case understanding plus systematic cross-case patterning.
3. CQR is predominantly constructivist with explicit postpositivist elements.
4. Open-ended but broadly comparable questions support cross-case analysis.
5. Traditional CQR commonly uses small intensive samples; 8–15 is a heuristic, not a hard validity threshold.
6. Consensus among analysts is constitutive and differs from participant consensus, majority voting and intercoder reliability.
7. Bias/expectation reflection and CQR-style bracketing are explicit methodological practices but do not eliminate researcher influence.
8. Domains organize within-case material; core ideas summarize participant meaning; cross-analysis builds categories/subcategories across cases.
9. Auditors independently challenge domain/core-idea/cross-analysis fidelity and are not merely extra coders.
10. General/typical/variant/rare labels describe case representation within the sample and do not estimate prevalence.
11. Category frequency is normally case-based rather than mention-based.
12. Saturation may be added by applied studies but is not the sole defining CQR adequacy rule.
13. Member checking can be useful but is less constitutive than consensus/audit/core CQR analysis.
14. CQR-M handles larger samples of short/simple qualitative data and may omit traditional core-idea construction.
15. CQR-C adapts CQR to intensive case/process analysis and should retain the parent case-study layer.
16. CQR must be separated from phenomenology, TA, content analysis, Framework Method, Delphi and GT.
17. Current 2026 exercise science and Paralympic research plus established athletic-training/sport literature confirm direct fitness relevance.
