## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a defensible attentional-focus model for later resistance-exercise coaching without:

- treating external focus as universally superior;
- treating internal focus as universally harmful;
- interpreting a no-instruction/control condition as a genuinely neutral internal state;
- collapsing attentional direction (internal/external) with attentional breadth (narrow/broad);
- assuming “more distal external” is always better;
- treating dual-task cost as a pure automaticity score;
- assuming practice automatically eliminates attentional demand;
- assuming experts and novices respond identically;
- treating conscious movement processing as universally damaging;
- using acute EMG/force differences as direct evidence of motor learning;
- using motor-learning findings to override a task-specific hypertrophy/recruitment goal.

### Primary downstream context

Healthy adults learning or refining resistance-exercise technique.

### Critical outcomes

- acute motor performance;
- delayed retention;
- transfer;
- technical execution;
- force/velocity/endurance where relevant;
- target-muscle recruitment where relevant;
- dual-task interference;
- robustness under pressure;
- cognitive/attentional load.

### Main methodological requirement

Separate:

`instructional focus label`
from
`actual focus adopted`
from
`performance outcome`
from
`durable learning`.

## 3. Internal focus

Operationally:

> attention directed toward the body, body part, muscle, joint action, or movement itself.

Examples:

- “drive your knees out”;
- “contract the pecs”;
- “keep your ribs down”;
- attending to hand/arm trajectory.

Important boundary:

**internal focus is an instructional/attentional direction, not equivalent to explicit learning.**

Internal focus can be:

- deliberate or habitual;
- narrow or broad;
- useful or disruptive;
- temporary or sustained.

### Evidence status

The older motor-learning literature frequently reported inferior performance/retention under internal vs external instructions.

However the current evidence base is contested.

The 2024 robust Bayesian reanalysis (PMID 39480294) reported that after models accounting for publication/reporting bias, estimated mean effects of external-over-internal focus were near zero for:

- performance;
- retention;
- transfer;
- EMG;
- external-focus distance.

It also found substantial heterogeneity.

Therefore:

**internal focus cannot be classified as universally harmful.**

Confidence: **HIGH against universal-harm claim**.

---

## 4. External focus

Operationally:

> attention directed toward the intended movement effect, implement, target, environment, or task outcome outside the body.

Examples:

- “push the floor away”;
- “drive the bar toward the ceiling”;
- “move the handle fast”;
- “send the ball to the target.”

### Traditional evidence

The 2021 meta-analysis (PMID 34843301) reported external-over-internal effects for:

- performance: Hedges g ≈ 0.26;
- retention: g ≈ 0.58;
- transfer: g ≈ 0.58.

This historically supported a strong external-focus recommendation.

### Current correction

PMID 39480294 reanalyzed the same broader literature with publication-bias models and estimated near-null average effects.

The 2026 network meta-analysis (PMID 42419609):

- included 87 studies;
- found that **78 required attentional-focus reclassification** under prespecified definitions;
- rankings changed after reclassification;
- distal external and holistic focus were favorable in the primary network;
- expertise altered rankings;
- small-study/reporting-bias sensitivity remained.

Thus:

**external focus is a useful candidate strategy with substantial positive evidence, but universal superiority is not established.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for usefulness / LOW for universal superiority**.

---

## 5. External-focus distance

External focus can be relatively proximal or distal.

Example:

- proximal: bar/implement movement;
- distal: target/end-point effect farther from the body.

The 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID 40949725; 20 RCTs, 497 participants) found distal external focus superior on average to:

- proximal external focus;
- internal focus.

But:

- distal vs proximal did not significantly differ in novices;
- expertise moderated effects.

The 2024 reporting-bias reanalysis found the average distance effect approximately null after bias adjustment.

Therefore:

**“farther is always better” is rejected.**

Distance should be selected for task relevance, learner level and clarity.

---

## 6. External-focus evidence is classification-sensitive

A closure-critical methodological issue:

many studies label cues “external” or “internal” inconsistently.

The 2026 network meta-analysis reclassified 78/87 studies.

This means attentional-focus conclusions can change because of:

- cue wording;
- what counts as the movement effect;
- proximal vs distal external cues;
- holistic/global cues;
- participant adherence;
- control-condition behavior.

Project rule:

**do not rely only on authors' attentional-focus label; inspect the actual instruction.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 7. Neutral / minimal cueing

A “neutral” or no-focus condition means the experimenter does not prescribe an attentional direction.

It does **not** mean the learner has no focus.

In the exploratory finger-task study (PMID 28286760):

- during the nominal control condition, participants spontaneously adopted internal, external or mixed strategies;
- most in that small sample reported internal focus.

Therefore:

`no focus instruction`
≠
`no attentional focus`.

### Practical implication

Minimal cueing is a legitimate option when:

- current execution is adequate;
- the learner already has a functional task representation;
- extra instruction would add no useful information;
- the goal is to observe self-organized performance.

But no strong evidence establishes:

`minimal cueing > well-designed cueing`

as a universal rule.

Project status:

**PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**.

## 8. Holistic focus

Holistic focus directs attention toward the overall/global feeling or effect of the movement rather than one body part or one narrow external effect.

It is not simply the same construct as:

- no instruction;
- broad visual attention;
- external focus.

Evidence:

- badminton RCT (PMID 33495909): holistic and external focus both supported learning relative to some comparison conditions;
- 2024/2025 sport-specific network meta-analysis (PMID 39119107): holistic focus ranked favorably at retention/transfer, but evidence came from very few studies;
- 2026 expertise NMA (PMID 42419609): holistic focus ranked highly in experienced tiers.

Therefore holistic focus is promising but under-replicated.

Canonical boundary:

**holistic focus is a separate attentional strategy, not proof that “broad attention” is universally better.**

---

## 9. Narrow vs broad attention

Attentional **breadth** is a different dimension from attentional **direction**.

### Direction

- internal;
- external.

### Breadth

- narrow: few sources/features;
- broad: larger set/spatial field of information.

A person can therefore have:

- narrow internal focus;
- broad internal focus;
- narrow external focus;
- broad external focus.

The attentional-breadth literature supports that different tasks require different spatial/information windows.

For resistance-exercise technique:

- a narrow task-relevant cue may help isolate one correction;
- a broader/global focus may help preserve integrated coordination.

However direct RCT evidence comparing narrow vs broad attention for compound-lift learning is sparse.

Project rule:

**breadth should match the information demands of the task; no universal narrow/broad winner is established.**

Confidence: **HIGH for taxonomy / LOW-MODERATE for RT prescription**.

## 10. Attentional load

Attentional load is the demand placed on limited cognitive/attentional resources.

It can rise through:

- multiple simultaneous cues;
- complex explicit rules;
- secondary cognitive tasks;
- pressure/anxiety;
- unfamiliar task demands;
- monitoring several body parts simultaneously.

The 2025 visuomotor-adaptation study (PMID 40924717) used a working-memory dual task and found:

- cognitive load suppressed explicit learning;
- implicit adaptation was comparatively spared;
- motor and cognitive tasks competed for cognitive resources.

Important transfer boundary:

this is visuomotor adaptation, not direct compound-lift learning.

It supports the general mechanism:

**high concurrent cognitive demand can interfere with deliberate/explicit components of motor learning.**

It does not establish a universal maximum number of cues.

Exact cue-number decisions remain NEU-16.

---

## 11. Dual-task interference

Dual-task interference occurs when a secondary task changes performance of the primary motor task and/or vice versa.

Possible mechanisms:

- shared attentional capacity;
- working-memory competition;
- response selection bottlenecks;
- task-switching/integration demands.

A dual-task cost can help probe attentional demand.

But:

**dual-task cost is not a pure or universal measure of automaticity.**

Why:

1. the secondary task matters;
2. task pairing matters;
3. priority instructions matter;
4. dual-task practice can improve coordination of the pair;
5. improved dual-task performance may reflect task integration rather than automaticity of the motor skill.

This is consistent with PMID 38484757 and PMID 41844636.

---

## 12. Automaticity

Automaticity is retained from NEU-11 as graded rather than binary.

Practice may reduce some cognitive demands, but skilled execution can still require attention.

The 2026 motor-sequence study (PMID 41844636):

- trained sequences became faster;
- dual-task costs did not simply disappear;
- dual-task cost increased for the practiced sequence under that paradigm;
- deeper sequence knowledge/explicit knowledge was associated with persistent attentional demand.

This directly rejects:

`better learned`
=
`attention free`.

Canonical rule:

**automaticity is task- and measure-dependent reduction/reorganization of control demand, not zero attention.**

---

## 13. Expertise as modifier

Expertise can modify which focus is functional.

Evidence is not fully consistent.

### 2021 meta-analysis

PMID 34843301 did not find skill level as a significant moderator in its meta-regression.

### 2025 distance meta-analysis

PMID 40949725 found expertise moderated attentional-focus-distance effects.

### 2026 network meta-analysis

PMID 42419609 found:

- distal external focus ranked favorably in lower expertise tiers;
- holistic focus ranked highest in higher tiers;
- internal focus appeared detrimental in the highest tiers;
- results remained sensitive to small-study effects/reporting bias.

Therefore:

**expertise is a plausible moderator, but no stable “novice rule” or “expert rule” is yet universal.**

Practical inference:

advanced performers may require less explicit/narrow control and may benefit from higher-level/holistic action goals, but this remains task dependent.

## 14. Direct resistance-exercise evidence: performance

Resistance exercise provides direct evidence that the attentional goal matters.

### Acute strength

Meta-analysis PMID 34822352:

- external focus showed a small acute strength advantage over internal focus;
- long-term strength difference was not statistically significant overall.

### Muscular endurance

Meta-analysis PMID 35010348:

- external > internal, d ≈ 0.58;
- external > control, d ≈ 0.42;
- internal vs control was not significant.

### Bench-press throw

PMID 38281488:

- external focus increased velocity/displacement at some loads;
- effects were load dependent;
- not every mechanical outcome changed.

These outcomes support external focus for some acute performance goals.

They do **not** by themselves prove superior technique learning or retention.

---

## 15. Direct resistance-exercise evidence: internal focus / recruitment

Internal focus can serve a different training objective.

### Long-term RCT

PMID 29533715:

- 30 untrained men;
- 8 weeks, 3 sessions/week;
- internal group focused on target-muscle contraction;
- external group focused on lift outcome;
- elbow-flexor thickness increased more in internal than external: 12.4% vs 6.9%;
- quadriceps thickness did not show the same clear group difference;
- strength differences were not statistically significant.

This is not a technique-learning study.

It demonstrates that:

**an internal focus may be useful when the target outcome is local muscle recruitment/hypertrophy rather than external movement performance.**

### Acute recruitment

PMID 26700744:

- trained men could increase pectoralis/triceps EMG with target-muscle focus at low-to-moderate loads;
- effect was not maintained at 80% 1RM.

PMID 33170116:

- internal focus during squat changed the distribution of muscle excitation.

PMID 40372111 provides a counterexample:

- repeated internal-focus bench-press sessions did not increase pectoralis EMG/RPE.

Therefore target-muscle focus effects are real in some contexts but not guaranteed.

## 16. When can an internal cue be useful for correction?

Internal cues are defensible when they provide **task-relevant information that the learner currently lacks**.

Possible use cases:

1. temporarily directing awareness to a body segment involved in a specific error;
2. changing target-muscle recruitment where that is the explicit goal;
3. increasing proprioceptive/body-position awareness;
4. movement preparation before execution;
5. diagnostic contrast: helping the learner perceive what a correction feels like.

### Evidence supporting non-binary use

PMID 35164654 found that an internal focus during **preparation** combined with external focus during **execution** was not equivalent to sustained internal focus; in adherence-sensitive analysis the switching group outperformed internal focus at retention.

PMID 42261257 (2026 visuomotor adaptation) found internal focus produced greater adaptation than external focus in a task where proprioceptive hand information was directly relevant.

These studies reject:

`internal focus is always harmful`.

### Practical boundary

An internal cue should be:

- tied to a diagnosed problem;
- as brief as possible;
- evaluated by task outcome;
- removed/faded if it stops helping execution.

The exact wording/frequency belongs NEU-16.

Status: **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC grounded in task-relevance evidence**.

---

## 17. Internal focus ≠ “mind-muscle connection” in every context

A major category error is to generalize bodybuilding-style target-muscle focus to all motor skills.

Internal focus can target:

- muscle contraction;
- joint position;
- limb trajectory;
- breathing/body sensation;
- posture.

These are not equivalent interventions.

Similarly, an external focus can target:

- implement;
- surface;
- target;
- trajectory;
- movement effect.

Project rule:

**the actual information target matters more than the binary label alone.**

## 18. Conscious control / reinvestment

Reinvestment refers to conscious engagement in movement control or decision processes, especially after a skill has become established.

A common claim is:

> conscious control disrupts automatic movement.

The evidence is more conditional.

The movement-specific reinvestment meta-analysis:

- 29 studies;
- 410 effect sizes;
- no significant overall association between reinvestment score and motor performance;
- negative association emerged under moderators including:
  - psychological pressure;
  - object manipulation;
  - speed outcomes.

The 2025 psychophysiology systematic review (PMID 40222713) found potential neural/autonomic/oculomotor correlates, but methods and results were heterogeneous.

Therefore:

**conscious motor processing is not universally performance-destroying.**

---

## 19. When can excess conscious control disrupt skilled movement?

The strongest defensible formulation is conditional.

Risk is higher when:

- the movement is already well learned;
- performance is under pressure;
- the performer attempts detailed step-by-step conscious control during execution;
- attentional resources are simultaneously taxed;
- speed/fluidity is important;
- the task involves object manipulation.

Possible mechanisms include:

- reinvestment / explicit monitoring;
- disruption of integrated coordination;
- attentional competition;
- slower online corrections.

But:

- pressure also causes distraction;
- conscious control is not the only choking mechanism;
- meta-analytic associations do not prove one causal mechanism in every performer.

Canonical rule:

**avoid unnecessary detailed conscious control during execution of a well-established skill, especially under pressure, but do not treat all conscious attention as harmful.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for contextual risk / LOW for universal causal rule**.

## 20. Pressure and attentional control

Pressure can alter:

- attentional allocation;
- self-monitoring;
- conscious motor processing;
- distraction;
- arousal.

Current pressure literature supports multiple mechanisms.

Therefore “choking” should not be reduced to:

`pressure → internal focus → failure`.

The 2026 intervention meta-analysis (PMID 41951087) shows that multiple strategies can reduce pressure-related performance loss, especially in closed skills.

This supports flexibility rather than one attentional ideology.

## 21. Neutral/minimal vs focused instruction: practical synthesis

For later strength-technique coaching, the evidence supports a conditional hierarchy rather than one universal focus.

### Minimal/no new cue can be preferable when

- execution is already acceptable;
- the current task representation is working;
- additional instruction would compete with execution;
- the coach wants to observe unprompted performance.

### External/task-effect cue can be preferable when

- task outcome is clear;
- speed/force/endurance/performance is the immediate objective;
- it reduces unnecessary body-part monitoring.

### Internal cue can be preferable when

- a specific body-position/recruitment error is diagnosed;
- proprioceptive information is central;
- target-muscle activation is the goal;
- it is used in preparation/brief correction rather than continuous micromanagement.

### Holistic/global cue can be preferable when

- the skill is well established;
- integrated coordination should be preserved;
- narrow technical micromanagement is counterproductive.

Status:

**PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not a proven universal algorithm.

## 22. Direct compound-lift learning boundary

Direct attentional-focus evidence in resistance training is mostly about:

- acute force;
- muscular endurance;
- EMG;
- hypertrophy;
- velocity/power.

There is far less direct evidence on:

- delayed retention of squat/deadlift/press technique;
- transfer across load;
- error correction under internal vs external cues;
- attentional-focus strategy across novice-to-expert lift learning.

Therefore:

**RT performance/recruitment evidence must not be mislabeled as motor-learning evidence.**

Exact compound-lift cue design remains NEU-16/TEC/COA.

## 23. Narrow vs broad / internal vs external — orthogonal model

A useful project taxonomy:

| | Narrow | Broad / holistic |
|---|---|---|
| Internal | one muscle/joint/body part | overall body state/whole-movement feel |
| External | one target/implement feature | global task effect/environmental relation |

This table is conceptual, not a validated prescription matrix.

It prevents a common error:

`external = broad`
and
`internal = narrow`.

Neither identity is correct.

## 24. Established content-level findings

### HIGH

1. Internal and external focus are attentional-direction constructs, not learning-process labels.
2. Attentional breadth is separate from internal/external direction.
3. No-focus/control conditions do not guarantee absence of attentional focus.
4. Dual-task cost is not a pure automaticity metric.
5. Automaticity is graded and task specific.
6. Acute RT performance/EMG outcomes are not equivalent to durable technique learning.
7. Focus effects depend on the actual cue/information target, not only author labels.

### MODERATE-HIGH

8. External focus is often useful for motor performance and learning, but universal superiority is not established after current reporting-bias analyses.
9. Expertise plausibly modifies optimal focus strategy.
10. Internal target-muscle focus can alter recruitment in some resistance exercises.
11. External focus can improve acute strength/endurance/velocity outcomes in some resistance tasks.
12. Excess conscious movement control can impair established skill in some demanding/pressure contexts.

### MODERATE

13. Holistic focus is a promising alternative to the internal/external binary.
14. Brief internal focus during preparation/correction may be useful while execution uses a task-effect focus.
15. Minimal cueing can be appropriate when extra instruction adds load without useful information.

## 27. Evidence-ledger candidates

1. External-focus superiority is not universal; older meta-analytic benefit is challenged by reporting-bias-adjusted reanalysis.
2. Focus classification and expertise materially alter meta-analytic rankings.
3. No-focus/control conditions can contain spontaneous internal/external focus and are not process-neutral.
4. Attentional breadth is distinct from internal/external direction.
5. Cognitive load can selectively impair explicit components of adaptation; no universal cue-count threshold follows.
6. Dual-task cost probes attentional demand but is not a pure automaticity score.
7. Automaticity does not imply zero attention.
8. Internal focus can be task-relevant and useful for recruitment/proprioceptive correction; it is not universally harmful.
9. External focus can improve some acute resistance-performance outcomes but this is not direct technique-learning evidence.
10. Reinvestment/conscious control is contextually associated with performance decrements, especially under pressure, not universally.
11. Direct compound-lift attentional-focus learning evidence remains sparse.
