## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a sufficiently accurate balance / neuromotor-fitness model for later testing, exercise selection, technique, programming and individualization without:

- treating balance as one general trait;
- treating vision, vestibular input and somatosensation as fixed independent contributors with universal percentages;
- equating eyes-closed performance with “vestibular balance”;
- treating static balance as a proxy for dynamic/reactive/proactive balance;
- treating one balance test as a global balance score;
- treating lower postural sway as universally better;
- treating center-of-pressure movement as identical to center-of-mass movement or whole balance capacity;
- equating proactive/anticipatory balance with reactive balance;
- assuming generic balance training strongly transfers to maximal strength;
- assuming unstable-surface strength training is superior to stable strength training for strength development;
- using fitness balance tasks to diagnose vestibular, neurologic or other clinical balance disorders.

### Question type

Mechanistic + training-effect + assessment/transfer evidence in healthy adults, athletes and healthy older adults, with clinical guidelines used only to define the medical boundary.

### Population/context

Primary downstream target:

- healthy adults;
- resistance-training context.

Supporting populations:

- athletes for higher-demand balance tasks;
- healthy older adults for balance-domain and perturbation-training evidence where the literature is substantially larger.

Clinical populations are not used to infer normal training effects; they are used only to establish the clinical boundary.

### Critical outcomes

- static steady-state balance;
- dynamic balance;
- postural sway / COP;
- proactive/anticipatory balance;
- reactive balance;
- sensory weighting/reweighting;
- transfer to untrained balance tasks;
- transfer to strength/power performance;
- medical red flags / referral boundary.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 10 exact Section 10 items must be covered at content level. The pass must preserve domain specificity, sensory reweighting, COP/sway interpretation limits, proactive-vs-reactive distinction, transfer limits and clinical ownership.

## 3. Core construct — balance is an emergent task-specific capability

Balance is best retained as a functional outcome of postural-control processes:

> the ability to maintain, attain or restore a controlled body state / center-of-mass relationship relative to the base of support under a defined task and environment.

It depends on interaction among:

- visual information;
- vestibular information;
- somatosensory/proprioceptive information;
- central sensory integration/reweighting;
- anticipatory control;
- reactive control;
- neuromuscular force/coordination;
- biomechanical constraints;
- cognition/attention when task demands require it.

The current 2026 integrative review (PMID 41976890) explicitly treats balance as an emergent outcome of distributed postural-control processes rather than a single isolated system.

The 2018 systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID 30323769) is closure-critical: correlations among static, dynamic, proactive and reactive balance types were generally **small**, supporting domain/task specificity rather than one global “balance ability”.

Canonical rule:

**balance is multidimensional and task specific; one test or one sensory condition cannot represent total balance capacity.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 4. Visual contribution

Vision provides information about:

- body orientation relative to the environment;
- optic flow;
- environmental motion;
- upcoming terrain/obstacles;
- self-motion relative to visual references.

Removing or degrading vision can increase sway, but the effect depends on:

- surface reliability;
- stance;
- age;
- task;
- availability/reliability of vestibular and somatosensory inputs.

The 2025 sensory-afference systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID 41314405) found significant effects of visual deprivation on multiple posturographic domains in healthy older adults, with some effects also present in younger adults.

The 2024 RCT in healthy young adults (PMID 39562772) showed that training with systematic visual-input manipulation altered visual sensory contribution and improved an untrained balance task more than conventional balance training in that protocol.

Canonical rule:

**vision is an important balance input, but its weight is context dependent and trainable; eyes-closed performance does not isolate a single “nonvisual balance system”.**

Confidence: **HIGH for contribution / MODERATE-HIGH for trainable reweighting.**

---

## 5. Vestibular contribution

The vestibular system contributes information about:

- head angular/linear motion;
- orientation relative to gravity;
- self-motion when visual/somatosensory cues are limited, unreliable or conflicting.

Vestibular contribution cannot be interpreted as a fixed percentage of balance.

The 2025 light-touch/vestibular-evoked study (PMID 39625307) demonstrated that adding reliable tactile/somatosensory information changed vestibular-evoked balance responses, directly supporting sensory reweighting.

Current balance literature therefore supports:

- vestibular input matters;
- its effective weighting depends on other available sensory cues;
- vestibular challenge is not equivalent to simply closing the eyes.

Canonical rule:

**vestibular control operates inside multisensory integration; “eyes closed = vestibular test” is an invalid simplification.**

Confidence: **HIGH for contribution/reweighting; MODERATE for exact contribution in any specific field test.**

---

## 6. Somatosensory contribution

Somatosensory balance information includes:

- plantar/foot contact;
- cutaneous input;
- muscle/joint proprioception;
- body-support interaction.

On a firm, reliable surface, somatosensory information is often highly informative for upright stance.

When support-surface information becomes unreliable (e.g. foam/moving surface), the system must reweight other modalities.

PMID 41314405 found substantial balance effects when proprioceptive/somatosensory information was experimentally disturbed in healthy older adults.

The 2026 healthy-adult study PMID 42704458 found increased sway and prefrontal activity when vision was occluded and the support surface was unstable, consistent with greater sensory/attentional demand under degraded inputs.

Canonical rule:

**somatosensory contribution is major but context dependent; foam/unstable-surface performance reflects multisensory reweighting, not “proprioception alone”.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 7. Sensory integration and reweighting

The three major sensory streams are not simply added with fixed weights.

The system changes relative reliance based on:

- availability;
- reliability;
- congruence/conflict;
- movement velocity;
- task;
- prior exposure/training.

Current direct evidence shows reweighting when:

- vision is manipulated;
- support-surface information is altered;
- light touch is added;
- vestibular information is perturbed.

Project model:

`visual + vestibular + somatosensory evidence`
→
`context-dependent weighting / state estimate`
→
`postural control strategy`.

Canonical rule:

**balance is a multisensory estimation/control problem, not three independent balance subsystems.**

## 8. Static balance

Static balance generally refers to maintaining a controlled body position with a stationary base of support, for example:

- quiet bipedal stance;
- tandem stance;
- single-leg stance.

Common outcomes include:

- time maintained;
- errors;
- COP path/velocity/amplitude;
- instrumented sway measures.

Important boundary:

**static balance is one domain, not a general balance score.**

PMID 30323769 found only small associations between static balance and other balance types.

A person can therefore perform well in quiet single-leg stance and still have mediocre:

- dynamic;
- proactive;
- reactive

balance.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 9. Dynamic balance

Dynamic balance involves maintaining or restoring stability during:

- locomotion;
- transitions;
- reaching/limits-of-stability tasks;
- tasks where center of mass and/or base of support changes.

The 2024 systematic review of dynamic/functional balance tests (PMID 38699672) shows that different tests measure different functional demands and have different validity/reliability properties.

The 2026 athlete meta-analysis (PMID 42975096) found only a **small** association between dynamic-balance test performance and change-of-direction time overall, with substantial heterogeneity and direction-specific moderation.

Canonical rule:

**dynamic balance is task specific; a Y-Balance/SEBT/LOS result is not a universal proxy for every moving balance demand or athletic performance outcome.**

Confidence: **HIGH for task specificity; MODERATE for transfer to specific sport outcomes.**

---

## 10. Static vs dynamic balance are not interchangeable

The old binary static/dynamic distinction is useful but incomplete.

A more useful project taxonomy is:

1. static steady-state;
2. dynamic steady-state;
3. proactive/anticipatory;
4. reactive.

PMID 30323769 found small correlations among balance types across the lifespan.

The new 2026 network meta-analysis (PMID 42600321) also treats global, static, dynamic, proactive and reactive balance as separable domains and found exercise-modality effects differing by domain.

Canonical rule:

**if a downstream decision depends on a specific balance demand, test and train that demand rather than substituting another balance domain.**

---

## 11. Postural sway

Postural sway is the continuous small movement used while maintaining upright posture.

Instrumented measures commonly use center of pressure (COP), including:

- path length;
- velocity;
- area;
- RMS/amplitude;
- frequency-domain features.

Important boundaries:

### 11.1 COP ≠ COM

COP is the point of application of the ground-reaction force and is a control-related mechanical variable.

It is not identical to:

- center of mass;
- whole-body balance;
- neural control quality.

### 11.2 Less sway is not universally better

PMID 41458879 is closure-critical.

Under height-induced postural threat in healthy people:

- sway amplitude decreased;
- sway frequency increased;
- dynamic gait velocity decreased.

This was interpreted as a protective/stiffening strategy.

Therefore a reduction in sway amplitude can coexist with:

- higher-frequency corrections;
- greater stiffness/caution;
- slower movement.

### 11.3 Metric specificity

The 2026 normative study (PMID 42531757) provides test/platform-specific reference data and shows age/BMI effects.

The 2026 wearable-sensor validation review further shows that some sway variables correlate strongly with force-plate measures while others do not and agreement is not guaranteed.

Canonical rule:

**postural sway is a family of measurement outcomes; direction of change must be interpreted by task, metric and strategy rather than “less = better”.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 12. Reactive balance

Reactive balance is the ability to recover/maintain stability after an **unexpected** perturbation.

Examples:

- slip;
- trip;
- unexpected platform translation;
- unexpected waist pull;
- mechanical perturbation during gait/stance.

Reactive balance relies heavily on:

- rapid sensory feedback;
- automatic/postural responses;
- stepping or change-of-support strategies when required;
- available strength/power and mechanical constraints.

Perturbation-based balance training is the most direct training paradigm.

The 2026 systematic review/meta-regression (PMID 42102008) found substantial heterogeneity and **low certainty** for reactive-balance control and very low certainty for real-world falls when analyzing training-parameter relationships; optimal program characteristics remain unclear.

The 2026 scoping review (PMID 42594763) similarly found large methodological variation, many laboratory-based and single-session paradigms, and inconsistent follow-up.

Canonical rule:

**reactive balance is perturbation-specific and cannot be inferred reliably from quiet stance alone; training effects are real but protocol-specific and optimal dosing is not established.**

Confidence: **HIGH for construct / MODERATE for training-effect generalization / LOW-MODERATE for optimal dose.**

---

## 13. Anticipatory / proactive balance

Proactive balance is preparation for a **predicted** postural challenge.

Examples:

- preparing for a known surface change;
- gait adjustment before a predictable perturbation;
- shifting center of mass before changing base of support;
- postural preparation for a voluntary action.

Anticipatory postural adjustments (APAs) are one mechanism of proactive balance, but the constructs are not identical.

Proactive control can include:

- altered posture;
- altered step length;
- altered muscle activation/coactivation;
- changed COM position;
- changed mechanical strategy before the event.

Current walking-perturbation evidence shows proactive changes before a known challenge and reactive changes after perturbation onset.

Canonical rule:

**proactive/anticipatory balance uses feedforward preparation; reactive balance uses feedback-dominant compensation after unexpected disturbance.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 14. Balance domains are weakly coupled rather than one general ability

This is one of the most important closure boundaries.

PMID 30323769 found only small correlations among balance types.

The older specificity meta-analysis (PMID 26993132) found:

- large improvement in trained balance tasks;
- little or no improvement in non-trained balance tasks.

The new 2026 unstable-surface meta-analysis similarly reports markedly larger gains for tasks resembling unstable-surface training than for stable-ground laboratory measures.

Therefore:

`better at one balance task`
does not imply
`better balance everywhere`.

Canonical rule:

**balance adaptation shows substantial task specificity.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 15. Transfer from balance training to strength tasks

Direct transfer from balance training to maximal strength is limited.

The balance-training performance review (PMID 20617915) found:

- balance/postural-control improvements;
- conflicting or absent strength effects;
- strength/plyometric training often performed as well or better for strength/power outcomes.

The 2025 athlete instability-training meta-analysis (PMID 41229270) found clear balance improvements but limited jump-performance transfer and no significant improvement in countermovement jump.

Thus balance training can improve balance and sometimes related motor-performance outcomes, but:

**balance training is not a substitute for specific resistance training when maximal strength is the target.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 16. Unstable-surface resistance training vs stable resistance training

This distinction prevents a common fitness inference:

> instability increases “stabilizer activation” → therefore instability training produces superior strength.

Evidence does not support that as a universal chronic rule.

The 2015 systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID 26359066) found:

- unstable-surface strength training can improve strength/power/balance versus no training;
- compared with stable-surface strength training, additional benefits were limited/inconsistent, particularly in adolescents/young adults.

The 2026 older-adult meta-analysis (PMID 42468010) similarly found no significant short-term lower-limb strength advantage of unstable over stable resistance training.

Therefore:

**instability can be useful when the target itself includes balance/instability, but stable resistance training remains the more direct route for maximizing force production under stable strength tasks.**

This is a transfer/specificity conclusion, not a prohibition on unstable exercises.

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 17. Reverse transfer: resistance training can improve balance

Transfer is not one-way.

The resistance-exercise systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID 33203156) found improvements in several balance outcomes after resistance exercise in adults/older adults.

This is plausible because balance tasks also require:

- sufficient force;
- rate of force development;
- joint control;
- functional capacity.

However:

`resistance training improves some balance outcomes`
does not imply
`strength alone explains balance`.

Sensory integration, anticipatory/reactive control and task-specific coordination remain independent constraints.

Project rule:

**strength can support balance capacity, but balance remains a sensorimotor task rather than a pure strength test.**

## 18. Neuromotor-fitness interpretation

For project use, “neuromotor fitness” should not be treated as one hidden neural score.

It can include task families such as:

- balance;
- coordination;
- agility;
- gait/locomotor control;
- proprioceptive/sensorimotor challenge.

This topic owns balance specifically.

Downstream testing should avoid constructing one composite “neuromotor” number unless:

- component tests are defined;
- weighting is justified;
- intended decision is explicit.

Canonical rule:

**neuromotor fitness is an umbrella category; balance remains multidimensional within it.**

## 19. Measurement / interpretation boundaries

### 19.1 Single-leg stance

Useful for a defined static task.

Not a universal balance score.

### 19.2 Eyes open vs eyes closed

Useful sensory manipulation.

Does not isolate vestibular function.

### 19.3 Foam / unstable surface

Changes somatosensory reliability and task mechanics.

Does not measure “proprioception alone”.

### 19.4 Y-Balance / SEBT

Dynamic reach tests requiring:

- balance;
- mobility;
- strength;
- anthropometry;
- coordination.

Do not interpret as a pure neural balance measure.

### 19.5 COP metrics

Different COP variables describe different aspects of sway.

Lower values are not universally better.

### 19.6 Balance test batteries

Can broaden domain coverage, but one total score can conceal domain-specific weakness.

### 19.7 Perturbation tests

Best matched to reactive balance, but transfer depends on perturbation type/direction/intensity and context.

## 20. Clinical boundary — Area 01 / MED

This PASS covers **fitness balance in otherwise healthy people**.

It does not diagnose:

- vestibular hypofunction;
- BPPV;
- Ménière disease;
- vestibular migraine;
- cerebellar/brainstem disease;
- stroke/TIA;
- neuropathy;
- sensory ataxia;
- medication-related imbalance;
- cardiovascular/syncope causes;
- persistent postural-perceptual dizziness;
- other clinical balance disorders.

Current clinical guidance supports immediate/urgent medical evaluation for combinations such as:

- sudden-onset persistent vertigo/dizziness with marked gait unsteadiness;
- new focal neurological deficits;
- inability to stand/walk unaided;
- direction-changing or vertical nystagmus in an appropriate acute syndrome;
- new unilateral hearing loss with acute vestibular symptoms;
- syncope/chest pain/arrhythmia-type warning features through the appropriate pathway.

NICE NG127 specifically treats sudden acute vestibular syndrome with gait unsteadiness as a potential stroke presentation requiring trained HINTS assessment or urgent stroke-pathway referral when benign explanations do not account for the syndrome.

AAO-HNS also treats persistent dizziness/postural instability from vestibular/CNS pathology as a clinical vestibular-rehabilitation domain.

Project rule:

**fitness balance tests can identify performance limitations; they do not determine the medical cause of dizziness, vertigo, unexplained falls or pathological imbalance.**

Clinical diagnosis and referral remain MED.

## 21. Practical downstream rules

1. **Do not use one balance test as a global balance score.**
2. **Treat static, dynamic, proactive and reactive balance as distinct but related task families.**
3. **Do not interpret eyes-closed stance as an isolated vestibular test.**
4. **Do not interpret foam stance as “pure proprioception”.**
5. **Use sensory manipulations to challenge reweighting, not to assign fixed sensory percentages.**
6. **Do not assume lower postural sway is always better.**
7. **Name the exact sway/COP metric before interpreting change.**
8. **Use perturbation tasks when the target is reactive balance.**
9. **Use predictable/self-initiated challenges when the target is proactive balance.**
10. **Expect substantial task specificity in balance training.**
11. **Do not use generic balance work as a substitute for resistance training when maximal strength is the target.**
12. **Do not assume unstable-surface resistance training is superior to stable resistance training for strength.**
13. **Allow strength training to contribute to balance without reducing balance to strength.**
14. **For unexplained dizziness/vertigo/falls or neurological/vestibular red flags, move the question to MED rather than “train balance harder”.**

## 23. Confidence / unresolved issues

### HIGH

- balance depends on integrated visual, vestibular and somatosensory information;
- sensory weighting is context dependent;
- balance is multidimensional/task specific;
- static balance is not interchangeable with dynamic/proactive/reactive balance;
- postural sway metrics require construct-specific interpretation;
- reactive and proactive balance are distinct control demands;
- balance training strongly favors trained/similar balance tasks;

### MODERATE-HIGH

- generic balance training has limited transfer to maximal strength;
- unstable-surface RT has no established universal strength advantage over stable RT;

- degree of transfer from one balance task to another;
- transfer from balance training to jump/agility/sport performance;
- exact value of a given sway metric for individual performance decisions;
- optimal perturbation-training dose;

- one universal balance test;
- one fixed visual/vestibular/somatosensory weighting;
- eyes closed as a pure vestibular assessment;
