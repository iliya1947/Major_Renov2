## 3. Forecast contract
A prediction must specify unit/actor, observable event or measured outcome, observation window, horizon, context, eligibility/opportunity, forecast issue time, available evidence, criterion for resolution, comparator, and model/version. Distinguish prediction of an action from inferred feelings, intention, causal mechanism and another person's autonomous choice. An unobservable motive has no independently adjudicable binary outcome merely because a later action is consistent with it. Record conditional and unconditional events separately: a conditional forecast under a specified opportunity is not automatically falsified when that opportunity never arises. An unconditional event that was demonstrably absent may be a negative outcome. Missing information, unobservable event, ambiguous resolution and true non-occurrence must not be conflated.

Freeze forecast and evidence before outcomes; preserve original and revised versions with timestamps and rationale. Resolve by prespecified rules, not by reinterpretation after seeing results. Avoid selective inclusion of successful forecasts, dependence among overlapping windows and duplicate reports, and hindsight leakage. For numeric forecasting, use probabilities only when defensible; qualitative uncertainty or explicitly conditional scenarios are appropriate otherwise. Verbal labels do not establish numerical calibration. Forecasts with no observable resolution criterion cannot be honestly scored.

## 4. Metrics and their limits
For binary resolved y in {0,1} and forecast p, Brier loss=(p-y)^2 (single-event binary convention, not doubled multicategory convention), log loss= -[y log(p)+(1-y)log(1-p)]. Lower loss is better. Expected Brier loss under true event probability q is q(1-q)+(p-q)^2, minimized at p=q. Log loss likewise favors the true probability in expectation under the stated ideal probability model; a zero probability assigned to a realized event incurs infinite loss. This is not permission to manufacture extreme or precise probabilities. For continuous outcomes use explicitly chosen errors/interval scores suitable for the target (e.g. MAE for point forecasts and coverage/width for intervals), not binary metrics.

Calibration concerns agreement between stated probabilities and event frequencies in a defined relevant collection of forecasts. Mean calibration alone is insufficient; subgroup/conditional calibration and uncertainty matter. Discrimination concerns separation/ranking of events and nonevents; high discrimination can coexist with overconfidence. Resolution concerns differences in conditional event frequencies across forecast categories. A constant base-rate forecast can be well calibrated yet have no discrimination. Accuracy at a chosen threshold discards probability information; its utility depends on actual costs and context. Decision utility differs from all these metrics. One correct prediction is not evidence of calibration, and a single surprising result need not establish a defective probabilistic forecast. Compare repeated prospective outcomes at the same target/horizon and relevant population; no implied independent evidence from repeated dependent predictions.

Use a pre-specified feasible benchmark: historical base rate estimated only from information available at issue time, persistence for continuous states, or another simple adequate model. Compare like targets/horizons and evaluation samples. Random row splits can leak future data or same-person/shared-episode information; use chronological evaluation for within-person forecasts and grouped splits for new-person generalization. Fit/tune/recalibrate using designated data, reserve untouched later data for testing. Temporal change, selection, censoring, measurement and data sparsity may invalidate comparisons. No invented calibration curve, success percentage or personal likelihood.

## 6. Operational reasoning and revision
1. Define an observable forecast target and a relevant outcome-resolution rule; record issue time, horizon, context and opportunity.
2. Separate facts, reports, interpretation, explanatory hypotheses, predictions and decisions. Compare competing models and the simplest adequate baseline.
3. Use legitimate available evidence only. Track provenance, dependence, missingness and alternative causal/contextual accounts; do not invent probabilities, manipulate situations or monitor private activity.
4. If sufficient data and a defensible probability model exist, use proper scoring, calibration, discrimination and uncertainty; evaluate chronological holdouts against baseline. If not, use bounded conditional predictions without claims of measured calibration.
5. Record unresolved and excluded outcomes with reasons, preserve all versions and avoid selective success counts. Update the narrowest supported claim and propagate consequences of revisions.
6. Assess predictive performance separately from causal identification and decision utility. Explicit choices, autonomy and safety are constraints; forecast disagreement does not override them.
7. Reserve integration across constructs, contexts and levels and dependency-aware revision for SYS-03.2/.3. No private individual forecast or calibrated model created here.
