# PSY-RES-08 — Qualitative evidence and mixed-methods use in psychological questions

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Establish when qualitative and mixed-methods evidence is appropriate in psychological research, what claims it can support, how methodological rigor should be evaluated, and how qualitative findings should be integrated with quantitative evidence.

The core objective is to avoid two opposite errors:

`qualitative evidence = anecdote`

and

`participant narrative / theme = causal proof`.

Qualitative evidence can be rigorous scientific evidence when the question, design, data generation, analysis, reflexivity, and claims are coherent. It answers different kinds of questions from many quantitative designs.

## 1. Qualitative and quantitative evidence answer different primary questions

### Qualitative evidence is especially suited to questions such as:

- What is the nature of an experience?
- How do people interpret an event, relationship, symptom, intervention, or identity?
- What meanings and categories do participants use?
- What processes appear to unfold over time?
- How is a phenomenon shaped by context?
- What barriers, facilitators, expectations, or implementation processes are present?
- What candidate mechanisms or theoretical constructs emerge from data?
- Which dimensions of a phenomenon may be missing from existing measures?

### Quantitative evidence is especially suited to questions such as:

- How frequent is a phenomenon in a defined population?
- How large is an association/effect?
- How precise is an estimate?
- Does a measure have specified psychometric properties?
- Does an intervention cause an outcome under an identified design?
- How accurately can an outcome be predicted/classified?

Neither family is globally superior.

The relevant question is:

> Which form of evidence is capable of answering the claim being made?

## 2. Qualitative finding is not merely an anecdote

An anecdote is an isolated narrative or observation without a systematic research process.

A qualitative study can involve:

- explicit research questions;
- purposive/theoretical sampling;
- systematic data generation;
- documented analytic procedures;
- comparison across cases;
- iterative analysis;
- reflexivity;
- search for variation and disconfirming material;
- transparent grounding of findings in data;
- methodological auditability.

Therefore:

`non-numeric evidence != anecdotal evidence`.

The distinction depends on method, not whether the data are expressed as numbers.

## 3. Major qualitative approaches answer different kinds of questions

Do not treat "qualitative research" as one uniform design.

### 3.1 Thematic analysis

Useful for identifying and organizing patterned meanings/themes across a data set.

Can be conducted under different epistemological assumptions.

A theme is an analytic construction supported by patterned data, not merely a frequently used word.

### 3.2 Grounded theory

Used to develop explanatory theoretical models/processes grounded in systematically collected and analyzed data.

Common features can include:

- iterative collection and analysis;
- constant comparison;
- theoretical sampling;
- category development.

Do not call any inductive coding exercise "grounded theory."

### 3.3 Phenomenological approaches

Focus on how phenomena are experienced and given meaning.

Approaches differ substantially in philosophical commitments and analytic procedures.

### 3.4 Narrative approaches

Examine how people organize experience through stories, temporal structure, identity, plot, or discourse.

The unit of interpretation may be the narrative as a whole rather than isolated coded statements.

### 3.5 Discourse / discursive approaches

Examine how language constructs actions, identities, categories, power relations, or social realities.

These approaches do not necessarily treat statements as transparent reports of internal mental states.

### 3.6 Ethnographic approaches

Study practices, meanings, interactions, and cultural/social organization in context, often through prolonged observation and participation.

### 3.7 Case study

Provides intensive contextual analysis of a bounded case, potentially integrating multiple sources of evidence.

Case study is not equivalent to a casual anecdotal example.

### 3.8 Qualitative content analysis

Systematically organizes textual or other qualitative material into categories/meanings.

Depending on the approach, it may be more descriptive or interpretive.

## 4. Methodological integrity is the central qualitative rigor principle

APA qualitative-method guidance recommends evaluating **methodological integrity**, rather than imposing one fixed checklist of procedures on every qualitative tradition.

Methodological integrity has two broad components:

### 4.1 Fidelity to the subject matter

The methods should remain faithful to the phenomenon as conceptualized within the research approach.

Relevant features include:

- **data adequacy** — sufficient richness/diversity for the research aim;
- **perspective management in data collection** — awareness of how researchers influence what is generated;
- **perspective management in analysis** — reflexive handling of interpretive standpoint;
- **groundedness** — findings are demonstrably rooted in data.

### 4.2 Utility in achieving research goals

The procedures should generate findings that meaningfully answer the research question.

Relevant features include:

- contextualization;
- data capable of producing insight;
- meaningful contribution;
- coherence across findings, including explanation of variation or contradictions.

Canonical rule:

**Rigor = coherent fit among question, epistemology, sampling, data generation, analysis, reflexivity, and claim.**

Not:

**rigor = presence of a specific ritual such as member checking or intercoder agreement.**

## 5. Reflexivity is not optional self-disclosure for its own sake

In qualitative research, researchers can influence:

- which questions are asked;
- how participants respond;
- what is noticed;
- coding/category construction;
- interpretation;
- which findings are emphasized.

Reflexivity makes these influences inspectable.

Useful reflexive practices can include:

- positionality statements;
- analytic memos;
- reflexive journals;
- documentation of assumptions;
- discussion of researcher-participant relationships;
- explicit description of how prior expectations shaped or were constrained during analysis.

Reflexivity does **not** eliminate subjectivity.

Its purpose is to make interpretive influence visible, reasoned, and accountable.

## 6. Sampling logic differs from population sampling

Qualitative sampling is often designed for:

- information richness;
- theoretical relevance;
- maximum variation;
- critical cases;
- typical cases;
- deviant/disconfirming cases;
- purposive representation of relevant perspectives.

Therefore a small purposive sample is not automatically methodologically weak.

But it cannot silently support population-frequency claims.

Canonical distinction:

`adequate qualitative sample for understanding variation`

does not imply

`representative sample for estimating prevalence`.

## 7. Sample adequacy is question-dependent

There is no universally valid qualitative sample-size threshold.

Assess adequacy relative to:

- research aim;
- approach to inquiry;
- heterogeneity of the phenomenon;
- richness/depth of data;
- sampling strategy;
- analytic complexity;
- degree of contextual variation;
- level of claim.

APA qualitative guidance emphasizes adequacy and richness rather than a fixed participant number.

## 8. Saturation is not a universal quality requirement

"Saturation" can have different meanings:

- no new codes;
- no new themes;
- theoretical saturation;
- sufficient informational power;
- redundancy within a specified analytic frame.

Some qualitative traditions use saturation centrally.

Others do not.

Therefore:

`study does not report saturation -> invalid`

is not a universal rule.

If saturation is invoked, require:

1. a definition of what saturated;
2. how it was assessed;
3. at what analytic level;
4. why that criterion fits the methodology.

## 9. Interview/focus-group quality depends on interaction

Data from interviews are jointly produced through interaction.

Potential threats include:

- leading questions;
- interviewer expectations;
- social desirability;
- status/power differences;
- poor probing;
- inconsistent interview practice;
- group conformity in focus groups;
- dominance of some participants;
- context-sensitive disclosure.

Therefore transcripts are not raw windows into an uncontaminated inner state.

Interpretation must consider how data were generated.

## 10. Researcher influence can be data, bias, or both

In some paradigms, researcher-participant interaction is treated partly as a source of insight.

In others, it is treated primarily as a source requiring control/reflexive management.

The correct handling depends on the inquiry framework.

Do not import a positivist "observer must disappear" rule into every qualitative approach.

Do not use interpretivism as permission for unconstrained interpretation either.

## 11. Coding is not discovery of objectively pre-existing labels

Codes/categories/themes can be:

- inductive;
- deductive;
- abductive;
- theory-driven;
- semantic;
- latent/interpretive.

Researchers should report:

- units of analysis;
- how codes/categories were developed;
- whether coding framework was a priori or emergent;
- how revisions occurred;
- how disagreements or interpretive diversity were handled where relevant;
- what software was used, if any.

Software assists data organization.

It does not perform methodological reasoning by itself.

## 12. Intercoder reliability is method-dependent

Intercoder agreement can be useful when the analytic goal treats codes as categories that should be applied consistently.

It is not a universal validity criterion for all qualitative methods.

Some reflexive/interpretive approaches treat researcher differences as analytically meaningful rather than as simple error.

Therefore:

`no kappa/intercoder reliability -> invalid qualitative study`

is incorrect.

Ask whether the treatment of multiple analysts is coherent with the chosen methodology.

## 13. Triangulation can strengthen inference but is not automatic validation

Triangulation may compare:

- different participants;
- data sources;
- investigators;
- methods;
- settings;
- theories.

Convergence can strengthen confidence.

Divergence can reveal:

- contextual differences;
- method effects;
- subgroup variation;
- different levels of a phenomenon;
- theoretical inadequacy.

Do not force discordant sources into artificial agreement.

## 14. Member checking is optional, not a universal truth test

Participant feedback can be useful for:

- correcting factual misunderstandings;
- testing resonance;
- gaining additional interpretation;
- identifying disagreement.

But participants do not hold automatic final authority over every analytic interpretation.

A participant may:

- change perspective;
- disagree with a theoretical interpretation;
- interpret the researcher's task differently;
- be unable to evaluate cross-case analytic claims.

Use member checking when methodologically useful, not as a mandatory validity stamp.

## 15. Negative/disconfirming cases are high-value evidence

A strong qualitative analysis should not erase cases that do not fit the dominant pattern.

Disconfirming material can:

- narrow a claim;
- identify boundary conditions;
- reveal subtypes;
- improve theory;
- distinguish rival interpretations.

Canonical rule:

**A finding is stronger when variation and contradiction are explained rather than hidden.**

## 16. Quotes illustrate evidence; they do not replace analysis

Participant quotations can demonstrate grounding.

But:

`compelling quote -> theme proven`

is invalid.

The report should show:

- how the quote relates to the broader data;
- whether contrary cases exist;
- how analytic interpretation was reached;
- whether the quotation is representative, illustrative, exceptional, or disconfirming.

Avoid cherry-picked quotations that support a predetermined narrative.

## 17. Frequency words require caution

Terms such as:

- "most";
- "many";
- "common";
- "rare";
- "typical";

can imply quantitative prevalence.

When a qualitative sample is purposive/nonrepresentative, numerical frequency cannot automatically be generalized to a population.

Counts may still be useful descriptively inside a qualitative dataset if their meaning and sampling limits are explicit.

Canonical rule:

**within-sample occurrence != population prevalence.**

## 18. Qualitative evidence can support process and mechanism hypotheses but not automatically causal identification

Qualitative data can provide strong evidence about:

- perceived mechanisms;
- temporal sequences;
- context;
- decision processes;
- implementation pathways;
- candidate causal structures;
- explanations that require quantitative/experimental testing.

But a participant saying:

> X caused Y

does not establish that X caused Y.

Self-reports of causality can be influenced by:

- hindsight;
- attribution;
- memory reconstruction;
- cultural explanations;
- theory exposure;
- incomplete access to underlying processes.

Therefore:

**qualitative explanation -> candidate mechanism / contextual evidence**

unless the broader research design independently identifies causality.

## 19. Lived experience is evidence about experience

If the research question is:

> How is phenomenon X experienced?

then first-person accounts are directly relevant evidence.

Do not downgrade them merely because they are subjective.

Subjective experience can itself be the target phenomenon.

But retain the boundary:

`evidence about experienced meaning`

is not automatically

`evidence that all external causal beliefs contained in that account are true`.

## 20. Transferability differs from statistical generalization

Qualitative studies often support transfer through sufficiently rich contextual description that allows readers/researchers to judge whether findings may apply in another context.

Assess:

- participant/context similarity;
- phenomenon similarity;
- institutional/cultural setting;
- theoretical relevance;
- boundary conditions.

Do not claim representative population generalization unless the sampling/design supports it.

## 21. Reporting guideline is not a quality certificate

APA JARS–Qual, SRQR, and COREQ improve transparency.

They do not prove methodological integrity merely because all items were reported.

COREQ is specifically designed for interviews and focus groups.

SRQR is broader.

Canonical rule:

`reporting completeness != trustworthiness automatically`.

Use reporting standards to determine whether the study can be appraised.

Use methodological appraisal to judge whether claims are warranted.

## 22. Qualitative critical appraisal should assess methodological congruity

JBI's qualitative appraisal framework asks whether there is congruity among:

- philosophical perspective;
- methodology;
- research question/objectives;
- data collection;
- representation/analysis;
- interpretation.

It also evaluates:

- researcher positioning;
- researcher influence;
- adequate representation of participants' voices;
- ethics;
- whether conclusions flow from analysis/data.

This is preferable to applying a generic numeric "quality score" disconnected from design.

## 23. Qualitative evidence synthesis is a distinct evidence-synthesis method

A qualitative evidence synthesis (QES) systematically combines findings from qualitative studies.

Current Cochrane-Campbell guidance includes methods such as:

- thematic synthesis;
- framework synthesis;
- meta-ethnography;
- meta-aggregation;
- realist and other interpretive approaches.

A QES is not a meta-analysis of numeric effect sizes.

Its purpose can include:

- synthesizing experiences;
- understanding barriers/facilitators;
- identifying mechanisms/processes;
- developing explanatory frameworks;
- explaining implementation or context.

## 24. Confidence in qualitative synthesis findings can be assessed explicitly

GRADE-CERQual evaluates confidence in **individual qualitative review findings** using four components:

1. methodological limitations;
2. coherence;
3. adequacy of data;
4. relevance.

This assesses whether a review finding is a reasonable representation of the phenomenon of interest.

It is not a quality score for the primary studies or review as a whole.

## 25. Mixed methods require a real mixed-method question

A study is not meaningfully mixed methods merely because it contains:

- a questionnaire plus a few quotes;
- numbers plus open-ended comments;
- a qualitative study followed by unrelated quantitative analyses.

Mixed methods require a reason to combine qualitative and quantitative evidence such that integration produces information not obtained from either strand alone.

APA JARS–Mixed explicitly requires justification for:

- why mixed methods are appropriate;
- the specific mixed-methods design;
- the qualitative and quantitative components;
- the added value of integration.

## 26. Core mixed-methods design families

### 26.1 Convergent design

Qualitative and quantitative strands are conducted in roughly parallel and then integrated.

Useful when:

- different forms of evidence address complementary aspects of the same question;
- convergence/divergence itself is informative.

### 26.2 Explanatory sequential design

`quantitative -> qualitative -> integration`

Useful when qualitative follow-up helps explain:

- unexpected quantitative findings;
- subgroup differences;
- mechanisms/processes;
- why an intervention succeeded/failed;
- why measures behaved as they did.

### 26.3 Exploratory sequential design

`qualitative -> quantitative -> integration`

Useful when qualitative work helps:

- define constructs;
- generate items/measures;
- build hypotheses;
- identify relevant variables;
- develop an intervention/model later tested quantitatively.

### 26.4 Embedded / intervention mixed methods

A qualitative strand may be embedded within a quantitative intervention/experimental design to study:

- implementation;
- acceptability;
- fidelity;
- participant experience;
- mechanisms;
- unexpected outcomes.

Mixed methods can also be nested in longitudinal, participatory, implementation, or other designs.

## 27. Integration is the defining operation of mixed methods

Qualitative and quantitative strands can each be rigorous but still fail to form a rigorous mixed-methods study if they are never integrated.

Integration can occur through:

- **connecting** — one strand determines sampling/follow-up for another;
- **building** — findings from one strand shape instruments/interventions/questions in another;
- **merging** — results are directly compared or combined;
- **embedding** — one strand is nested inside another design;
- **joint displays** — qualitative themes and quantitative results are placed in a common analytic representation;
- **integrated interpretation** — final claims explicitly use evidence from both strands.

Canonical rule:

**mixed methods = rigorous qualitative strand + rigorous quantitative strand + defensible integration.**

## 28. Mixed methods do not allow one weak strand to hide behind the other

Evaluate separately:

### Qualitative strand

- methodological integrity;
- sampling/data adequacy;
- reflexivity;
- grounding;
- analytic coherence.

### Quantitative strand

- measurement validity;
- design;
- bias;
- precision;
- causal/predictive identification.

### Integration

- rationale;
- timing/sequence;
- priority;
- integration procedure;
- handling of discordance;
- additional inference produced by mixing.

A strong quantitative study plus weak qualitative interviews is not automatically strong mixed methods.

The reverse is also true.

## 29. Discordance is not failure

If qualitative and quantitative results disagree, possible explanations include:

- different constructs being measured;
- different time scales;
- different populations/subgroups;
- measurement limitations;
- contextual variation;
- selection effects;
- qualitative data revealing mechanisms invisible in averages;
- quantitative data contradicting participants' causal attributions;
- genuine theoretical inconsistency.

Do not average away discordance.

Treat it as evidence requiring explanation.

## 30. Mixed-methods causal claims must preserve causal identification rules

Qualitative evidence can explain context or propose mechanisms around an RCT or quasi-experiment.

But adding interviews does not upgrade an observational association into a causal effect.

Likewise, a randomized effect does not automatically validate every qualitative mechanism proposed by participants.

Keep separate:

- causal effect;
- experienced explanation;
- implementation process;
- hypothesized mechanism.

## 31. Mixed methods are especially useful for individualized psychological modeling

At a generic methodological level, mixed evidence can support richer individual reasoning because:

- quantitative instruments constrain interpretation with standardized comparisons;
- qualitative material preserves meaning, context, chronology, exceptions, and self-understanding;
- repeated observations can identify within-person dynamics;
- contradictions between modalities reveal model weaknesses.

But individual inference still requires the safeguards from PSY-RES-01 through PSY-RES-07.

Qualitative richness does not bypass psychometrics, bias control, clinical boundaries, or transportability.

## 32. Qualitative and Mixed-Methods Gate

Before accepting a qualitative or mixed-methods finding, ask:

### A. Question fit

1. What exact question is being answered?
2. Is qualitative evidence appropriate for that claim?
3. Is the claim about meaning, experience, process, context, prevalence, effect, causality, or prediction?
4. Is the chosen approach coherent with that claim?

### B. Sampling and data

5. How were participants/data sources selected?
6. Why are these sources information-rich for the question?
7. Is relevant diversity/variation represented?
8. Is data adequacy justified rather than assumed from sample size?
9. If saturation is claimed, what exactly was saturated and how?

### C. Data generation

10. How were interviews/observations/documents generated?
11. Could researcher expectations or interaction shape the data materially?
12. Are context and researcher-participant relationships described?

### D. Reflexivity

13. Is researcher standpoint/positionality relevant and reported?
14. How was researcher influence managed or used analytically?
15. Are assumptions visible enough to appraise the interpretation?

### E. Analysis

16. Is the analytic method explicit?
17. Are units of analysis and coding/theme development described?
18. Are findings demonstrably grounded in data?
19. Are negative/disconfirming cases represented?
20. Are alternative interpretations considered?
21. Is intercoder agreement/member checking/triangulation being used appropriately for this methodology rather than ritualistically?

### F. Claim calibration

22. Does the report distinguish participant beliefs from externally verified causal facts?
23. Are within-sample frequencies being mistaken for population prevalence?
24. Are causal claims stronger than the design permits?
25. Are transferability limits explicit?

### G. Mixed methods

26. Why are both qualitative and quantitative strands needed?
27. What design is used: convergent, explanatory sequential, exploratory sequential, embedded, or another justified design?
28. Is each strand independently rigorous?
29. Where does integration occur?
30. What new inference is produced by integration?
31. Are discordant results explained rather than suppressed?
32. Does mixing preserve the causal/measurement limitations of each strand?

### H. Synthesis

33. If this is a qualitative evidence synthesis, was an appropriate synthesis method used?
34. Is confidence in review findings assessed where relevant (e.g., CERQual)?
35. Are synthesis findings grounded in contributing studies and context?

### I. Final judgement

36. What is the strongest supported qualitative/mixed-methods claim?
37. What would be an overreach?
38. Which uncertainty is interpretive, contextual, sampling-related, measurement-related, or causal?
39. What additional evidence would most strongly discriminate among remaining interpretations?

## 33. High-value failure modes

### Failure A — qualitative = anecdotal

Incorrect.

Correct:

Systematic qualitative designs can provide rigorous evidence about meaning, experience, context, and process.

### Failure B — first-person explanation = causal mechanism

Incorrect.

Correct:

First-person accounts are strong evidence about reported experience/meaning, but causal attributions require independent causal support.

### Failure C — small N = bad qualitative research

Incorrect.

Correct:

Adequacy depends on question, design, richness, diversity, and analytic goals.

### Failure D — saturation required everywhere

Incorrect.

Correct:

Saturation is approach-specific and must be defined when used.

### Failure E — member checking = validity proven

Incorrect.

Correct:

Participant feedback is one possible procedure whose utility depends on the methodology.

### Failure F — intercoder kappa = qualitative rigor

Incorrect as a universal rule.

Correct:

Agreement metrics fit some coding paradigms but not all qualitative epistemologies.

### Failure G — compelling quote = theme established

Incorrect.

Correct:

Quotes illustrate grounding; the analytic pattern must be supported across the relevant data.

### Failure H — frequency in purposive sample = prevalence

Incorrect.

Correct:

Qualitative occurrence counts do not automatically estimate population frequencies.

### Failure I — quant + qual = mixed methods

Incorrect.

Correct:

Integration and an explicit mixed-method rationale are required.

### Failure J — qualitative strand explains RCT mechanism automatically

Incorrect.

Correct:

Interviews can generate/support mechanism hypotheses but do not alone establish mediation/causal pathway.

### Failure K — disagreement across methods = one method failed

Incorrect.

Correct:

Discordance may expose construct, context, timing, or theory differences and should be analyzed.

### Failure L — reporting checklist = methodological quality

Incorrect.

Correct:

Reporting standards enable appraisal; they do not certify trustworthiness.

## Canonical working rule

**Match the question to the evidence type -> establish methodological integrity -> preserve context and reflexivity -> ground interpretations in data -> calibrate the claim -> integrate methods only when integration adds information.**

Never:

**"subjective" -> dismiss**

and never:

**"rich narrative" -> treat interpretation as causal fact.**

## Scope note

Qualitative methodologies differ in epistemology and purpose. This block deliberately avoids imposing one universal philosophical framework.

Reporting standards such as JARS–Qual, SRQR, and COREQ support transparency; they are not treated as standalone proof of methodological quality.

GRADE-CERQual is designed for confidence in findings from qualitative evidence syntheses, not for scoring individual primary qualitative studies.
