# Part A — Textual evidence is a distinct evidence class

## 1. JBI recognizes textual evidence as reviewable evidence

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

JBI explicitly treats three related but distinct textual-evidence types:

1. **Narrative**
2. **Expert opinion**
3. **Policy / consensus-guideline text**

These are not merely failed versions of empirical research.

They can contribute when the question concerns:
- practice guidance;
- policy direction;
- professional judgement;
- stakeholder experience/account;
- normative or procedural recommendations.

Project consequence:
textual evidence deserves its own question type and source-specific appraisal.

---

## 2. Textual evidence and empirical research answer different claim types

### Project methodological distinction grounded in JBI
**Confidence: HIGH**

### Empirical effect question
> Does intervention A improve outcome Y versus B?

Best answered by empirical designs capable of estimating that effect.

### Textual/policy question
> What do professional policies recommend?
> What rationale is offered for practice X?
> What procedural principles govern situation Y?

These may legitimately require:
- policy;
- consensus guidance;
- expert opinion;
- narrative evidence.

Project rule:
do not use expert opinion as though it were a randomized estimate of treatment effect.

Likewise, do not dismiss policy text merely because it does not estimate an effect when the question is actually normative/procedural.

---

# Part B — Three textual evidence types

## 3. Narrative

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

JBI uses narrative to refer to accounts of:
- experience;
- perspective;
- events;
from patients, professionals or other legitimate stakeholders.

Narrative evidence is especially context-dependent.

Project relevance:
it may be useful when the question concerns:
- lived experience;
- historical/practice accounts;
- stakeholder perspective not captured in formal qualitative research.

But:
a narrative account is not automatically evidence of prevalence, causal effect or effectiveness.

---

## 4. Expert opinion

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Expert opinion draws on:
- knowledge;
- experience;
- professional judgement;
and may also reference external evidence.

JBI appraisal focuses on issues such as:
- identity/standing of the source;
- relevant population focus;
- logical defence of the argument;
- use of extant literature;
- handling of disagreement with literature.

Project consequence:
“expert said so” is not self-validating.

Authority must not substitute for:
- logic;
- source transparency;
- consistency with empirical evidence.

---

## 5. Policy / consensus guideline

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

JBI treats policy as deliberate principles/statements intended to guide:
- decisions;
- procedures;
- action.

Policy/consensus texts may embed external evidence, but the document itself remains a textual evidence source whose:
- authority;
- purpose;
- evidentiary support;
- logic;
- context
must be appraised.

Project consequence:
a guideline recommendation is not equivalent to the raw empirical evidence supporting it.

This distinction connects later to RES-20 guideline appraisal.

---

# Part C — When textual evidence is appropriate

## 6. Textual evidence is appropriate when the question itself is textual/normative/procedural

Examples:

1. What do professional bodies recommend regarding scope of practice?
2. What policies govern return-to-training after a specified event?
3. What expert rationale exists where empirical evidence is absent?
4. What narratives describe a practice phenomenon not well represented in formal research?
5. What consensus positions exist on a contested procedural issue?

These are legitimate textual-review questions.

---

## 7. Textual evidence can complement, but not silently replace, empirical evidence

### Established finding / project rule
**Confidence: HIGH**

A decision may require both:

### Empirical stream
What happens?

### Textual/policy stream
What is recommended/allowed/considered acceptable?

Example:
a professional-practice question may require:
- empirical safety evidence;
- professional scope-of-practice policy.

These streams should remain separately labeled.

Do not merge:
> “the guideline recommends X”

with
> “X has proven efficacy”

unless empirical evidence independently supports the latter.

---

## 8. “Best available evidence” does not erase evidence-type hierarchy by claim

### Project rule
**Confidence: HIGH**

When no direct empirical research exists, expert/policy evidence may be the best available evidence for a practical question.

But wording must remain proportional:

Correct:
> Current professional guidance recommends X.

Incorrect:
> X is scientifically proven effective.

The type of claim must match the type of evidence.

# Part D — Question framing

## 9. JBI commonly uses PICo for textual evidence reviews

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

JBI textual-evidence guidance commonly frames questions with:

1. **P — Population**
2. **I — Intervention / phenomenon of interest**
3. **Co — Context**

The question should also make clear that it concerns textual evidence.

Project consequence:
PICo is a suitable default for textual-evidence questions, but the source type itself must also be explicit.

---

## 10. Source type is part of the review question/eligibility

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

JBI requires clarity about whether the review includes:

1. narrative only;
2. expert opinion only;
3. policy/consensus only;
4. any combination of the three.

JBI describes two valid strategies:

### Restricted
Pre-specify one or more textual types.

### Inclusive
Include all relevant textual types if the question warrants them.

Whichever route is chosen must be justified.

---

## 11. Source type should follow the question

### Project rule grounded in JBI
**Confidence: HIGH**

Do not choose:
> “expert opinion only”

because it is easier to search.

Ask:
> Which textual source types can actually answer the question?

Examples:

### Scope-of-practice question
Likely:
- policy;
- professional guideline;
- regulatory/professional statements.

### Lived-experience narrative question
Likely:
- narrative.

### Expert rationale under evidence scarcity
Likely:
- expert opinion.

---

## 12. Context is often essential

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Textual claims may vary strongly by:
- jurisdiction;
- profession;
- healthcare/training setting;
- organization;
- historical period;
- local practice.

Project consequence:
policy text from one jurisdiction cannot automatically be treated as governing another.

For professional-practice questions, context/jurisdiction may be a mandatory eligibility field.

---

# Part E — Primary and secondary questions

## 13. A textual evidence review normally has a primary question

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

JBI allows:
- one primary question;
- optional subquestions when needed to examine attributes of:
  - population;
  - context;
  - phenomenon.

Project rule:
do not multiply subquestions unless they materially structure the review.

---

# Part F — Distinguish textual evidence from qualitative research

## 14. Narrative text is not automatically qualitative research

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

JBI explicitly distinguishes:
- qualitative research;
from
- narrative / expert opinion / policy textual evidence.

Project consequence:
a first-person essay, position paper or professional opinion should not be included in a qualitative-research synthesis merely because it contains words rather than numbers.

Question type and appraisal method must match the source.

---

## 15. Policy/guideline documents require different appraisal from primary research

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

JBI evaluates policy/consensus text on dimensions such as:
- authority;
- purpose;
- relevance to intended recipients;
- evidence support;
- logic.

Project consequence:
do not apply an RCT risk-of-bias tool to policy text.

Conversely, do not use a policy-appraisal tool to claim certainty about an empirical treatment effect.

---

# Part G — Potential conflicts between textual sources

## 16. Disagreement between policies/opinions is itself review information

### Established finding / project consequence
**Confidence: HIGH**

Textual evidence may contain:
- competing recommendations;
- conflicting expert positions;
- different jurisdictional standards.

Project rule:
do not resolve disagreement by:
- counting documents;
- choosing the most prestigious organization automatically.

Instead record:
1. source type;
2. authority/context;
3. underlying evidence;
4. logic;
5. population;
6. date/version;
7. reason for divergence where identifiable.

Detailed conflict-resolution methodology remains later.

---

# Part H — Versioning matters strongly for policy/guideline text

## 17. Current version is part of source identity

### Project rule grounded in source type
**Confidence: HIGH**

For policy/guideline evidence record:

1. issuing body;
2. document title;
3. jurisdiction/scope;
4. publication/update date;
5. version;
6. superseded status;
7. whether recommendation is current.

A superseded guideline can be useful historically but should not be presented as current policy.

# Part I — Fitness-project use

## 18. Likely legitimate uses

In this project textual evidence may matter particularly for:

1. professional scope and coaching practice;
2. safety/procedural recommendations;
3. return-to-activity guidance;
4. policy/consensus definitions;
5. professional competency expectations;
6. situations where empirical evidence cannot directly answer a normative question.

---

## 19. Likely illegitimate substitution

Do not use textual evidence alone to establish:

1. hypertrophy superiority;
2. strength superiority;
3. dose-response magnitude;
4. injury causation;
5. prevalence/incidence;
6. measurement validity;
7. prognosis.

Those require empirical evidence appropriate to those claim types.

Textual sources may summarize or interpret that evidence, but remain a secondary/normative layer.

---

# Part J — Project Textual Evidence Question Module

Use only when question type = **TEXTUAL / POLICY / EXPERT EVIDENCE**.

1. **Decision/use**
2. **Why empirical evidence alone cannot answer this question**
3. **Population / relevant actor**
4. **Phenomenon / intervention / issue**
5. **Context / setting**
6. **Jurisdiction**, if relevant
7. **Textual evidence type(s)**
   - narrative;
   - expert opinion;
   - policy;
   - consensus guideline;
   - combination.
8. **Why these textual types are eligible**
9. **Primary question**
10. **Subquestions**, if necessary
11. **Eligible issuing/source bodies**
12. **Version/date requirements**
13. **Superseded-document handling**
14. **Need to extract underlying empirical citations?**
15. **Expected claim type**
   - normative;
   - procedural;
   - descriptive;
   - experiential;
   - expert rationale.
16. **Claims this review is NOT allowed to make**
17. **Conflict-handling plan**
18. **Separate empirical evidence stream required? YES/NO**

# Part K — Architecture decision

## 20. Textual evidence remains a conditional module

Do not add:
- policy fields;
- expert credentials;
- jurisdiction
to every Question Card.

Router:

### Effect / causal / prognosis / measurement / frequency
→ empirical question module.

### Policy / professional guidance / expert rationale / narrative textual question
→ Textual Evidence module.

### Decision requires both
→ parallel labeled evidence streams, integrated only at decision stage.

---

## 21. Practical rules produced by PASS-09D

1. Treat narrative, expert opinion and policy as distinct textual evidence types.
2. Do not treat textual evidence as failed empirical research.
3. Match evidence type to claim type.
4. Use empirical designs for empirical effect/causal claims.
5. Use textual evidence for normative, procedural, policy, expert-rationale or narrative questions where appropriate.
6. PICo is a suitable default for textual question framing.
7. State eligible textual source types explicitly.
8. Justify restricted vs inclusive textual-source eligibility.
9. Keep context/jurisdiction explicit where material.
10. Distinguish narrative evidence from formal qualitative research.
11. Appraise textual sources using source-appropriate criteria.
12. Do not convert guideline recommendations into empirical efficacy claims.
13. Treat conflicts between policies/opinions as evidence to analyze, not vote-count.
14. Track policy/guideline version and supersession.
15. Add a conditional Textual Evidence Question Module to the modular QUESTION_CARD.
16. When a decision needs both empirical and textual evidence, keep the evidence streams separately labeled until decision synthesis.
