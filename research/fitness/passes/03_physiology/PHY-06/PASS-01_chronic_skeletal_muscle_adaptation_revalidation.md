## 3. Hypertrophy

Repeated resistance training can increase skeletal-muscle size.

Valid whole-muscle/fiber outcomes include:

- muscle volume;
- anatomical cross-sectional area;
- muscle thickness;
- fiber cross-sectional area.

The 2026 ACSM overview of 137 systematic reviews confirms resistance training as an effective intervention for muscle hypertrophy in healthy adults.

Important boundaries:

1. early post-training increases can contain edema/swelling;
2. one ultrasound site does not represent whole-muscle growth;
3. fiber CSA and whole-muscle size are related but not identical;
4. regional growth can be nonuniform;
5. group-average hypertrophy does not guarantee equal individual response.

Project rule:

**hypertrophy is retained tissue enlargement, not acute swelling.**

Detailed molecular mechanisms → PHY-07.

---

## 4. Time course of hypertrophy

There is no single exact week when “hypertrophy starts.”

Detectability depends on:

- training status;
- program;
- muscle;
- measurement method;
- measurement error;
- edema control;
- sampling schedule.

Older time-course work showed that early CSA increases can be confounded by edema and that clearer retained hypertrophy becomes detectable after several weeks.

A 2026 within-subject 12-week trial found both progressive-load and nonprogressive-load legs increased mCSA by week 12; the progressive condition maintained high relative effort and showed greater late-phase growth from sessions 18 to 24.

Canonical rule:

**hypertrophy develops over repeated exposures and is detectable on a weeks-to-months scale, but exact onset is measurement- and protocol-dependent.**

No universal “week 3” or “week 6” rule is justified.

---

## 5. Physiological cross-sectional area

PCSA is conceptually linked to the amount of contractile tissue arranged in parallel and therefore to force capacity.

In simplified terms, PCSA depends on:

- muscle volume;
- fascicle length;
- architecture.

Resistance training can increase PCSA, but this is not automatic if muscle volume and fascicle length change proportionally.

Human studies show:

- PCSA can increase after resistance training;
- specific tension / force per PCSA can also change;
- PCSA estimates depend strongly on measurement assumptions.

Project rule:

**anatomical CSA, PCSA and specific tension are distinct constructs.**

A larger muscle can gain force through:

- more contractile tissue in parallel;
- neural changes;
- architectural changes;
- changes in specific tension;
- combinations of these.

Detailed strength integration → PHY-08.

---

## 6. Fascicle length

Fascicle length can change with training, but magnitude and direction are protocol-, muscle-, region- and method-dependent.

Evidence is not uniform:

- some longitudinal RT studies report increased fascicle length;
- some meta-analytic datasets show little or no pooled change;
- eccentric/lengthened-position or specific sport protocols may produce larger changes in some muscles;
- ultrasound field-of-view and extrapolation can materially affect estimates.

The 2024 team-sport meta-analysis found resistance training increased muscle thickness and pennation angle overall but did not show an overall fascicle-length increase.

Project rule:

**do not assume resistance training universally adds fascicle length.**

Fascicle length is one architectural adaptation, not a mandatory hypertrophy marker.

---

## 7. Pennation angle

Pennation angle can increase with resistance training, particularly as pennate muscles hypertrophy, but responses vary by:

- muscle;
- region;
- program;
- measurement technique;
- training status.

Current meta-analytic evidence supports possible/modest increases in some populations, while other syntheses show uncertain or small effects.

Project rule:

**pennation angle can remodel with hypertrophy but is not a universal or sufficient marker of adaptation quality.**

---

## 8. Muscle quality

“Muscle quality” is not one universal biological variable.

Common operational definitions include:

- strength per unit muscle size/mass;
- ultrasound echo intensity;
- CT/MRI attenuation or intramuscular adipose indices;
- phase angle;
- specific tension.

These constructs are related but not interchangeable.

Resistance training can improve some muscle-quality metrics, but changes are:

- measure-specific;
- population-specific;
- muscle-specific.

Recent older-adult data show improvements in some echo-intensity and strength outcomes while other sites/metrics did not change.

Project rule:

**always state how muscle quality is defined before interpreting change.**

---

## 9. Capillarization

Skeletal-muscle capillarization is plastic.

Resistance training can increase capillary supply in some populations and protocols.

Examples:

- 12 weeks of resistance training increased capillary contacts and capillary-to-fiber perimeter exchange in older men;
- in young men, capillary-to-fiber ratio changes were detectable early during a 12-week RT program.

However, capillarization must be interpreted carefully:

- capillaries per fiber/contact can rise;
- capillary density per tissue area can stay unchanged or fall if fiber size expands faster than capillary growth;
- responses depend on age, baseline fitness and training modality.

Broader exercise meta-analysis shows endurance/HIT/SIT consistently stimulate mitochondrial/capillary growth, with lower initial fitness associated with larger percentage adaptations.

Project rule:

**capillary supply and capillary density are not interchangeable.**

Resistance training does not automatically create the same angiogenic phenotype as endurance training.

---

## 10. Mitochondrial apparatus

Resistance training can remodel skeletal-muscle mitochondria, but the response is not adequately described by “mitochondria increase” or “mitochondria decrease.”

Possible adaptations include changes in:

- respiratory capacity;
- electron-transport-chain proteins;
- fusion/fission proteins;
- mitochondrial content;
- relative mitochondrial volume density;
- redox handling.

Human studies show:

- improved mitochondrial respiratory capacity after some RT programs;
- increased electron-transport-chain proteins/remodeling markers in older adults;
- other low-volume protocols showing little change in oxidative capacity;
- hypertrophy can reduce relative mitochondrial volume density through a dilution effect even if absolute mitochondrial function/content is maintained or improved.

Project rule:

**mitochondrial function and mitochondrial volume density are distinct outcomes.**

RT mitochondrial adaptation is protocol/population dependent and generally less consistently oxidative than classic endurance training.

---

## 11. Enzymatic profile

Chronic training can alter skeletal-muscle enzyme activity.

Resistance training studies report changes in:

- hexokinase;
- lactate dehydrogenase;
- citrate synthase;
- β-HAD;
- other glycolytic/oxidative enzymes,

but findings are not uniform across programs.

Examples:

- some 12-week RT studies report increases in oxidative enzyme activities;
- others find modest or no changes in citrate synthase/oxidative capacity;
- human work also supports remodeling of glycolytic enzyme activity after RT.

Canonical rule:

**enzyme adaptation is pathway- and protocol-specific; resistance training does not impose one universal “more glycolytic” or “more oxidative” enzyme profile.**

Detailed bioenergetics → BIO.

---

## 12. Substrate stores

Repeated resistance training can alter resting intramuscular energy/substrate stores.

Evidence supports possible increases in:

- muscle glycogen;
- phosphocreatine/creatine pool;
- ATP-related high-energy phosphate stores in some protocols.

A classic human detraining study showed glycogen content was higher in the trained state than after 90 days of detraining.

Older direct biopsy studies also report increased resting glycogen and phosphagen stores after heavy resistance training.

However:

- exact magnitude is protocol/nutrition dependent;
- glycogen changes are highly affected by dietary carbohydrate and timing;
- acute depletion must not be confused with chronic storage adaptation.

Project rule:

**substrate-store adaptation is real but not a fixed universal magnitude and cannot be interpreted without nutrition/recovery context.**

Detailed substrate metabolism → BIO/NUT.

Confidence: **MODERATE** for exact RT-specific magnitude.

---

## 13. Extracellular matrix

The skeletal-muscle extracellular matrix is an active remodeling compartment.

Repeated mechanical loading can change:

- collagen turnover;
- matrix-related gene expression;
- adhesion/structural proteins;
- force-transmission environment.

Current 2026 integrative human analysis identifies ECM-remodeling signatures across endurance and resistance training, with resistance training associated with structural maturation/reinforcement signatures.

Acute resistance exercise also increases collagen-remodeling activity, establishing that ECM responds to loading.

But:

**acute collagen turnover ≠ proof of a specific chronic ECM architecture.**

Direct chronic human structural quantification is less mature than evidence for muscle-fiber hypertrophy.

Project rule:

**ECM adapts with training, but exact collagen quantity/crosslinking/architecture outcomes require tissue- and protocol-specific evidence.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for remodeling existence; MODERATE for exact chronic structural phenotype.**

---

## 14. Increased resistance to exercise-induced damage

Repeated exposure to damaging/eccentric exercise produces the **repeated-bout effect**.

Current 2026 meta-analysis supports reduced subsequent:

- MVC loss;
- DOMS;
- CK response.

This adaptation can appear after an initial exposure and persist across weeks, although exact protection depends on interval and outcome.

Likely contributors span:

- mechanical;
- cellular;
- neural;
- connective-tissue/remodeling factors.

Project rule:

**greater resistance to damage is a chronic protective adaptation distinct from hypertrophy.**

Reduced soreness/damage after repeated training does not mean the muscle stopped adapting.

---

## 15. Regional adaptation

Hypertrophy and architecture can be nonuniform across:

- proximal vs mid vs distal regions;
- different heads within a muscle group;
- superficial/deep regions;
- muscles contributing to the same exercise.

A 2024 methodological review emphasizes that one measurement site cannot establish whole-muscle adaptation.

A 2025 meta-analysis of longer vs shorter mean muscle-length RT found only trivial regional differences overall, with a small distal trend and high probability of practical equivalence across sites.

Therefore:

**regional adaptation is real as a phenomenon, but not every programming manipulation creates a large or predictable regional hypertrophy advantage.**

Project rule:

**one site does not fit all, and regionality must be measured directly rather than assumed from exercise sensation or anatomy alone.**

## 16. Time-course integration

Different adaptations operate on different clocks.

### Very early / first exposures
Can appear rapidly:
- repeated-bout protection;
- early angiogenic/capillary signaling;
- neural/skill changes (owned mainly by NEU/PHY-08).

### Several weeks
Can become measurable:
- retained muscle-size increase;
- some PCSA/architecture changes;
- capillary remodeling;
- enzyme/metabolic remodeling;
- mitochondrial functional changes.

### Months / longer
Can continue accumulating:
- hypertrophy;
- architecture;
- ECM remodeling;
- substrate/metabolic phenotype;
- tissue-specific structural adaptation.

The exact sequence is not fixed.

Canonical rule:

**there is no single skeletal-muscle “adaptation time.”**

Each outcome requires its own measurement schedule and error model.

## 19. Established project-level model

Chronic skeletal-muscle adaptation is multidimensional:

`repeated training exposure → retained remodeling across fiber size + PCSA/architecture + microvasculature + mitochondria/metabolic enzymes + substrate stores + ECM + damage resistance`

These dimensions:

- do not change in lockstep;
- can have different directions;
- can use different time scales;
- depend on training modality/dose;
- depend on baseline state;
- require distinct measurements.

Confidence:

- **HIGH** for hypertrophy and multi-compartment plasticity;
- **HIGH** for repeated-bout protection;
- **MODERATE-HIGH** for architecture/capillarization/mitochondrial remodeling as context-dependent adaptations;
- **MODERATE** for exact substrate-store/ECM/enzymatic magnitude under generic RT.

## 20. Training-relevant implications

1. **Muscle adaptation is not synonymous with hypertrophy.**
2. **A larger muscle is not defined only by one CSA/thickness site.**
3. **PCSA, fascicle length and pennation angle are distinct architectural variables.**
4. **Muscle quality requires an explicit metric definition.**
5. **Capillary supply can adapt even during hypertrophy; capillary density alone can mislead.**
6. **RT can remodel mitochondrial function, but relative mitochondrial volume may be diluted by fiber hypertrophy.**
7. **Metabolic enzyme and substrate adaptations are protocol/nutrition dependent.**
8. **ECM remodels with loading but is less directly quantified than fiber hypertrophy.**
9. **Repeated exposure increases damage resistance; lower later DOMS is expected adaptation, not evidence of ineffective training.**
10. **Regional hypertrophy exists, so one measurement site cannot represent an entire muscle.**
11. **Different adaptations require different monitoring time scales.**
12. **No acute marker from PHY-05 substitutes for measuring these retained chronic outcomes.**
