# 2. First canonical distinction: participant vs partner

A person can be involved in research in fundamentally different roles.

## Research participant / subject

Provides study data or receives study procedures:

- completes questionnaire;
- gives interview;
- wears accelerometer;
- performs exercise intervention;
- undergoes testing.

## Research partner / contributor

Influences the research itself:

- helps define priorities/questions;
- co-develops methods;
- decides what outcomes matter;
- recruits or collects data;
- participates in analysis/interpretation;
- co-designs intervention;
- shares governance;
- participates in dissemination;
- may share authorship/ownership.

### Critical rule

Taking part in a study is not equivalent to participatory research.

A trial with many participants can have zero meaningful participatory governance.

Conversely, a small co-design group can have high decision-making power.

---

# 3. Participation is a dimension, not one atomic design

Participatory research is best treated as an architecture of:

- governance;
- knowledge production;
- decision rights;
- action;
- power sharing.

It can coexist with:

- qualitative interviews;
- surveys;
- RCTs;
- case studies;
- implementation studies;
- mixed methods;
- intervention development.

### Canonical representation

parent scientific design
+
participatory governance architecture
+
data methods
+
action/design process
+
power/ownership metadata.

Example:

mixed-method feasibility study
+
co-designed intervention
+
patient research partners
+
single-arm pilot.

Do not collapse all of this into:

> design = participatory.

---

# 4. Participation spectrum

Current literature uses overlapping terminologies.

A pragmatic project spectrum is:

1. participation as study subject only;
2. consultation;
3. repeated/advisory involvement;
4. collaboration;
5. co-design/co-development;
6. co-production/shared governance;
7. community/user-led research.

This is not a universal scale and should not be used as a mechanical quality score.

### Critical rule

Higher nominal involvement is not automatically better for every research question.

But the label must match actual decision rights.

---

# 5. Patient/public involvement vs participation vs engagement

Current UK/NIHR convention distinguishes:

## Participation

People take part in research procedures.

## Involvement

Research is carried out with/by the public rather than merely to/about/for them.

## Engagement

Information/knowledge about research is shared with broader audiences.

### Project rule

Do not use PPI/PPIE as a synonym for study participation.

Record:

- subject participation;
- research involvement;
- public engagement;

separately.

---

# 6. Action research

Action research links inquiry with deliberate change in practice.

Canonical cyclical structure:

planning
→ action
→ observation
→ reflection
→ revised planning
→ next cycle.

Data collection and analysis are iterative and feed the next action.

### Scientific target

Not only:

> understand the problem.

Also:

> change the local practice/system while learning from that change.

### Rule

A one-time needs assessment followed by an intervention does not automatically constitute action research.

The iterative action-reflection cycle must actually influence subsequent action.

---

# 7. Action research is not one specific data method

Action research can use:

- interviews;
- focus groups;
- observation;
- surveys;
- routine metrics;
- audits;
- diaries;
- workshops;
- experiments;
- combinations.

Its distinguishing feature is the iterative inquiry-action architecture.

---

# 8. Participatory Action Research (PAR)

PAR adds a stronger participatory and emancipatory commitment to action research.

Canonical elements:

1. people affected by the problem are co-inquirers rather than only subjects;
2. reflection and knowledge production are collective;
3. action is part of the research process;
4. cycles repeat;
5. local knowledge is treated as legitimate knowledge;
6. power and inequity are explicit concerns;
7. the process often aims at empowerment/social change.

### PAR cycle

problem/priorities identified collaboratively
→ gather/interpret information
→ plan action
→ act
→ observe consequences
→ reflect collectively
→ revise action.

### Critical rule

Action research can be practitioner-led with modest stakeholder participation.

PAR requires meaningful participation in the inquiry/action process.

---

# 9. PAR and empowerment

Empowerment is often an explicit PAR aspiration.

But it should not be assumed merely from the label.

Extract:

- who chose the question?;
- who controlled meetings?;
- who interpreted data?;
- who could veto decisions?;
- who controlled resources?;
- who owned outputs?;
- whether community priorities changed the project?;
- whether power actually shifted.

### Rule

Claiming empowerment requires evidence about process and consequences.

---

# 10. Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR)

CBPR is a participatory research approach centered on a community as a unit of identity and on long-term equitable partnership.

Stable principles across Israel-derived and current CDC/NIH guidance include:

1. recognize community as a unit of identity;
2. build on community strengths/resources;
3. collaborative/equitable partnership across research phases;
4. co-learning and capacity building;
5. balance knowledge generation with action/benefit;
6. address locally relevant problems;
7. use cyclical/iterative processes;
8. share/disseminate findings with partners;
9. attend to sustainability/long-term relationship;
10. address power and social inequalities.

Current CDC/NIH guidance further emphasizes:

- trustworthiness;
- community self-determination;
- willingness of institutions/researchers to release control.

---

# 11. Community is not merely a recruitment pool

CBPR "community" can be:

- geographic;
- identity-based;
- disease/health-condition based;
- professional;
- social network;
- shared-interest group.

### Critical rule

Recruiting many people from one neighborhood does not make a study CBPR.

The community must function as a partner in knowledge/action, not merely as a source of subjects.

---

# 12. CBPR vs community-based research

## Community-based

Research occurs in a community setting.

## Community-engaged

Community has some meaningful involvement.

## CBPR

Requires a stronger equitable partnership and participatory research process.

### Example

Researchers run an exercise survey in a community center:
→ community-based.

Community advisory panel helps adapt recruitment:
→ community-engaged / involvement.

Community organization co-selects the research question, co-designs intervention, interprets results and shares governance:
→ CBPR-compatible architecture.

---

# 13. CBPR vs PAR

They overlap strongly.

## PAR

Usually foregrounds:
- action/reflection cycles;
- participation;
- transformation/emancipation.

## CBPR

Usually foregrounds:
- community partnership;
- equitable governance;
- community strengths;
- local relevance;
- capacity;
- sustainability.

A project may legitimately be both.

### Project rule

Record both when both are explicitly implemented rather than forcing one label.

---

# 14. Co-design

Co-design involves stakeholders collaboratively developing or redesigning an intervention, service, tool, program or process.

Core logic:

understand needs/experience
→ define problem/priorities
→ generate ideas
→ prototype/develop
→ refine/test
→ iterate.

### Important current finding

A 2025 systematic review found major inconsistency in how public-health studies define and implement co-design.

Some studies calling themselves co-design involved participants only as informants.

### Critical rule

The label "co-designed" is not evidence of substantive co-design.

Extract actual roles and decisions.

---

# 15. Co-design vs consultation

## Consultation

Stakeholders provide views/feedback.

Researchers retain primary decision control.

## Co-design

Stakeholders materially participate in creating/refining the solution.

### Example

Researchers present a finished exercise app and ask participants whether they like it:
→ usability/consultation.

Users choose core functions, generate content, evaluate prototypes and influence redesign:
→ co-design.

### Rule

Feedback after the major design decisions are already fixed should not be upgraded to full co-design.

---

# 16. Prototype/iteration logic

Many design-oriented co-design processes include:

- ideation;
- prototyping;
- testing;
- iterative refinement.

However, the 2025 systematic review shows prototypes are not consistently present across published "co-design" studies.

### Project interpretation

Prototype/testing strengthens a product/intervention co-design claim where relevant, but no single prototype ritual universally defines co-design.

More important is:

- shared design work;
- influence on decisions;
- transparent iteration.

---

# 17. Experience-Based Co-Design (EBCD)

EBCD specifically uses patient/service-user and staff experience as the basis for service redesign.

Foundational EBCD logic includes:

1. understand service experience;
2. identify key "touchpoints";
3. bring service users and staff together;
4. agree improvement priorities;
5. co-design changes;
6. implement/review changes.

Original versions commonly used narrative/filmed interviews.

### Critical distinction

EBCD is not merely:

> interview patients about satisfaction.

The experiences must feed actual joint design/change.

---

# 18. Evidence-informed Experience-Based Co-Design (E2CD)

A 2024 framework extends EBCD by explicitly integrating:

- lived experience;
- research evidence;
- joint decision-making.

People with lived experience are intended to remain central across priority setting and design.

### Project value

This is a useful example of:

evidence synthesis
+
participatory design
+
lived-experience governance.

---

# 19. Co-production

Co-production generally implies broader/deeper shared power than task-specific consultation.

NIHR framing:

researchers, practitioners and public work together while sharing power and responsibility from start to end, including knowledge generation.

Stable principles include:

- sharing power;
- including diverse perspectives/skills;
- respecting different forms of knowledge;
- reciprocity;
- relationship building;
- joint ownership.

### Critical rule

Co-production should not be used as an ornamental synonym for "stakeholders were consulted".

---

# 20. Co-design vs co-production

Terminology is inconsistent and overlapping.

A useful project distinction:

## Co-design

Primary object:
- collaboratively designing a product/service/intervention/process.

Can occur in a bounded design phase.

## Co-production

Primary architecture:
- shared power/responsibility across a broader research or service-production process.

Can include co-design but often extends to:

- question formation;
- governance;
- analysis;
- dissemination;
- ownership.

### Caveat

This is a pragmatic routing distinction, not a universally standardized definition.

Current literature explicitly reports conceptual ambiguity.

---

# 21. Co-creation boundary

Co-creation is frequently used as an umbrella concept for collaborative creation.

Some sources distinguish co-creation, co-design and co-production by:

- timing;
- breadth;
- stakeholder role;
- power.

Others use them interchangeably.

### Project rule

Do not infer architecture from terminology alone.

Always extract:

- who was involved;
- when;
- in what decisions;
- with what power.

---

# 22. Governance and decision rights

For participatory studies, extract who controls:

1. research question;
2. funding/budget;
3. protocol;
4. recruitment;
5. data ownership/access;
6. analysis;
7. interpretation;
8. intervention design;
9. implementation;
10. dissemination;
11. authorship;
12. continuation/termination.

### Critical rule

Participation quality is more visible in decision rights than in the number of workshops.

---

# 23. Power sharing is not necessarily equal task sharing

A partnership can be equitable without every person doing identical work.

Community partners may not want to:

- run statistics;
- manage ethics documents;
- draft manuscripts.

Equity is better assessed through:

- meaningful influence;
- respected expertise;
- access to resources;
- compensation;
- transparency;
- choice;
- ability to challenge decisions.

### Rule

Equal ≠ identical.

---

# 24. Ownership and authorship

Participatory claims become stronger when reporting addresses:

- who owns data;
- who controls access;
- who can approve interpretation;
- authorship/co-authorship;
- acknowledgement;
- intellectual property;
- community consent for dissemination;
- return of results.

CBPR especially treats dissemination as a partnership process rather than extraction of knowledge by researchers.

---

# 25. Compensation and resources

Meaningful involvement requires resources.

Potential equity issues:

- unpaid lived-experience labor;
- travel/access barriers;
- childcare;
- digital exclusion;
- inaccessible meeting times;
- professional jargon;
- training imbalance;
- researchers controlling all funds.

### Project extraction

Record:

- compensation;
- accessibility supports;
- training/capacity building;
- budget control;
- time burden.

---

# 26. Tokenism

Tokenistic involvement can look participatory while preserving all important researcher control.

Red flags:

- one "representative" included late;
- no influence on decisions;
- feedback repeatedly ignored;
- no explanation of what changed;
- community involvement only after protocol fixed;
- no compensation/support;
- "co-design" workshop with predetermined outputs.

### Rule

Number of stakeholders does not measure participatory depth.

---

# 27. Researcher role

Participatory researchers may act as:

- facilitator;
- co-inquirer;
- technical expert;
- evaluator;
- organizer;
- broker;
- advocate.

These roles can conflict.

### Reflexive questions

- Does the researcher dominate because of expertise/funding?
- Can stakeholders disagree safely?
- Are institutional constraints transparent?
- Who defines what counts as valid knowledge?
- Who decides when consensus has been achieved?

---

# 28. Consensus is not always required

Participatory research can reveal legitimate conflict.

Examples:

- clinicians prioritize safety;
- patients prioritize autonomy;
- funders prioritize scalability;
- coaches prioritize performance;
- community members prioritize access.

### Critical rule

Co-design should not erase disagreement merely to produce one solution.

Document:

- dissent;
- trade-offs;
- unresolved priorities;
- whose view prevailed and why.

---

# 29. Participation and qualitative data are separate axes

Participatory research is often qualitative-heavy but not intrinsically qualitative.

A participatory project may use:

- surveys;
- accelerometry;
- trials;
- administrative data;
- qualitative interviews;
- mixed methods.

### Example

A co-designed physical-activity program can later be evaluated in:

- RCT;
- feasibility trial;
- single-arm pilot;
- cohort;
- implementation study.

The fact that intervention was co-designed does not determine the evaluation design.

---

# 30. Intervention-development vs intervention-effect evidence

Co-design can establish:

- stakeholder priorities;
- acceptability;
- contextual fit;
- design requirements;
- feasible implementation features.

It does not establish:

- efficacy;
- effectiveness;
- causal effect;

unless a separate evaluation design supports those claims.

### Critical example

A co-designed exercise intervention being acceptable to older adults does not prove it prevents falls.

That requires outcome evaluation.

---

# 31. Embedded evaluation

Participatory design and evaluation can be embedded in a larger program.

Possible architecture:

co-design phase
→ feasibility study
→ pilot
→ RCT
→ implementation evaluation.

### Project rule

Each phase should retain its own design label and inferential claims.

Do not allow:

> intervention was co-designed

to upgrade evidence from:

> uncontrolled pilot

into causal efficacy evidence.

---

# 32. Participatory evaluation

Stakeholders can participate in evaluating:

- implementation;
- acceptability;
- process;
- outcomes;
- interpretation.

Participatory evaluation is different from:

> researchers evaluate a participatory intervention.

The participatory component concerns who shapes the evaluation itself.

---

# 33. GRIPP2 role

GRIPP2 is a reporting framework for patient/public involvement.

It helps record:

- aims of involvement;
- methods;
- stages;
- results/impact;
- reflections;
- limitations.

### Critical rule

GRIPP2 is not a study-design classification or proof of high-quality involvement.

It is a reporting aid.

---

# 34. Ethics in participatory research

Current NIH CBPR guidance highlights ethical issues beyond individual consent:

- community harms/benefits;
- collective trust;
- power;
- governance;
- fair distribution;
- historical exploitation;
- community expertise.

### Important distinction

Community partnership does not replace:

- individual informed consent where required;
- privacy;
- research ethics review.

Community approval and individual consent are different ethical layers.

---

# 35. CBPR and community self-determination

Current CDC principles explicitly state that self-determination belongs to communities; an external body does not "give" a community power to act in its own interest.

### Project implication

Avoid language such as:

> researchers empowered the community

unless the specific claim is justified.

Prefer:

- supported capacity;
- shared control;
- reduced barriers;
- created decision opportunities;

when that is what actually occurred.

# 36. Fitness/exercise crosswalk

| Question / structure | Correct routing |
|---|---|
| Older adults help choose components of a post-falls exercise-maintenance program | co-design |
| Older adults only rate a finished program | consultation/usability, not full co-design |
| Women from culturally diverse communities co-create local physical-activity programs with local government | participatory co-design |
| Kindergarten staff iteratively co-design and test physical-activity practices in their workplaces | co-design + design-based/iterative research |
| Dialysis patients/carers help adapt an exercise intervention before a later cRCT | co-design/development phase; cRCT remains separate |
| Autistic adults help create program, followed by a single-arm feasibility trial | co-designed intervention + feasibility design |
| Community chooses PA research priorities and shares governance, analysis and dissemination | CBPR-compatible architecture |
| Coaches and athletes iteratively plan, implement, observe and revise a training-system change | action research candidate |
| Athletes are interviewed about a new program but do not influence it | qualitative participation only |
| Stakeholders attend one workshop after all major decisions are fixed | consultation, not strong co-production |
| Exercise program is labelled "community-based" because it occurs in a local center | not automatically CBPR |
| Co-designed program improves outcome in uncontrolled pre/post data | co-design does not establish causal effect |

# 37. Current exercise exemplar: older-adult exercise maintenance

A 2026 study co-designed an exercise-maintenance intervention for older adults leaving falls-prevention programs.

It used:

- community stakeholder consultation;
- professional stakeholder consultation;
- implementation-oriented development.

co-design is the intervention-development architecture.

It does not itself establish effectiveness of the final intervention.

The intervention remains subject to later feasibility/effectiveness evaluation.

|---|---|
| study participant vs research partner | one provides data; the other influences research |
| participation vs PPI/involvement | taking part is not shared research work |
| engagement vs involvement | dissemination/communication differs from governance |
| community-based vs CBPR | location alone does not create partnership |
| community-engaged vs CBPR | engagement can be shallower than equitable all-phase partnership |
| action research vs any iterative project | inquiry-action-reflection cycle is required |
| PAR vs action research | PAR adds meaningful participatory/power orientation |
| PAR vs qualitative study | PAR can use quantitative/mixed evidence |

| consultation vs co-design | feedback does not equal shared creation |
| co-design vs co-production | co-design may be bounded to design; co-production usually broader power sharing |
| co-design vs co-creation | terminology overlaps; actual roles must be extracted |

| number of workshops vs depth of participation | decision rights matter more |
| stakeholder presence vs power sharing | presence can be tokenistic |
| equal partnership vs identical tasks | equity does not require identical work |

| participatory evaluation vs evaluation of participatory program | governance of evaluation differs |

| community approval vs individual consent | distinct ethical layers |
| empowerment label vs demonstrated power shift | outcome must be evidenced |

# 43. Confidence

## HIGH

Well-supported:

- participation as a research subject differs from involvement as a partner;
- action research uses iterative planning-action-observation-reflection cycles;
- PAR adds participatory/action/power commitments;
- CBPR emphasizes community identity, equitable partnership, co-learning, local relevance, action and sustainability;
- co-design involves stakeholders in actual design rather than feedback alone;
- co-production emphasizes shared power/responsibility;
- participatory architectures can coexist with qualitative, quantitative, trial and mixed-method designs;
- co-designed interventions require separate evaluation for efficacy/effectiveness;
- GRIPP2 is reporting guidance rather than a design taxonomy.

## MODERATE-HIGH

Project routing choices:

- participation spectrum;
- practical co-design vs co-production boundary;
- thresholds for labeling involvement as tokenistic vs substantive;
- unified extraction router.

Reason:

current literature repeatedly documents inconsistent terminology across PPI, co-design, co-creation, co-production, participatory research, PAR and CBPR.
