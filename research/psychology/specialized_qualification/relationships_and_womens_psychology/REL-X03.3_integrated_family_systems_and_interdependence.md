E1. Kluwer, Karremans, Riedijk & Knee (2020), Autonomy in Relatedness: How Need Fulfillment Interacts in Close Relationships. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167219867964 ; https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/autonomy-in-relatedness-how-need-fulfillment-interacts-in-close-r/ . Complete institutional abstract inspected; indexed publisher passages inspected. Three studies (N=388, 241, 220); association of relatedness need fulfillment with accommodation was stronger or present only at higher reported autonomy, depending on study. This supports examining both dimensions jointly, not treating proximity as the opposite of autonomous action. Different subscales and study designs can yield different interactions. Full design, coefficients across studies, precision and alternative specifications not independently checked; no intervention effect asserted.

E2. Righetti, Sakaluk, Faure & Impett (2020), The link between sacrifice and relational and personal well-being: A meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000297 ; https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/the-link-between-sacrifice-and-relational-and-personal-well-being/ . Complete institutional abstract directly inspected; PubMed CAPTCHA. Across 82 datasets, 9,547 effect sizes and 32,053 participants, willingness to sacrifice and satisfaction with sacrifice showed positive associations with selected own and partner well-being outcomes; enacted sacrifice had a small negative association with own personal well-being (r=-.07); cost of sacrifice had negative associations with selected own and partner outcomes. Distinct estimands, not a single overall benefit of sacrifice. Pooled coefficients are not person-specific effects, and direction of association may reflect prior relationship quality, selection or reporting. Full moderator, dependence and bias analyses not checked. No conclusion that costly concessions prove commitment or should be increased.

E3. Zhang et al. (2023), Family outcome disparities between sexual minority and heterosexual families: a systematic review and meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjgh-2022-010556 ; https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1567823 . Complete institutional abstract directly inspected; indexed PMC abstract/method and table extracts; direct PMC access blocked. 34 articles in narrative synthesis, 16 in quantitative synthesis. Reported small differences favoring sexual-minority families for child adjustment and parent-child relationship; no significant pooled difference for several other outcomes, including couple satisfaction (SMD=.26, 95% CI -.13 to .64) and parenting stress (.01, -.20 to .22). Null results are not equivalence and uncertainty differs by endpoint. Social support, discrimination, context and nonrandom family selection are relevant; no causal attribution to family structure or prediction about any individual. Exact heterogeneity and study-specific adjustments not independently checked.

E4. Kil, Robichaud & Mageau (2022), Trajectories of coparenting quality across ethnically diverse and interethnic parents. https://doi.org/10.1177/02654075221106997 . Direct publisher abstract, introduction and results excerpt inspected. Study 1 compared 574 interethnic mother-father unions with matched same-ethnicity counterparts in a higher-risk family dataset, reporting less favorable aggregate coparenting trajectories. Study 2 analyzed 1,148 parents from the interethnic sample: estimated trajectory profiles included a stable relatively positive majority (75.5%), decreasing-then-stabilizing (13.1%), and increasing-then-stabilizing (11.4%). These are sample/model-specific profile estimates, not natural types or individual probabilities; not an independent replication of Study 1. Ethnicity is not a psychological mechanism; social context, support, resources, selection and measurement may matter. Fragile Families cohort overlaps potentially with REL-X03.2 sources. Detailed matching, profile uncertainty and causal adjustment not verified. Counterexample to interpreting average group differences as universal individual decline.

E5. Columbus, Molho, Righetti & Balliet (2021 issue), Interdependence and cooperation in daily life. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32584097/ . Indexed original author abstract inspected; direct access CAPTCHA. Two one-week experience-sampling studies: 139 couples (278 individuals, 6,766 situation reports) and 284 individuals (7,248 situation reports). Examines perceived dependence, conflicting interests, power asymmetry and cooperation in everyday encounters. Useful design evidence for sampling situation-specific interdependence rather than reducing a relationship to one power score. Exact findings, effect sizes and uncertainty not independently verified here; no specific direction or causal effect asserted.

E6. Longitudinal relations between coparenting and father engagement in low-income residential and nonresidential father families. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31750693/ . Indexed original abstract inspected; direct access CAPTCHA. Building Strong Families sample of 1,908, cross-lagged models at two time points comparing residential and nonresidential fathers. Concurrent associations of coparenting with caregiving and play in both groups reported. Exact prospective path pattern, estimates and attrition not independently verified, so no causal or longitudinal directional claim. Residence, caregiving engagement and romantic status are distinct. Supports inclusion of nonresident configurations, not a model of all fathers.

E7. Coparenting and Relationship Quality Effects on Father Engagement: Variations by Residence, Romance (2011). https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2011.00834.x . Indexed publisher abstract inspected; DOI open 403. Reported stronger prospective association of perceived coparenting support with later father engagement among nonresidential nonromantic parents than among residential or nonresidential romantic parents. This is outcome- and configuration-specific predictive evidence, not proof of causal effect of separation or a prescription for family decisions. Sample count, adjustment, estimates and precision not independently checked.

E8. Coparenting from multiple perspectives and associations with child and family functioning. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40932756/ . Indexed author abstract inspected; direct access CAPTCHA. 160 dual-earner different-sex parent families with infants, predominantly White and married; parent reports and observational coding. Latent profiles with similarly favorable reports but differing observed coparenting scores showed differing associations with family/child outcomes. Profile solutions are descriptive and sample-specific. Reporter and observed measures can capture different contexts or constructs; disagreement does not by itself prove deception. Exact coefficients, model stability and temporal design not independently verified; supporting measurement evidence only.

## 3. Integrated framework
A family is a set of overlapping relationships embedded in material, developmental and social context. The romantic, coparenting, parent-child and other relational subsystems can share actors and resources but have distinct goals, functions, evaluations and possible transitions. Record family configuration as context, not motive or quality score. System-level stability does not establish individual well-being, desire to remain, fairness or safety.

Represent each decision in its actual domain and time window. Separate:
- actor-specific goals, value and relative priority;
- actual feasible options, external constraints, role duties and resources;
- degree and direction of interdependence, influence and ability to decline;
- voluntary dedication, desire for a bond, felt constraint and objective continuation;
- willingness to accommodate, actual behavior, cost and satisfaction;
- actor-specific reports, perceived responses, observed coordination, individual and shared outcomes.

Candidate multilevel model (not a fitted or identified causal graph): prior histories, values and resources influence role selection and current opportunities; shared and individual stressors may influence appraisals and interactions; actions in one subsystem may be associated with outcomes in another; partner feedback, joint decisions and changed opportunities can alter future expectations and behavior. A shared event can create correlated change without direct partner influence. A person may maintain a valued role while experiencing resource scarcity, changing priorities or relationship-specific dissatisfaction. Multiple goals can be complementary, independent or conflicting depending on actual constraints; do not presume fixed zero-sum competition.

Distinguish within-person spillover, cross-person crossover, shared exposure, assortative selection, actor and partner associations, reciprocal dynamics and measurement dependence. Candidate hypotheses require corresponding evidence. A dyadic coefficient, observational cross-lag, statistical mediation or group trajectory does not by itself identify causation. Keep system structure separate from subjective appraisal and behavior from intention. Voluntary reliance and autonomy are compatible; coercion, harm and the ability to refuse must be assessed separately rather than treated as symmetrical relationship processes.

## 4. Operational integration and revision
1. Define the specific question, actor(s), subsystem, desired outcome and time window. Do not substitute family functioning, continued cooperation or contact for romantic preference or mutual commitment.
2. Record known facts, voluntary statements, observed conduct, timing and legitimately available contextual information separately. Identify unavailable data as unknown; do not infer other family members' private motives.
3. Compare at least the following when relevant: stable goal value with reduced opportunity; changed preference or priority; role expectation or obligation; individual or shared stress; cross-domain or partner influence; voluntary commitment; material/perceived constraint; measurement/reporting difference. More than one may apply.
4. Specify discriminating observations in ordinary comparable contexts: actual change in available opportunity; freely expressed choice; persistence/recovery across time; domain specificity; actor versus partner outcomes. No engineered scarcity, stress, dependence or testing through another family member. If rival models predict the same event, no diagnostic update.
5. Check selection and baseline trajectories, demographic and relationship context, measurement level, reporter, lag, uncertainty and cohort overlap. Distinguish aggregate level from change, subgroup coefficient from moderator test, null from equivalence. Use evidence for its actual population and endpoint.
6. Respect autonomy and each person's ability to decline. Do not exploit family obligations, financial barriers, children, vulnerability or dependence as leverage. System interdependence does not imply equal responsibility for coercion or harm.
7. Update only the supported proposition and specify what remains uncertain. No numerical personal forecasts without calibration. Clinical, legal and safety judgments require separately appropriate evidence and expertise.

## 5. Internal synthetic transfer review
Rubric: construct separation, competing explanation, distinguishing evidence, inference boundary. Critical errors: unsupported motive/diagnosis, group-to-person determinism, causal inflation, ignored contrary evidence, coercion or privacy breach. Six original generic scenarios, not held-out or independent.

A. Someone freely offers to trade work shifts for a partner but declines a more costly change. Response: willingness, actual behavior, cost and autonomy differ; resource limits and preferences can coexist. Compare feasible options and voluntary account. No inference of diminished affection from a declined costly action.
B. A family-structure comparison yields a nonsignificant difference in satisfaction but a significant difference in another outcome. Response: distinct endpoints, uncertainty, selection and context; inspect confidence intervals and compatible measures. No equivalence or individual prediction.
C. A sample has a lower average trajectory but most members belong to a stable positive profile. Response: group comparison and within-group heterogeneity are compatible; profile modeling, selection and measurement matter. Compare uncertainty and subgroup distributions; no individual assignment.
D. Two adults coordinate recurring shared duties despite separate personal plans. Response: joint responsibility and romantic dedication are distinct; routine, values or practical necessity compete. Actual mutually expressed relationship preference is relevant; no inferred transition.
E. Both partners report cooperative behavior, while one observed interaction is less coordinated. Response: subjective appraisal and context-specific behavior differ; selection of observed situation, reporting and observer reliability are alternatives. Repeated independent measures could distinguish. No deception inference.
F. A person with high reported closeness also maintains independent plans. Response: relatedness and autonomy are distinct and compatible; preferences and opportunities shape actions. Voluntarily reported goals and repeated contexts clarify. No assumption of avoidance or causal prescription.

Self-review: 24/24 rubric criteria explicitly addressed across six self-authored responses; no critical error identified in this limited review. This is checklist coverage, not independent competence validation, model retraining or forecast calibration.
