## Evidence
Targeted public searches 2026-11-25: sex differences ideal partner preferences speed dating meta-analysis; worldwide preference matching; romantic love sex differences; autonomous relationship pursuit; longitudinal romantic desire and relationship history among women. Selected primary and synthesis evidence including contrary findings; not systematic or raw-data reanalysis.

E1. Walter et al. (2020), Sex Differences in Mate Preferences Across 45 Countries: A Large-Scale Replication. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620904154 . Publisher indexed abstract and APS journal summary inspected. N=14,399 in 45 countries. Replicated average stated preferences for younger and attractive partners among men and older partners with financial prospects among women, relative to the other group. These are ratings of preferences, not observed choice, causality, universal individual rankings or actual partner characteristics. Full methods, effects and country-level sensitivity not audited.

E2. Eastwick et al. (2014), The predictive validity of ideal partner preferences: a review and meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0036225 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23586697/ . Indexed primary abstract only. 97 studies: attractiveness correlated with romantic evaluations around r=.40 in both sexes, earning prospects around r=.10, sex differences in correlations around .03 and nonsignificant. This qualifies extrapolation from stated preferences to actual evaluations; does not establish equivalence or invalidate stated preferences. Evaluations are not actual partnership choices, and designs and measures vary.

E3. Eastwick et al. (2025 issue), A Worldwide Test of the Predictive Validity of Ideal Partner Preference-Matching. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12622239/ ; PMID39480282. Direct full-text HTML abstract, methods overview, selected results and discussion inspected (including Tables 1/4 excerpts); not end-to-end. Registered report, N=10,358, 43 countries, 22 languages. Distinctive matching across 35 attributes predicts romantic evaluations (standardized beta .19; .11 after including normative matching, normative beta .34); average attribute-level interaction beta .04. Additional relationship-formation subsample showed corrected metric .24 for steadily and newly partnered, .01 for steadily single participants. Stated attractiveness preferences underestimated revealed associations, especially among women; women overestimated earning-potential importance on average. Revealed means associations with perceived partner traits and evaluations here, not experimentally revealed causal utility or actual partner choice. Partners did not supply their own ratings. Common method, sample selection, limited trait coverage and observational design constrain transfer. Nonzero but modest matching effects are compatible with E2's weaker individual-trait matching evidence. Do not infer a woman's preference from group membership or score a person's attractiveness as universally objective.

E4. Baxter et al. (2022), Initial impressions of compatibility and mate value predict later dating and romantic interest. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2206925119 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36322750/ . Indexed primary abstract and publisher excerpts; PMC direct opening failed. Three speed-dating studies, N=559, over 6,600 dates; social relations model distinguished actor general desirousness, consensual partner desirability and unique dyadic liking. These components predicted later evaluations/contact/date outcomes. Unique liking is operational statistical relationship variance, not proof of enduring compatibility or causal mechanism. Follow-up selection, opportunities and model-specific coefficients not fully verified. No verified female-specific moderator. General human relationship formation evidence.

E5. Bode, Luoto & Kavanagh (2025), Sex differences in romantic love: an evolutionary perspective. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13293-025-00698-4 . Publisher HTML selected methods, results (number, timing, intensity, thinking and commitment), discussion and limitations inspected. N=808 ages 18–25, selected English-speaking online respondents in relationships currently in love for at most 23 months, above PLS threshold; predominantly students, multiple orientations. Female participants reported later onset relative to relationship formation (mean 1.92 vs .98 months), slightly greater intensity and more time thinking about loved one; men reported more past episodes of love. Sex added about 1% adjusted variance for frequency/timing/intensity and 4% for repetitive thoughts in specified regressions; commitment difference nonsignificant in adjusted analysis (additional .28%). Timing is retrospective; not direct observation. Authors' evolutionary explanations are theoretical interpretations, not identified causes. Restricted range/selection, country aggregates, recalled onset, overlapping constructs and exploratory multiple testing limit generalization. Biological sex categories and gender identity not identical in sample. Thinking about someone is not established commitment or clinical obsession. Related 2025 cluster paper https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2025.113108 uses 809 participants from same Romantic Love Survey; indexed abstract, direct opening failed. Exploratory clusters are descriptive, not validated lover types or independent replication; no detailed cluster claims.

E6. Why Do You Want a Romantic Relationship? Individual Differences in Motives for Romantic Relationship Pursuit (2025 online). https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672251331699 ; PMID40353532. Indexed primary abstract/excerpts; publisher 403 and PMC verification blocked. SDT-based AMRPS studies N=1,280 and 3,186; greater autonomous motivation among singles associated with partnering six months later. Distinguishes autonomous pursuit, controlled motives, readiness, fear of singlehood and intentions. Does not establish causal effect or partner-specific desire; exact estimates, subgroup moderation and measurement validity not fully audited. General evidence, not female-specific mechanism.

E7. Sisters Are Doin' It for Themselves: Gender Differences in Singles' Well-Being (2024 online). https://doi.org/10.1177/19485506241287960 ; PMID40546788. Indexed publisher abstract/results, direct PMC blocked. N=5,941, 50% men. Women reported higher singlehood satisfaction (absolute d=.30) and lower partner desire (d=.21); other well-being gaps smaller. Average differences within selected samples, not uniform preference. Some samples measured sex rather than gender. Cross-sectional comparisons do not identify causal consequences of partnership or ability to be close to others.
E8. Love as a Low Priority: Gender and Relationship History Differences in Singles' Value of Romantic Partnership (2024). https://doi.org/10.1177/23294965241240781 . Indexed publisher abstract only. US heterosexual singles in DSL-COVID survey; among women, prior committed relationship or marriage associated with greater likelihood of reporting partnership not important at all, relative to no prior romantic relationship. Within-women evidence; not proof that prior relationships cause lower desire. Age, selection, prior experiences and constraints could explain associations. Pandemic setting, exact estimates and methods not audited.
E9. Happily Ever After? Singles' Expectations of Romantic Relationships Are Associated With Singlehood Satisfaction and Future Romantic Outcomes (2026). https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672261438616 ; PMID42032860. Indexed final publisher abstract/results excerpts; PubMed opening failed. Six waves N=5,113. Women reported higher intimacy and negative expectations; within-person higher intimacy expectations associated with more partner desire, negative expectations with less. Some within-person associations with desire stronger for men, most implications similar across genders. Expectations also associated with later partnering and satisfaction. No causal identification; exact estimates, sample overlap with other singles studies, attrition and methods unverified. Distinguish between- and within-person estimates, desire and realized partnership.

Evidence limits: E3/E5 substantive direct text, others primarily indexed abstract/excerpts; no full article read end-to-end, exhaustive correction check or independent validation. E5 related cluster study not independent; E6/E7/E9 potential sample overlap not audited. E8 within-women analysis is abstract-level, not sufficient to establish developmental mechanism. Detailed love theory, commitment and attachment evidence remains scheduled REL-X01/02/03; sexual desire WOM-03.2.

## Operational distinctions and practice
General desire for partnership, attraction to a particular person, affection/caring, experienced intimacy, passionate love, attachment-related expectations, subjective compatibility, intention, willingness/readiness, mutual agreement and sustained commitment are distinct. No assumed fixed sequence. A person may value partnership without wanting a particular partner, or feel attraction without choosing a relationship. Distinguish actual reciprocal behavior from a person's evaluation; attachment is not a label for disinterest. Explicit boundaries govern decisions regardless of hypothesized feelings.
Separate actor tendency, perceived partner attributes, unique interaction and actual dyadic outcomes. Stated ideals may be informative but are imperfect predictors. Consider goal hierarchy, opportunity, expectations, context, prior experience, norms, selection and feedback. Use repeated comparable observations and voluntary reports, rival conditional predictions and revision triggers. Do not infer concealed attraction from refusal or use social pressure or covert testing. Unknown motives remain unknown.
Original generic exercises: (A) Two fictional survey respondents rate an ideal trait equally yet evaluate different dates positively; compatibility and normative desirability are not the same as ideal matching. (B) A woman reports liking a date but says she is not seeking a relationship; attraction and intention can differ, and her stated choice is not overridden by inference. (C) Someone frequently thinks about a partner but commitment scores are unclear; thinking alone cannot establish intent. E3 and E5 counter universal matching and thinking-to-commitment claims; E7/E8/E9 challenge uniform female partnership motivation. Internal reasoning only, not blinded or independently scored.
