## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a defensible cueing framework for later resistance-exercise coaching without:

- treating “one cue only” as a universal law;
- equating brevity with quality;
- assuming more detailed instruction is automatically more useful;
- assuming specificity means prescribing one exact movement geometry;
- restoring a universal external-over-internal hierarchy rejected in NEU-14;
- assuming analogy cues are process-pure implicit learning or universally superior;
- assuming outcome cues are always better than process cues;
- giving cues to visible symptoms when the diagnosed cause differs;
- assigning cue styles from fixed learner typologies;
- keeping cues indefinitely after they stop adding information;
- assuming a universal cognitive-load threshold or cue count;
- importing general ADHD claims into NEU-16 when exact ADHD implementation belongs Area 12 / PSY / COA.

### Primary downstream context

Healthy adult novice/intermediate resistance trainees learning or refining compound and accessory exercise technique.

### Critical outcomes

- immediate task performance;
- movement quality;
- delayed retention;
- transfer;
- ability to execute without the cue;
- cognitive load / working-memory demand;
- task-relevant force/velocity/recruitment where explicitly targeted.

## 2. Operational cue definition

The literature does not use “cue,” “instruction,” “attentional-focus instruction,” and “verbal coaching statement” with perfect consistency.

For this project:

> **Cue** = a concise verbal or symbolic instruction intended to direct attention, action intention, or one task-relevant movement relation before/during an attempt.

This is an operational project definition, not a claim of universal scientific taxonomy.

A cue can be:

- internal;
- external;
- analogy/metaphor;
- outcome-oriented;
- process-oriented;
- body-position oriented;
- action-verb based;
- error-correction oriented.

Cueing is distinct from:

- feedback after the attempt (NEU-15);
- demonstration/modeling (NEU-17);
- diagnosis of why the error occurred (NEU-18).

## 4. One cue vs multiple cues

There is no robust universal RCT evidence establishing:

`one cue > multiple cues`

for all motor skills.

However there is direct evidence that **multiple simultaneous explicit rules can interact negatively with working-memory capacity**.

PMID 28878701:

- children practiced basketball shooting for 240 trials;
- before each practice block they received **five explicit technique instructions**;
- higher-WM children improved;
- lower-WM children showed the opposite learning pattern.

This supports:

**instructional load can exceed the learner's available cognitive resources.**

It does not establish:

- one cue as universally optimal;
- five cues as universally harmful;
- the same threshold in adults or compound lifting.

PMID 26367306 compared:

- analogy instruction;
- “explicit light” with reduced informational load;
- traditional explicit instruction with larger informational load.

No statistically significant overall performance differences were found, but reducing explicit information was consistent with lower rule burden and may mitigate some costs of heavy explicit instruction.

Canonical rule:

**use the minimum number of simultaneous cues needed to solve the current information problem; “one cue at a time” is a practical default, not a biological law.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for overload risk / LOW for universal one-cue optimum**.

## 5. Cue brevity

Cue brevity and cue effectiveness are not equivalent.

A shorter cue can help by reducing:

- verbal working-memory load;
- rehearsal demand;
- execution-time self-monitoring.

But a cue can be too brief to specify the needed relation.

The 2026 trunk-coordination study (PMID 42190132) directly illustrates this:

- a simple directive, “activate your core,” did not significantly change low-back displacement/stiffness;
- detailed bracing coaching did;
- both directive and detailed coaching reduced maximal lifting force acutely.

Therefore:

**shorter is not automatically better, and more detailed is not automatically better.**

Brevity is useful only if the cue still carries enough task-relevant information.

Canonical rule:

**compress information without deleting the relation the learner needs to control.**

Confidence: **MODERATE**.

---

## 6. Cue specificity

Specificity means the cue identifies a task-relevant relation clearly enough to guide action.

It does **not** mean:

- prescribing every joint angle;
- adding more words;
- specifying one universal movement solution.

Evidence that wording detail matters:

### Bench press action verbs

PMID 40602018:

- 26 physically active participants;
- external/internal focus crossed with “push” vs “punch” wording;
- external conditions increased bar velocity vs internal conditions;
- “punch” produced higher bar velocity than “push” independent of focus direction.

Thus semantic/action wording itself can alter acute performance.

### Trunk coordination

PMID 42190132:

- generic “activate your core” was insufficient to modify the targeted coordination;
- detailed bracing coaching altered displacement/stiffness.

Therefore:

**cue specificity should be defined by informational precision, not word count.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for acute performance/coordination effects / LOW for durable lift-learning prescription**.

## 7. External vs internal cue

This item inherits NEU-14 and must not reopen the rejected universal hierarchy.

Current project boundary:

- external focus is often useful;
- reporting-bias-adjusted evidence does not establish universal external superiority;
- internal focus is not universally harmful;
- task information and measured outcome matter.

Recent task-specific counterexamples reinforce this.

PMID 41700657:

- movement-form acquisition task;
- internal-focus group improved reach distance/form more than external-focus group under that protocol.

PMID 42261257:

- internal focus improved adaptation more than external focus when proprioceptive hand information was task relevant.

Resistance-exercise studies also show outcome dependence:

- internal focus can alter muscle recruitment;
- external focus can improve velocity/endurance in some tasks.

Canonical rule:

**choose internal or external cue according to the information needed and outcome being optimized; do not treat focus direction as a universal cue-quality score.**

Confidence: **HIGH against universal hierarchy**.

## 8. Analogy cues

Analogy cues compress multiple movement relations into a metaphor or image.

Example structure:

> “move as if ___.”

Potential advantages:

- lower explicit rule burden;
- integrated representation;
- reduced conscious step-by-step control;
- possible robustness under stress/dual-task conditions.

### Meta-analytic evidence

PMID 39419241:

- 16 randomized controlled trials;
- analogy outperformed control overall, ES ≈ 0.32;
- analogy outperformed explicit instruction overall, ES ≈ 0.29;
- some favorable retention/stress findings;
- high heterogeneity in many comparisons;
- high risk of bias in included studies.

Therefore:

**analogy cueing is promising, but universal superiority is not established.**

### Direct lifting evidence

PMID 41558250:

- 20 novice participants;
- three consecutive days;
- analogy vs explicit vs control in lifting/isometric tasks;
- both instruction types altered lower-limb EMG;
- explicit instruction reduced early RFD relative to control;
- analogy maintained performance more closely.

This is **acute performance/control evidence**, not delayed technique-learning evidence.

Canonical rule:

**use analogy when it efficiently communicates a useful movement relation; do not assume that any metaphor is better than explicit instruction.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for usefulness / MODERATE for superiority claims**.

## 9. Analogy cue ≠ implicit learning

Upstream NEU-12 remains binding.

An analogy is an instructional method.

It does not prove that the learner:

- has no explicit knowledge;
- is unaware of a strategy;
- uses a process-pure implicit mechanism.

The 2024 meta-analysis and 2025 review discuss analogy as a route intended to reduce rule burden, but cognitive mechanism remains incompletely established.

Canonical rule:

**analogy is a cue format, not a process label.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 10. Cue tied to outcome

An outcome-oriented cue directs the learner toward the task effect or success criterion.

Potential examples:

- bar/implement trajectory;
- velocity target;
- landing target;
- pushing the environment;
- reaching a criterion.

The 2025 hex-bar deadlift study (PMID 41037919) is particularly useful:

- 24 experienced participants;
- internal-process, internal-outcome, external-process, external-outcome conditions;
- external-process cue produced the highest velocity relative to internal conditions;
- external-outcome maximized displacement;
- the best cue depended on the task outcome being measured.

Therefore the defensible rule is not:

`outcome cue > process cue`.

It is:

**cue content should align with the task goal that matters.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for acute task-goal alignment**.

---

## 11. Process cue can also be task-relevant

A process cue specifies a relation during movement rather than only the final outcome.

This can be useful when:

- the task success criterion depends on a controllable process variable;
- the learner cannot infer the process from outcome alone;
- a specific coordination relation must change.

PMID 41037919 showed an external-process cue was best for bar velocity, while external-outcome favored displacement.

Thus:

**“external” and “outcome” are not synonyms.**

Canonical boundary:

- focus direction: internal vs external;
- temporal/task target: process vs outcome.

These dimensions must remain separate.

## 12. Cue tied to error cause

A cue should ideally act on the **diagnosed cause** of a relevant error, not merely the visible symptom.

However direct cueing RCT evidence that formally separates:

`cue to error cause`
vs
`cue to visible symptom`

is sparse.

Therefore this item is retained as a **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not a proven general causal law.

Its rationale is supported indirectly by:

- NEU-15 prescriptive-vs-descriptive feedback evidence;
- cue-specific acute changes in lift coordination;
- instruction studies showing that initial task representation can persist and shape later execution (PMID 32997556).

Project rule:

**diagnose first, then choose a cue whose intended action has a plausible link to the error mechanism.**

Exact error diagnosis belongs NEU-18.

## 13. Bad cue can create a bad task representation

PMID 32997556 showed that initial explicit chunk instructions altered motor-sequence organization:

- aligned vs misaligned instructed chunks produced different early performance;
- instruction-dependent temporal patterns persisted after 10 days of practice;
- learners could gradually overcome a poor imposed structure.

This is not resistance exercise, but it establishes an important general boundary:

**instruction is not neutral; it can shape the learner's initial representation of the task and leave persistent traces.**

Therefore cueing should not be treated as harmless commentary.

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for general motor instruction**.

## 14. Individual response to wording

Individual response exists, but fixed “cue learner types” are not justified.

PMID 29617214:

- children improved under both internal and external conditions;
- no overall difference between conditions;
- task-specific focus preference correlated with differential performance (r ≈ 0.56).

PMID 36862750:

- 173 youth participants across multiple sites;
- most sprint/jump comparisons showed no meaningful difference among external, internal, analogy and neutral cues;
- limited task-specific differences existed;
- authors supported more individualized application.

PMID 42911075 (2026 exploratory CMJ study):

- choice over external-focus cues increased perceived autonomy but did not improve jump height;
- different cue formulations produced different performance;
- participants did not consistently reproduce the intended wording/features and often reinterpreted the cue.

Canonical rule:

**individualize by observed task response and cue interpretation, not by assigning a permanent cueing type.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 15. Cue semantics matter

Cue effects can differ even when broad category is unchanged.

Examples:

- “push” vs “punch” changed bench-press velocity (PMID 40602018);
- different external-focus formulations in the 2026 CMJ study produced different jump outcomes despite sharing the same nominal focus class;
- participants can reinterpret or simplify the cue internally.

Therefore:

**the semantic/action representation invoked by the phrase matters; category labels alone are insufficient.**

This parallels NEU-14's cue-classification problem.

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for acute performance**.

## 16. When should a cue be removed?

Direct evidence defining an exact verbal-cue removal schedule is sparse.

No validated universal rule exists such as:

- remove after N repetitions;
- remove after N successful trials;
- always fade every session.

A defensible criterion is functional:

Remove/fade/test without the cue when:

1. the target relation is consistently expressed;
2. the learner can self-detect the relevant error;
3. the cue no longer improves the target outcome;
4. the cue begins to increase conscious micromanagement or cognitive load;
5. independent retention/transfer is the next learning goal.

Important boundary:

**cue removal should be tested, not assumed.**

The learner should eventually demonstrate the target skill without needing the coaching phrase.

Status: **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**.

---

## 17. Cue removal ≠ feedback fading

Cueing and feedback are different.

- Feedback tells the learner something about a completed/current attempt.
- Cueing directs attention/action before or during an attempt.

Therefore NEU-15 faded-feedback evidence cannot be directly converted into a cue-fading schedule.

Context-learning evidence (PMID 22065045) shows that motor behavior can become sensitive to contextual cues early in learning and less sensitive after extended practice, but this is **indirect** evidence and not a verbal-cue removal trial.

Canonical rule:

**test cue-free execution and retention directly; do not infer cue independence from feedback literature.**

## 18. When does technical instruction become cognitive overload?

There is no universal cue count or word count.

Overload risk rises when:

- multiple rules must be actively maintained;
- rules compete for attention;
- the skill is unfamiliar;
- execution is rapid;
- working-memory capacity is lower;
- fatigue/pressure/dual-task demand consumes cognitive resources.

PMID 28878701 is the strongest direct motor-learning evidence checked:

- five explicit basketball instructions;
- lower-WM children showed worse learning pattern;
- higher-WM children improved.

PMID 24796598 independently found poorer performance when more task rules were specified in instructions in a multirule task.

PMID 27063681 showed explicit instructions imposed greater working-memory cost than analogy instructions in a speech motor task.

PMID 26367306 suggests reducing explicit informational volume can mitigate some explicit-instruction costs, although performance differences were not statistically decisive.

Canonical rule:

**cognitive overload is an interaction among instruction load, task demand, timing and learner capacity—not a fixed number of cues.**

Confidence: **HIGH for interaction principle / LOW for exact threshold**.

---

## 19. Detailed instruction can be necessary despite cognitive cost

Avoid a false rule:

`less instruction = always better`.

PMID 42190132:

- simple “activate your core” did not alter the target trunk coordination;
- detailed coaching did alter displacement and rotational stiffness;
- both conditions reduced maximal lifting force.

Thus detailed instruction can:

- solve an information deficit;
- simultaneously impose acute performance cost.

This is a direct example of:

`information benefit`
vs
`cognitive/attentional cost`.

Project rule:

**add detail only when the additional information changes a justified target relation.**

## 20. Cueing and acute strength performance

Cueing can affect performance independently of durable learning.

Direct examples:

- external/process cue changed hex-bar velocity (PMID 41037919);
- “punch” vs “push” changed bench-press velocity (PMID 40602018);
- “fast” / “hard and fast” altered isokinetic torque/work/power (PMID 30589722);
- detailed trunk cueing reduced maximal lifting force while changing stiffness (PMID 42190132);
- analogy vs explicit instruction changed RFD/EMG acutely (PMID 41558250).

Therefore:

**an acute cue effect is evidence that wording changes current motor organization/performance—not proof of durable skill learning.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 21. Cue tied to outcome vs cue tied to cause

These are different design questions.

### Outcome-targeted cue
Specifies what successful task effect should occur.

Useful when:

- outcome is directly controllable/observable;
- learner needs a clear action goal.

### Cause-targeted cue
Attempts to change a diagnosed determinant of the error.

Useful when:

- outcome information alone is insufficient;
- the cause is reasonably identified;
- the cue can act on that cause.

A cue can be both.

Example structure:

- outcome: “send the bar straight up”;
- cause-targeted: “keep pressure through the whole foot” if loss of foot pressure is actually the diagnosed driver.

Project rule:

**do not confuse the visible error with its cause; exact diagnosis is NEU-18.**

Status: **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**.

---

## 22. Specificity ≠ micromanagement

Cue specificity should answer:

> Which relation should the learner attend to or change?

It should not automatically answer:

> What exact joint geometry must every repetition copy?

Upstream NEU-09 and NEU-12 established:

- motor abundance;
- multiple task-successful solutions;
- constraints/task goals can shape movement without one unique kinematic template.

Therefore a specific cue can be:

- precise about the task effect;
- permissive about irrelevant degrees of freedom.

Canonical rule:

**be specific about the problem/goal, not unnecessarily prescriptive about every movement degree of freedom.**

## 23. External/internal cue evidence remains outcome-dependent

NEU-14 remains binding.

The 2026 attentional-focus network meta-analysis (PMID 42419609):

- reclassified 78/87 studies;
- rankings changed after reclassification;
- expertise altered rankings;
- small-study/reporting-bias sensitivity remained.

This reinforces a cueing-level lesson:

**the exact wording and target of the cue matter more than the nominal category label alone.**

No universal external cue default is authorized at NEU-16.

## 25. Practical cueing synthesis

A defensible future coaching heuristic is:

1. identify the task goal/error before cueing;
2. use no more simultaneous information than necessary;
3. prefer one clear target relation as the default starting point, not as a rigid law;
4. make wording short enough to execute but specific enough to be actionable;
5. choose internal/external/analogy format by task information and outcome;
6. align cue semantics with the actual performance goal;
7. change cue wording if the learner interprets it differently than intended;
8. test whether the cue changes the intended variable;
9. remove/fade/test cue-free execution once it stops adding useful information;
10. judge learning by retention/transfer, not acute cue-dependent improvement.

Status:

**PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not a universal cueing algorithm.

## 27. Measurement boundaries

### Cue count
Number of simultaneously active instructions, not total coaching statements over a session.

### Cue length
Words/complexity required to represent the cue; no validated universal cutoff.

### Cue specificity
Precision of the task-relevant relation, not merely number of words.

### Cue direction
Internal vs external.

### Cue target
Process vs outcome.

### Cue format
Literal/explicit vs analogy/metaphor.

### Cue effectiveness
Immediate change in the intended target variable.

### Cue learning effect
Delayed retention/transfer after cue removal.

### Cue dependence
Failure to express the learned relation without the cue; must be tested.

### Cognitive overload
Performance/learning cost due to instruction demand relative to available cognitive resources; not inferable from cue count alone.

## 28. Direct resistance-exercise boundary

Direct cueing evidence in resistance exercise is improving but remains dominated by acute outcomes.

Strongest checked examples:

- hex-bar deadlift focus/process/outcome study — acute velocity/displacement;
- bench-press action-verb study — acute bar velocity;
- trunk bracing cue study — acute stiffness/posture/force;
- back-squat focus study — acute EMG redistribution;
- analogy/explicit lifting study — acute EMG/RFD/force.

These establish:

**cue wording can alter current resistance-exercise motor organization and performance.**

They do **not** establish:

- optimal cue count for learning a compound lift;
- optimal cue length;
- optimal cue-removal schedule;
- superior long-term analogy/external/internal cue strategy;
- delayed transfer across loads/exercises.

Exact exercise cue libraries belong TEC/COA after NEU closure.
