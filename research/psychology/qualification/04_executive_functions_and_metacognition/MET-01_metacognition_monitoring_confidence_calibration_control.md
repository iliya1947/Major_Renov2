# MET-01 — Metacognition: monitoring, confidence, calibration, control, and first-order/second-order boundaries

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Establish a current architecture of metacognition that prevents confidence, self-report, calibration, metacognitive sensitivity, and actual task performance from being collapsed into one construct.

The block separates:

- first-order task performance from second-order evaluation;
- confidence level from confidence accuracy;
- metacognitive bias from metacognitive sensitivity;
- calibration from discrimination;
- meta-d′ from d′;
- metacognitive efficiency from metacognitive sensitivity;
- local confidence from global self-beliefs;
- prospective from retrospective metacognition;
- error awareness from general metacognitive ability;
- metacognitive monitoring from metacognitive control;
- task-based metacognition from questionnaire/self-report;
- domain-general from domain-specific metacognition;
- stable trait claims from state/task-specific metacognitive effects.

## Core rule

**Metacognition concerns monitoring and regulating one's own cognitive processes, but different measures capture different second-order properties. Confidence, calibration, discrimination, meta-d′/M-ratio, error awareness, self-report, and global self-beliefs are not interchangeable.**

A generic architecture is:

`first-order processing / decision`
-> `internal evidence + uncertainty + post-decision evidence`
-> `metacognitive estimate / confidence`
-> `monitoring output`
-> `control decision: continue, stop, revise, seek information, defer, change strategy`
-> `learning / global self-belief update`

## 1. First-order versus second-order cognition

### First-order

Performance on the primary task:

- perceptual discrimination;
- memory decision;
- reasoning answer;
- action choice.

### Second-order

Evaluation of the first-order state:

- confidence;
- error detection;
- judgment of learning;
- feeling of knowing;
- uncertainty estimate;
- decision to revise or seek help.

Canonical rule:

**good first-order performance != good metacognition.**

Likewise:

**poor first-order performance != poor metacognition.**

A person can be inaccurate but appropriately uncertain, or accurate but poorly calibrated.

## 2. Metacognition is broader than confidence

Confidence is a major operational route into metacognition, but metacognition also includes:

- error awareness;
- prospective judgments;
- judgments of learning;
- global self-performance estimates;
- monitoring of uncertainty;
- metacognitive control.

Therefore:

`metacognition = confidence rating`

is too narrow.

## 3. Confidence is an inferential second-order estimate

Fleming's 2024 synthesis treats propositional confidence as a self-directed estimate about one's own decision/action.

Confidence can be informed by:

- first-order evidence strength;
- post-decisional evidence;
- task beliefs;
- priors about own ability;
- response fluency;
- feedback;
- affect;
- contextual expectations.

Canonical rule:

**confidence is not simply copied from accuracy or sensory evidence.**

## 4. Local versus global metacognition

### Local confidence

Trial-level evaluation such as:

> How likely is this answer correct?

### Global confidence/self-belief

Longer-run estimate such as:

> How good am I at this task/domain?

Current 2025–2026 work supports a hierarchical architecture in which global self-beliefs are learned partly from:

- repeated local confidence;
- external feedback;
- prior self-beliefs.

Canonical rule:

**one local confidence judgment != global self-knowledge.**

## 5. Global self-beliefs can diverge from actual performance

Global confidence can be biased even when local task performance is intact.

The 2025 Katyal et al. work shows that global self-performance estimates can be altered by asymmetric feedback while objective performance is held constant.

Therefore:

**global self-belief is an inference learned from experience/feedback, not a transparent readout of actual ability.**

## 6. Prospective versus retrospective metacognition

### Prospective

Confidence/prediction before the relevant performance evidence is available.

Examples:

- judgment of learning;
- prediction of success;
- pre-task confidence.

### Retrospective

Confidence after producing/observing a response.

Retrospective confidence can use more information because:

- stimulus evidence;
- decision evidence;
- response fluency;
- post-decision evidence

are available.

A 2026 study found stronger within-person confidence–performance coupling for retrospective than prospective judgments.

Canonical rule:

**prospective and retrospective metacognition are different information problems.**

## 7. Mean confidence is not metacognitive sensitivity

A person's average confidence can be:

- high;
- low;

without revealing whether confidence distinguishes correct from incorrect responses.

Therefore:

`high confidence = high metacognitive ability`

is false.

## 8. Metacognitive bias

Metacognitive bias describes a systematic tendency to report confidence at a particular level independent of whether confidence discriminates correctness well.

Examples:

- overconfidence;
- underconfidence;
- conservative confidence policy;
- liberal confidence policy.

Canonical rule:

**bias concerns confidence level/policy, not necessarily sensitivity.**

## 9. Metacognitive sensitivity

Metacognitive sensitivity concerns how well confidence distinguishes correct from incorrect first-order responses.

A person with good sensitivity tends to show:

- higher confidence when correct;
- lower confidence when incorrect.

Sensitivity is a discrimination property.

It is not the same as being perfectly calibrated in absolute probability.

## 10. Calibration

Calibration asks whether stated confidence corresponds to empirical probability of correctness.

Example:

If decisions rated 80% confident are correct about 80% of the time, they are well calibrated at that confidence level.

Calibration can be assessed through:

- calibration curves;
- calibration error;
- Brier-type measures;
- confidence–accuracy gaps.

Canonical rule:

**calibration = absolute agreement between confidence and accuracy.**

## 11. Calibration and discrimination are separable

A person can be:

- systematically overconfident but still rank correct answers above incorrect ones;
- well calibrated on average but poor at distinguishing which specific answers are correct.

Therefore:

**calibration != metacognitive sensitivity.**

This distinction is essential in psychological inference.

## 12. Confidence criterion / bias and sensitivity can move independently

A manipulation can change mean confidence without changing ability to discriminate correct from incorrect decisions.

Likewise, discrimination can change while mean confidence remains similar.

Thus confidence-level effects should not automatically be called metacognitive-ability effects.

## 13. Type-1 sensitivity (d′)

In signal-detection terms, d′ quantifies first-order discriminability.

It describes primary task performance under model assumptions.

Canonical rule:

**d′ is first-order sensitivity, not metacognitive sensitivity.**

## 14. Meta-d′

Meta-d′ asks:

> What first-order d′ would an ideal observer need to generate the observed pattern of confidence discrimination?

It expresses confidence-based second-order discrimination in units comparable to d′.

Meta-d′ is useful because it attempts to normalize metacognitive sensitivity relative to first-order performance.

But it is model-derived and assumption-dependent.

## 15. M-ratio / metacognitive efficiency

A common measure is:

`M-ratio = meta-d′ / d′`.

Conceptually:

- M-ratio around 1: observed metacognitive sensitivity approaches what is expected from available first-order information under the model;
- below 1: some information useful for first-order choice is not reflected in confidence;
- above 1 can arise under some empirical/model conditions and should not automatically be treated as "super-metacognition."

Canonical rule:

**M-ratio is a model-dependent efficiency measure, not a literal percentage of introspective access.**

## 16. Meta-d′ / M-ratio are not assumption-free

Rausch, Hellmann, and Zehetleitner demonstrate that meta-d′/d′ is not invariant under every plausible generative model of confidence.

Its desirable independence from:

- first-order sensitivity;
- decision criteria;
- confidence criteria

holds under particular model structures more than others.

Therefore:

**meta-d′ is useful, not theory-free.**

## 17. Current metacognition measures depend on first-order performance

Rahnev's 2025 empirical comparison of 17 metacognition measures found many strong dependencies on task performance.

This is critical because the field often seeks "pure" measures of metacognitive ability independent of first-order accuracy.

Canonical rule:

**performance matching/modeling remains necessary when comparing metacognition across people/conditions.**

## 18. No current metacognition metric is perfect

Rahnev's comparison found:

- all 17 measures responded validly to manipulated corruption of confidence;
- most had broadly similar precision;
- different measures had different dependencies/psychometrics.

Thus there is no universally optimal single metric.

Measure choice should depend on:

- design;
- number of trials;
- first-order performance;
- confidence scale;
- research claim.

## 19. Split-half reliability can be high while test–retest reliability is poor

The 2025 Nature Communications analysis found:

- very high split-half reliability for many measures with sufficient trials;
- generally low test–retest reliability across days.

This creates the same key distinction seen in other cognitive-control measures:

**precise within-session measurement != stable trait measurement.**

## 20. Trial count matters

Metacognitive metrics can be noisy with limited trials.

The 2025 analysis suggests that individual-difference work may require substantially more trials than typical small experiments, with around 100 trials functioning as a rough lower bound in some analyzed conditions and much larger samples improving stability.

Canonical rule:

**one short confidence task is weak evidence for a stable metacognitive trait.**

## 21. Metacognitive bias can be functionally meaningful

A 2026 perspective argues that metacognitive bias should not automatically be treated as measurement error.

A confidence policy can regulate tradeoffs such as:

- exploration versus commitment;
- flexibility versus stability;
- acting versus seeking information.

This is a theoretical proposal, not a universal claim that bias is adaptive.

Canonical rule:

**over/underconfidence is a pattern whose function must be tested, not moralized.**

## 22. Confidence can continue to update after a decision

Post-decisional evidence accumulation can alter:

- confidence;
- error detection;
- change-of-mind probability.

A 2026 review on clinical decision-making emphasizes that decisions are not always cognitively "finished" at the moment of first response.

Canonical rule:

**initial confidence != final confidence state.**

## 23. Change of mind is a metacognitive-control outcome

A person may:

- retain the first answer;
- revise it;
- seek more evidence;
- defer the decision.

The quality of these actions depends on how monitoring signals are used.

Metacognitive control therefore concerns **what the system does with monitoring information**.

## 24. Monitoring and control are distinct

### Monitoring

Estimate:

- accuracy;
- uncertainty;
- learning state;
- error likelihood.

### Control

Use that estimate to:

- allocate study time;
- re-check;
- revise;
- seek help;
- stop searching;
- abstain.

Canonical rule:

**accurate self-monitoring does not guarantee adaptive metacognitive control.**

## 25. Poor monitoring can sometimes coexist with effective learned control

A person can use:

- habit;
- external feedback;
- rules;
- environmental structure

to make useful control decisions without high-quality explicit introspection.

Therefore behavior should not be interpreted only through self-reported insight.

## 26. Error awareness is one local metacognitive judgment

Error awareness concerns:

> Was that particular response wrong?

It overlaps with performance monitoring.

But it is not equivalent to:

- global confidence calibration;
- meta-d′;
- self-knowledge;
- general insight.

This preserves the EXE-05 boundary.

## 27. Domain generality is not settled as one universal trait

Cross-domain studies have produced mixed evidence.

Recent 2026 work across perception and memory supports some cross-domain regularity while emphasizing that domain generality can **emerge** through learning/development rather than being an inherent property of every confidence computation.

Canonical rule:

**metacognition should not be assumed fully domain-general or fully domain-specific a priori.**

## 28. Cross-task correlations depend on measurement quality

Hierarchical estimation and better reliability can increase estimated cross-task correlations.

Therefore low raw cross-task correlations can reflect:

- real domain specificity;
- measurement error;
- insufficient trial count;
- metric choice.

Canonical rule:

**measurement noise must be addressed before concluding domain specificity.**

## 29. Local metacognition can support global self-beliefs

The 2026 integrative neuroscience review proposes a multi-timescale architecture:

- local confidence from dynamic uncertainty/evidence;
- global self-beliefs integrating local signals across time and context.

This provides a useful bridge between:

- trial-level confidence;
- self-concept about ability.

But the exact neural/computational implementation remains active research.

## 30. Global confidence is vulnerable to feedback bias

The 2025 Katyal et al. experiments manipulated feedback while holding objective performance constant and changed global self-performance estimates.

Therefore:

**self-belief can become systematically distorted by feedback history even without matching performance change.**

This is directly relevant when interpreting self-assessments.

## 31. Self-report metacognition is not task-based metacognition

Questionnaires can measure:

- perceived metacognitive awareness;
- beliefs about thinking;
- self-reflective tendencies;
- perceived strategy use.

Task-based measures assess behavior such as:

- confidence–accuracy relationships;
- meta-d′;
- calibration;
- error detection.

These are different measurement levels.

Canonical rule:

**self-reported metacognitive ability != demonstrated metacognitive sensitivity.**

## 32. Questionnaire response style can create spurious metacognitive associations

A 2026 study found that:

- systematic rating bias;
- inattentive responding

could generate apparent associations between confidence and psychiatric self-report dimensions.

This is a strong warning for any analysis linking:

- confidence;
- personality/mental-health questionnaires;
- self-report insight.

Canonical rule:

**shared response style can masquerade as psychological covariance.**

## 33. Self-report requires response-quality controls

Useful protections include:

- attention checks;
- rare/infrequency items where appropriate;
- response-style indices;
- separate measurement of confidence bias;
- preregistered exclusions;
- replication across task-based measures.

Self-report should not be discarded; it should be interpreted at its actual level.

## 34. Confidence ratings can be affected by scale use

Confidence scales introduce choices about:

- number of categories;
- anchors;
- probability versus ordinal labels;
- time of judgment;
- incentive;
- response criterion.

Two people can have similar internal uncertainty but use the rating scale differently.

Canonical rule:

**observed confidence rating = internal confidence + reporting policy/criterion.**

## 35. Confidence scale compression/expansion matters

A participant may use:

- only middle categories;
- mostly extremes;
- a narrow range.

This changes apparent calibration and discrimination.

Signal-detection/model-based approaches can help separate confidence criteria from sensitivity, but only under their assumptions.

## 36. Confidence and accuracy can share first-order evidence

Confidence is not necessarily generated by an entirely separate second-order system.

Many models propose that confidence reuses or transforms evidence also used for the first-order decision.

Other models include:

- additional post-decision evidence;
- metacognitive noise;
- unequal evidence access;
- heuristic features.

Canonical rule:

**first-order and second-order processes are distinguishable levels, not necessarily independent evidence channels.**

## 37. Multiple computational models can explain confidence

Shekhar and Rahnev's 2024 comparison shows several plausible process models of confidence.

Candidate dimensions include:

- shared versus separate evidence;
- post-decisional accumulation;
- decision-congruent evidence;
- metacognitive noise;
- response-specific evidence.

Therefore:

**one confidence pattern does not uniquely identify its computational mechanism.**

## 38. Confidence is influenced by nonaccuracy cues

Confidence can be shaped by:

- response time/fluency;
- familiarity;
- affect;
- expected difficulty;
- prior feedback;
- reward expectation;
- social information.

Some cues can be ecologically useful.

Others can distort calibration.

Thus a cue-driven confidence effect is not automatically irrational.

## 39. Fluency can be informative or misleading

Fast/easy processing often correlates with correctness in familiar environments.

Using fluency as a confidence cue can therefore be adaptive.

But experimentally or contextually altered fluency can create miscalibration.

Canonical rule:

**heuristic confidence cue != metacognitive failure by definition.**

## 40. Confidence is not insight in the clinical sense

Clinical "insight" can involve awareness of:

- symptoms;
- illness;
- functional impairment;
- need for treatment.

Trial-level confidence accuracy is a narrower construct.

The 2025–2026 clinical metacognition literature explicitly distinguishes:

- core confidence/calibration;
- self-reflection;
- beliefs about mental processes;
- broader clinical insight.

Canonical rule:

**meta-d′ != clinical insight.**

## 41. Metacognition is not introspective infallibility

Metacognitive judgments are themselves inferences.

They can be:

- noisy;
- biased;
- learned;
- context-sensitive;
- partly based on heuristic cues.

Therefore metacognition is not a privileged error-free window into hidden cognitive mechanisms.

## 42. "I know why I did it" is not direct mechanistic evidence

A person's explanation of their own behavior can be:

- informative about conscious reasons;
- informative about current self-model;
- partially correct;
- post-hoc or incomplete.

Canonical rule:

**introspective explanation = evidence about reported reasons/self-model, not automatic proof of causal mechanism.**

Causal claims require convergence with behavior, context, prediction, and alternative explanations.

## 43. Metacognitive accuracy can improve control

Good metacognitive sensitivity can support:

- information seeking;
- selective revision;
- study allocation;
- help seeking;
- abstention;
- social communication of uncertainty.

But usefulness depends on whether control policies consume the monitoring signal appropriately.

## 44. Metacognition can be locally good but globally distorted

A person can discriminate correct from incorrect individual decisions reasonably well while holding a globally inaccurate belief about their ability.

Conversely, a reasonably accurate global self-view can coexist with noisy trial-level confidence.

Canonical rule:

**local and global metacognition must be measured separately.**

## 45. Prospective global confidence versus retrospective local confidence are especially different

A pre-task global estimate draws heavily on:

- prior self-beliefs;
- past feedback;
- expected difficulty.

A post-response local confidence judgment can additionally use:

- trial evidence;
- decision fluency;
- post-decision signals.

Therefore the strongest inference depends on judgment timing and scale.

## 46. Metacognitive Inference Gate

Before interpreting a metacognitive result, ask:

### A. Level

1. What is the first-order task?
2. What is the second-order judgment?
3. Is the judgment local or global?
4. Is it prospective or retrospective?

### B. First-order performance

5. What is accuracy/d′?
6. Is first-order performance matched across groups/conditions?
7. Could low/high first-order performance distort the metacognitive metric?
8. Is task difficulty separated from metacognition?

### C. Confidence bias

9. What is mean confidence?
10. Is there overconfidence or underconfidence?
11. Could confidence-scale use/criterion explain the difference?
12. Is bias being confused with metacognitive ability?

### D. Calibration

13. Does stated confidence correspond to empirical correctness probability?
14. Which calibration metric is used?
15. Is calibration being confused with discrimination?
16. Could average calibration hide poor item/trial discrimination?

### E. Sensitivity/discrimination

17. Does confidence distinguish correct from incorrect trials?
18. Is AUC2, gamma, phi, Δconfidence, or meta-d′ used?
19. Is the measure valid under the task design?
20. Does it depend on first-order performance?

### F. Meta-d′ / efficiency

21. Is meta-d′ clearly separated from d′?
22. Is M-ratio/meta-d′/d′ used?
23. Are signal-detection assumptions plausible?
24. Is the ratio being interpreted as literal introspective access?
25. Could model choice change the inference?

### G. Reliability

26. How many trials are available?
27. Is split-half reliability adequate?
28. Is test-retest reliability known?
29. Is a precise within-session estimate being converted into a stable trait?

### H. Monitoring versus control

30. Is the system merely estimating confidence/uncertainty?
31. Does the judgment change behavior?
32. Is information seeking/revision/abstention measured?
33. Could good monitoring coexist with poor control?

### I. Error awareness

34. Is error awareness measured directly?
35. Is error awareness being generalized to global metacognition?
36. Is performance monitoring being confused with explicit confidence?

### J. Domain/generalization

37. Which domain is tested: perception, memory, reasoning, social judgment, other?
38. Is domain generality assumed or demonstrated?
39. Is measurement reliability sufficient for cross-domain correlations?
40. Does local metacognition generalize to global self-belief?

### K. Self-report

41. Is the measure a task or questionnaire?
42. Could response style/inattention affect the correlation?
43. Are task-based and self-report metacognition being treated as equivalent?
44. Is a clinical insight construct being inferred from confidence accuracy?

### L. Mechanism

45. Could response fluency, familiarity, feedback, affect, reward, or expected difficulty drive confidence?
46. Is post-decision evidence available?
47. Which computational confidence models remain viable?
48. Is introspective explanation being treated as causal proof?

### M. Individual inference

49. Does the pattern replicate across occasions/tasks?
50. What is the strongest supported metacognitive conclusion?
51. What rival explanation remains?
52. What measurement/manipulation would discriminate the alternatives?

## 47. High-value failure modes

### Failure A — high confidence = good metacognition

Incorrect.

Correct:

Mean confidence is a bias/level measure, not sensitivity.

### Failure B — accuracy = metacognition

Incorrect.

Correct:

Accuracy is first-order performance.

### Failure C — calibration = discrimination

Incorrect.

Correct:

Calibration concerns absolute probability agreement; discrimination concerns ranking/distinguishing correct from incorrect responses.

### Failure D — overconfidence = poor sensitivity

Incorrect.

Correct:

A person can be biased upward yet discriminate correct from incorrect trials well.

### Failure E — meta-d′ = first-order d′

Incorrect.

Correct:

Meta-d′ is a model-derived second-order sensitivity measure expressed in d′ units.

### Failure F — M-ratio = percentage introspective access

Incorrect.

Correct:

M-ratio is model-dependent metacognitive efficiency.

### Failure G — meta-d′/M-ratio is assumption-free

Incorrect.

Correct:

Recent work shows dependence on generative confidence-model assumptions and first-order performance.

### Failure H — high split-half reliability = stable trait

Incorrect.

Correct:

Most metacognitive measures show much weaker test-retest reliability across days.

### Failure I — one short confidence task = stable self-knowledge trait

Incorrect.

Correct:

Trial count, reliability, task domain, and state/context matter.

### Failure J — error awareness = global metacognition

Incorrect.

Correct:

Error awareness is one local monitoring judgment.

### Failure K — task-based metacognition = self-report metacognition

Incorrect.

Correct:

Questionnaires and confidence-based performance measures operate at different measurement levels.

### Failure L — confidence/self-report correlation = shared mechanism

Incorrect.

Correct:

Response style and inattentive responding can create spurious covariance.

### Failure M — domain-general confidence is inherent

Incorrect.

Correct:

Current evidence supports partial/common structure but also task/domain dependence and developmental emergence.

### Failure N — self-belief = actual ability

Incorrect.

Correct:

Global self-beliefs are learned/inferred and can be distorted by feedback history.

### Failure O — introspective explanation = causal mechanism

Incorrect.

Correct:

Self-report is evidence about reported conscious reasons/self-model; causal mechanism requires independent support.

### Failure P — good monitoring = good control

Incorrect.

Correct:

Monitoring and use of monitoring information are separable.

## Canonical working rule

**Separate first-order performance from second-order judgment; distinguish confidence bias, calibration, sensitivity, and efficiency; identify judgment timing and scale; validate reliability/model assumptions; separate monitoring from control and task-based measures from self-report; then infer only the metacognitive property supported by converging evidence.**

Never:

**high confidence / articulate self-explanation -> accurate self-knowledge -> true causal mechanism.**

## Current-standard note

As of 2026-11-19:

- the newest integrative review models metacognitive self-evaluation as structured transformation of uncertainty across multiple timescales, from local confidence to global self-beliefs;
- confidence level, calibration, sensitivity/discrimination, and metacognitive efficiency remain distinct measurement targets;
- the 2025 comprehensive comparison of 17 measures shows no perfect universal metric, substantial dependence on first-order performance, high split-half but generally weak test-retest reliability;
- meta-d′/M-ratio remain widely useful but are not generative-model-free or assumption-free;
- current 2026 evidence argues against assuming that metacognition is inherently domain-general, while also supporting emergent/common structure across domains under some conditions;
- global self-beliefs can be systematically shifted by feedback independently of objective performance;
- self-report/confidence correlations can be materially distorted by response style and inattentive responding, requiring explicit quality controls.
