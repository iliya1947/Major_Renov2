# LEA-05 — Extinction, renewal/reinstatement, inhibitory learning, and reconsolidation: boundary conditions and evidence validity

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**

Evidence checked: **2026-11-19**.
Scope: generic associative-learning science, not clinical instruction or individualized interpretation.

## Purpose and inferential failure

Earlier LEA blocks established separate boundaries for associative learning, reinforcement learning, and habit measurement. This block prevents a further major inference error:

**A formerly automatic reaction stops appearing; therefore the original association was erased, overwritten, or reconsolidated.**

The behavioral endpoint is real, but it is not a process identifier. Similar observed attenuation can result from extinction/inhibitory learning, altered context and retrieval, changed value or state representation, competing new associations, stronger goal-directed action, habituation, unlearning, or multiple mechanisms. Strong mechanistic attribution requires competing hypotheses, boundary controls, and recovery testing.

## 1. Core distinctions

1. **Acquisition**: formation of predictive CS–US or response–outcome relations.
2. **Extinction**: a procedure in which a previously reinforced CS or response is repeatedly encountered without the expected reinforcer; the term is also used for resulting loss of conditioned responding.
3. **Inhibitory extinction learning**: new context-sensitive information about nonoccurrence of the expected US/outcome that competes with earlier learning.
4. **Forgetting**: reduced accessible memory or expression across time; not synonymous with extinction.
5. **Habituation**: response decrement after repeated stimulus exposure that need not involve a learned change in CS–US contingency.
6. **Memory retrieval/reactivation**: access or expression of a memory; not automatically destabilization.
7. **Destabilization**: transition under certain conditions to a modifiable/labile mnemonic state, often inferred indirectly from sensitivity to post-reactivation interference.
8. **Reconsolidation**: processes associated with restabilization after destabilization; not every retrieval induces reconsolidation.
9. **Reconsolidation interference/update**: experimental attempt to alter later expression or content after reactivation, requiring additional evidence to distinguish from new learning, retrieval interference and performance suppression.
10. **Erasure**: strong claim that original memory information is no longer retained; generally *not* demonstrated by absence of a response.

## 2. Basic Pavlovian extinction design

Acquisition: CS+ predicts US, CS− does not (or predicts it less).
Extinction: present CS+ without US.
Immediate probe: conditioned responding commonly declines.

Key limitation: this observation does not identify whether the original CS–US representation disappeared. Alternate extinction-related representations may inhibit its expression, and the test can be affected by attention, expectancy, fatigue, measurement habituation, or task demand.

Extinction may involve multiple processes rather than exclusively one neural or computational mechanism.

## 3. Instrumental extinction is related but distinct

Instrumental extinction removes the response-contingent reinforcer after action–outcome training; the dependent variable is action frequency/topography or choice, not necessarily Pavlovian CR.

Do not treat Pavlovian CS-no US and instrumental response-no outcome as interchangeable:
- actions can be sustained by other reinforcers;
- contingency knowledge, competing actions and effort matter;
- inhibitory discriminative/context control differs;
- resurgence and response allocation can change when an alternative reinforcer disappears.

Bouton and colleagues' *Learning to Stop Responding* distinguishes operant extinction, punishment, and feature-negative/stop-signal learning; all can suppress performance without the same learning history.

## 4. What recovery after extinction establishes

A previously weakened response can return without redoing full original training.

This supports preservation or reaccessibility of some relevant influence of prior learning, but it does not prove the original neuronal trace is intact in a simple, unchanged form. Recovery can reflect retrieval competition, contextual gating, remaining associations, or fresh inference.

**No recovery in a limited test also cannot prove erasure:** the test may be insensitive or the recovery trigger inadequate.

## 5. Spontaneous recovery

An extinguished response may increase after time passes following extinction, despite no new CS–US pairings.

Time can function as a change in temporal context. This is a useful model, not a complete explanation of all spontaneous-recovery effects.

2026 systematic review of human fear conditioning reports substantial variation in designs, common ~24-hour extinction-to-test intervals, and unresolved mechanism/clinical transport questions.

Control for retention interval, circadian/time-of-day effects, measure choice, baseline responding, and re-exposure test learning.

## 6. Renewal

Response returns when the contextual circumstances of extinction change.

Notation:
- **ABA**: acquisition A, extinction B, test A;
- **ABC**: acquisition A, extinction B, test novel C;
- **AAB**: acquisition/extinction A, test B.

Renewal is a *test-context manipulation*, not necessarily a subjective report of conscious contextual recognition.

A 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis of renewal in human fear conditioning found that multiple extinction contexts and greater acquisition/extinction context similarity can reduce renewal. Effects depend on paradigm, contexts, and measurement.

Do not assume extinction learning is absent merely because response is expressed elsewhere.

## 7. Reinstatement

After extinction, unsignaled presentations of the US (or related aversive events in a fear-conditioning preparation) may restore responding to CS at subsequent test.

Distinguish reinstatement from:
- reacquisition of the original CS–US contingency;
- sensitization/nonassociative arousal;
- shifts in US expectancy;
- contextual US associations.

Assess controls such as US-only, CS−, context, and generalized response reactivity.

## 8. Rapid reacquisition

When the CS–US or action–outcome relation is restored, responses can relearn more quickly than during initial acquisition. This can support residual history of learning but may also reflect practice or retained structural knowledge.

Do not infer exact molecular trace preservation from savings alone.

## 9. Resurgence and alternative responses

Instrumental resurgence: a previously extinguished action returns when reinforcement sustaining a more recently trained alternative action is removed or degraded.

This is distinct from reinstatement by noncontingent US exposure and from context renewal, though they may share mechanisms relating to contextual/reinforcement changes.

Relevant especially when claims concern cessation of repeated actions rather than Pavlovian fear.

## 10. Why inhibitory learning remains a leading model

Many extinction studies find a response reduction followed by context-, time-, or US-dependent recovery. These results are consistent with new inhibitory or occasion-setting-like information that regulates expression of older associative knowledge.

However:
- "new inhibition" is a broad functional explanation;
- neural and computational implementations vary;
- extinction need not always leave the original memory entirely unchanged;
- reports of erasure-like effects require stronger experimental evidence, not categorical dismissal.

Canonical rule: **extinction usually does not license a memory-erasure inference**.

## 11. Context is more than the physical room

Context variables may include:
- physical location, visual/olfactory cues;
- temporal interval;
- internal physiological state;
- sequence of preceding events;
- background reinforcement conditions;
- implicit task structure or latent state;
- verbal instructions and expected contingencies.

A response can reappear when an apparently irrelevant context component shifts. Context sensitivity is not proof of a single specific storage locus.

## 12. Prediction error during extinction

An expected US omitted after CS produces an outcome mismatch. This can support extinction learning and potentially other updating operations.

Separate:
- **signed outcome PE**;
- **surprise/unsigned PE**;
- **state/task change inference**;
- **prediction of timing or identity**.

If the learner concludes that the environment entered a new state, prior CS–US learning may be preserved in one representation while a new no-US rule is learned for another state.

Thus **PE is not a unique reconsolidation signature**.

## 13. Extinction expression versus retention

Within-session reduction and next-session extinction retention are distinct endpoints.

A subject may show sharp decrement during extinction but elevated responding at delayed retest. Conversely, stable low responding may reflect effective retrieval of inhibitory information, not destruction of original excitatory learning.

Analyze:
1. acquisition;
2. early/late extinction;
3. delayed recall;
4. recovery tests;
5. transfer outside the trained context.

## 14. Expectancy violation versus mere affect reduction

An inhibitory-learning approach in exposure-related literature emphasizes falsification of a concrete outcome expectation. Immediate reduction in fear/arousal is not the same as retention or generalization of learning.

Craske et al. (2014) proposed clinical optimization ideas including expectancy violation, variability, multiple contexts and retrieval cues. These are a mechanistic framework and implementation proposals, **not universal evidence that any single exercise cures fear or rewrites memory**.

Clinical intervention details are deferred to the later clinical/intervention domains.

## 15. Reconsolidation: original causal evidence

Nader, Schafe and LeDoux (2000) showed in animal auditory fear conditioning that after memory reactivation, interfering with amygdala protein synthesis impaired later responding, with timing-dependent effects.

This is strong experimental support for postretrieval molecular dependence in that preparation.

It does **not** establish:
- every retrieval destabilizes every memory;
- behavioral change without molecular intervention is reconsolidation;
- human autobiographical memories can be permanently erased on command;
- every therapeutic improvement works through reconsolidation.

## 16. Retrieval, destabilization, and restabilization are three questions

1. Was memory accessed/expressed?
2. Did reminder conditions induce a destabilized, interference-sensitive state?
3. Did a specific post-reactivation manipulation change the later memory outcome through restabilization-related mechanisms?

A reminder alone answers question 1, not 2 or 3. To support reconsolidation, compare reminder/no-reminder controls, interference timing, destabilization conditions, and delayed expression; distinguish immediate performance effects.

## 17. Prediction error is a conditional candidate, not a magic key

Research on reminder-triggered reconsolidation frequently implicates mismatch between what was expected and what happened.

But mismatch also drives extinction, latent-state inference, attention, and memory updating. PE type, degree, timing, and structure matter; too short/weak a reminder may only retrieve, while more extended no-US exposure can produce extinction rather than the intended destabilization window.

2025 experimental work explicitly investigated prediction-error degree and 2025 strength manipulations as possible boundary conditions. Neither establishes a universal scalar PE threshold or reliable individual "rewrite switch."

## 18. Memory strength, age, and reminder structure

Candidate moderators:
- memory strength and number of acquisition trials;
- age/remoteness of memory;
- reminder duration and number;
- omission, mismatch and cue structure;
- retrieval context;
- interval between retrieval and intervention;
- retention/testing delay;
- original task and outcome type.

The literature contains conflicting findings on how these factors alter destabilization. Boundary conditions must be empirically identified per protocol, species and memory system, not assumed from a simple rule.

## 19. Retrieval–extinction / postretrieval extinction

A common procedure presents a brief reminder CS and then extinction after a specified delay, intending to act within a putative reconsolidation window.

Schiller et al. (2010) reported less return of conditioned fear after postretrieval extinction than standard extinction in a human study.

However independent checks, including registered replication/verification reports (Chalkia et al. 2020), did not reliably reproduce persistent benefit. The 2018 addendum also clarified exclusion procedures in the original article.

**This is a contested translational finding, not an established reproducible memory-erasure method.**

## 20. A successful retrieval–extinction outcome need not identify mechanism

Improved delayed responding could arise from:
- stronger/contextually broader inhibitory extinction;
- expectation change or state reclassification;
- retrieval-induced learning or enhanced attention;
- memory integration and new associative competition;
- genuine reconsolidation-mediated updating;
- differences in baseline acquisition/measure exclusion;
- experimental demand or limited probe sensitivity.

Only designs that rule out alternatives warrant stronger mechanistic claims.

## 21. Null effects do not falsify all reconsolidation

A registered replication of one postretrieval-extinction protocol may fail because the original mechanism is unreliable, because memory destabilization conditions differ, or because measurement sensitivity is poor.

The correct conclusion is **limited/reduced confidence in that particular effect and its generality**, not "reconsolidation cannot exist" in any species or memory system.

Separate:
- robust animal pharmacological causal evidence in some preparations;
- mixed human behavioral protocol evidence;
- clinical applicability, requiring another evidence layer.

## 22. Neural evidence constraints

Animal circuit and protein-synthesis interventions can support causal inference about selected memory processes.

Human skin conductance, startle, fMRI BOLD, recall ratings, and questionnaire responses are different measurements; none directly visualize an unchanged versus erased engram.

Neural correlates and PE-like signals can accompany more than one learning process.

Avoid **signal X changed → memory trace Y deleted**.

## 23. Current 2026 translational updates

A 2026 *Nature Reviews Psychology* review stresses laboratory-to-natural-environment distinctions and interprets recovery, variability, and persistence adaptively in environments where contingencies can change.

A 2026 systematic review of TMS + reactivation/reconsolidation paradigms reports heterogeneous designs and limits of mechanistic identification. It distinguishes reconsolidation, retrieval interference and extinction-like updating; positive clinical-analog outcomes cannot be automatically assigned to reconsolidation.

This evidence supports **careful experimental discrimination**, not therapeutic guarantees.

## 24. Pavlovian, instrumental, episodic, and autobiographical memory are different targets

Classical fear conditioning:
- defined CS/US and physiological responses;
- often short laboratory histories.

Instrumental conditioning:
- action contingencies and reinforcement.

Episodic memory:
- event-level recollection, source and contextual details.

Autobiographical/clinical memories:
- complex, distributed, repeatedly retrieved and reconstructed, embedded in meaning and social context.

Do not transport exact reactivation windows, retrieval criteria, or mechanism claims between these systems without tests. A change in emotional response to a memory is not necessarily forgetting its narrative content.

## 25. Extinction is not a verdict on agency or compulsion

A repeatedly evoked cue–response association can be influenced by value reappraisal, context, attention, motivation, inhibition, competing goals or changed environmental access.

Persistent response ≠ proof of immutable memory. Absent response ≠ proof of removal.

Do not infer psychiatric diagnoses from response recovery or its absence.

## 26. Experimental design: minimum controls

For an extinction/reconsolidation claim evaluate:
1. acquisition learning check;
2. comparable baseline conditioned responding;
3. CS+ versus control cue;
4. extinction dose and within-session curve;
5. delayed retention;
6. spontaneous recovery after a delay;
7. renewal after context switch;
8. reinstatement with US-only control;
9. reacquisition where ethical/appropriate;
10. proper no-reminder control;
11. intervention inside vs outside hypothesized time window;
12. intervention without retrieval;
13. prediction error and reminder structure manipulation;
14. memory age/strength stratification when claimed;
15. task awareness and state representation;
16. blinding, preregistration, allocation and exclusions;
17. measurement reliability and sensitivity;
18. generalization across task, context, population.

Not every study requires all 18, but omitted controls limit specific inferences.

## 27. Measurement matters: response, memory, interpretation

Measured physiological arousal can change because of habituation, autonomic variability, cognitive expectancy, and trial timing. Subjective fear ratings and contingency expectancy can dissociate from skin conductance.

Memory content, access, emotional reactivity, and behavioral choice are distinguishable endpoints.

Statement "the fear memory is gone" is stronger than "conditioned skin conductance response did not recover in this test."

## 28. Recovery tests are necessary but not infallible

To evaluate durability and scope:
- delay tests sufficiently and specify interval;
- vary relevant cues/context;
- check reinstatement and reacquisition where suitable;
- test original association knowledge;
- avoid excessive probes that themselves cause further extinction or learning.

Absence of relapse in one session is weak evidence for lifetime permanence.

## 29. Mechanism competition table

| Observation | Plausible interpretations | Overclaim blocked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reduced CR during CS-alone trials | Extinction, habituation, attention, changed expectation | Original memory erased |
| No CR after weeks | Strong inhibitory retrieval, revaluation, competing control, possible updating | Reconsolidation uniquely proven |
| Return after delay | Spontaneous recovery, context/temporal change | Person "never learned" extinction |
| Return in context A | Renewal; contextual inhibitory gating | New CS–US learning required |
| Return after unsignaled US | Reinstatement, context arousal/expectancy | Unique resurgence mechanism |
| Fast relearning | Retained prior structure, savings | Exact unchanged neural trace demonstrated |
| Reminder before improved extinction | Reminder-driven updating/attention/extinction and perhaps reconsolidation | Old memory rewritten |
| PE during reminder | Surprise, state inference, extinction, candidate destabilization | Reconsolidation triggered |
| Changed neural signal | Correlate of retrieval/learning/performance | Direct evidence of erased engram |

## 30. Extinction/Reconsolidation Inference Gate — 54 questions

### A. Target and outcome
1. Pavlovian, instrumental, episodic, or autobiographical memory?
2. What exactly is conditioned and what is measured?
3. Are response expression and memory retention separated?
4. Are outcome value, timing, identity and state representation specified?
5. Could task awareness alter responding?

### B. Acquisition/extinction
6. Was acquisition shown rather than assumed?
7. Is a CS− or other control available?
8. Is no-US/no-reward training duration recorded?
9. Is within-session change separated from delayed retention?
10. Could habituation or measurement artifact explain change?
11. Does extinction alter a prior association or add inhibitory information?
12. What evidence discriminates these?
13. Could a latent context/state shift explain learning?

### C. Return of responding
14. Was spontaneous recovery tested after an adequate delay?
15. Is temporal context comparable?
16. Were ABA/ABC/AAB renewal forms specified?
17. Were extinction and acquisition contexts matched appropriately?
18. Was reinstatement assessed with unsignaled-US controls?
19. Was reacquisition assessed separately from reinstatement?
20. Could resurgence of an alternative instrumental response explain a result?
21. Could probe trials themselves create new learning?

### D. Mechanistic evidence
22. Was a memory reminded or experimentally destabilized?
23. What independent sign of lability exists?
24. Was reminder/no-reminder comparison used?
25. Was intervention timing controlled?
26. Was postretrieval influence compared with no-retrieval intervention?
27. Were immediate and delayed effects separated?
28. Did the design rule out ordinary extinction?
29. Could strengthened inhibitory retrieval account for outcome?
30. Could retrieval interference/performance suppression account for outcome?

### E. Prediction error/condition
31. What type of PE is involved?
32. Is PE observed, induced, or simply inferred retrospectively?
33. Was the size/quality of mismatch independently manipulated?
34. Could PE cause new state inference rather than destabilization?
35. Are memory strength and age measured?
36. Was reminder structure standardized and reported?
37. Is an effective reconsolidation window independently supported?
38. Is the outcome specific to a memory rather than generalized arousal?

### F. Study quality and transport
39. Are the exclusion criteria transparent and prospective?
40. Is sample size/power adequate for target effect?
41. Is the protocol preregistered?
42. Was there independent replication?
43. Are outcome measurements reliable and convergent?
44. Does the benefit survive alternate context and time?
45. Is translation from species/task/setting justified?
46. Is a laboratory CR being overextended to clinical memory change?

### G. Final inference
47. Is behavioral suppression being confused with deletion?
48. Is no relapse being confused with permanent erasure?
49. Is a failure to replicate one protocol being confused with nonexistence of reconsolidation?
50. Does "retrieval" incorrectly stand for "destabilization"?
51. Is "prediction error" being used as a universal causal switch?
52. Are neural signals being overidentified as memory mechanisms?
53. What plausible alternative mechanism remains?
54. What precise task-bound conclusion survives all alternatives?

## 31. Failure modes

A. Extinction -> erasure: **invalid**.
B. No conscious urge -> original memory removed: **invalid**.
C. Return after extinction -> failed learning: **invalid**.
D. Time alone -> all spontaneous recovery explained: **overclaim**.
E. US re-exposure -> reconditioning only: **invalid**; reinstatement exists.
F. PE -> reconsolidation: **invalid**; PE participates in other updating.
G. Retrieval -> destabilization: **invalid**.
H. Retrieval + therapy -> trace rewriting: **invalid absent mechanism controls**.
I. Animal causal effects -> universally successful human protocol: **invalid translation**.
J. One failed replication -> reconsolidation disproved: **invalid generalization**.
K. Short-term no relapse -> durable permanent effect: **invalid**.
L. Physiological signal absent -> autobiographical content erased: **invalid**.
M. Context-dependent suppression -> globally fixed personality change: **invalid**.
N. Reconsolidation language as metaphor -> established neuroscientific explanation: **invalid**.

## Canonical inference rule

**Identify memory type and target response -> verify acquisition -> specify extinction manipulation and context -> test delayed recovery (time/context/US/reacquisition) -> distinguish retrieval from destabilization -> check PE and reminder boundary conditions -> compare inhibitory learning, state inference, retrieval interference and reconsolidation -> validate measurement and replication -> state only task-bound conclusion.**

Never: **response disappeared -> original association erased; remembered -> memory destabilized; mismatch -> reconsolidation; no relapse -> permanent rewriting**.
