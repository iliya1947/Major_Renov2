# 2. Canonical architecture: parent study and qualitative component are separate layers

The parent study and qualitative component answer different questions.

Example:

**Parent design**
randomized controlled trial.

**Qualitative component**
Interpretive Description interviews with a purposive trial subsample.

These labels must not be collapsed.

### Canonical record

parent study design
+
qualitative scientific purpose
+
qualitative methodology
+
sampling from parent study
+
timing
+
analysis
+
integration relationship
+
supported claim.

### Critical rule

“Nested in an RCT” is not itself a qualitative methodology.

---

# 3. What the parent trial establishes

If appropriately designed and analysed, a randomized trial can support causal estimates such as:

- intervention effect;
- arm contrast;
- treatment benefit/harm;
- intention-to-treat estimand.

The qualitative component may help understand:

- experience;
- implementation;
- acceptability;
- context;
- intervention receipt;
- trial conduct;
- plausible mechanisms;
- unexpected pathways;
- outcome relevance.

### Critical rule

The qualitative component does not inherit randomization.

An interviewee is not “randomized evidence” merely because they came from an RCT.

---

# 4. What embedded qualitative research can add to a trial

O'Cathain/Rooshenas frameworks show qualitative research can contribute to:

## Intervention

- develop/refine content;
- understand delivery;
- identify fixed vs adaptable components;
- explore acceptability.

## Trial design/conduct

- recruitment;
- equipoise;
- consent;
- retention;
- outcome selection;
- burden;
- usual-care characterization.

## Process

- implementation;
- fidelity/adaptation;
- recipient/provider response;
- context.

## Interpretation

- why outcomes may vary;
- unexpected effects;
- perceived mechanisms;
- theory refinement;
- future hypotheses.

# 5. Timing: before the definitive trial

Qualitative work before a definitive trial can address:

- whether the intervention makes sense;
- stakeholder priorities;
- current practice/usual care;
- language/materials;
- intervention components;
- outcome relevance;
- recruitment acceptability;
- randomization/equipoise;
- anticipated implementation barriers.

### Design role

This may be:

- intervention-development research;
- feasibility research;
- pre-trial qualitative study;
- co-design;
- pilot-process work.

### Critical rule

Pre-trial qualitative research is not evidence of intervention effectiveness.

---

# 6. Intervention-development qualitative work

The 2019 complex-intervention development guidance treats development as iterative.

Relevant qualitative contributions:

- understand needs/context;
- refine programme theory;
- identify stakeholders;
- explore feasibility/acceptability;
- prototype components;
- adapt delivery;
- anticipate implementation.

### Canonical representation

development programme
+
qualitative development study
+
stakeholder/co-design layer if present
+
later evaluation design.

### Rule

Development and evaluation must remain separate inferential stages.

---

# 7. Feasibility study vs pilot trial

Eldridge et al.:

## Feasibility study

Asks:

> Can this be done? Should we proceed? If so, how?

It may be predominantly qualitative.

## Pilot study

A subset of feasibility research in which the future study, or part of it, is run on a smaller scale.

### Critical rule

Qualitative feasibility work does not require a randomized pilot.

A major uncertainty may be answerable without randomization.

---

# 8. Qualitative work in feasibility studies

O'Cathain 2015 guidance emphasizes that qualitative research can address uncertainty about:

- intervention content/delivery;
- mechanisms;
- recruitment;
- randomization;
- consent;
- retention;
- outcome measures;
- implementation;
- usual care;
- future trial design.

### Dynamic approach

In feasibility phases, early qualitative findings may legitimately trigger changes to:

- recruitment procedures;
- information sheets;
- intervention delivery;
- training;
- outcome collection.

Further qualitative work can then examine whether the changes helped.

---

# 9. Dynamic qualitative work is not automatically appropriate in a definitive trial

In a definitive efficacy/effectiveness trial, ongoing qualitative feedback can itself alter:

- intervention delivery;
- participant behavior;
- recruiter behavior;
- adherence;
- contamination between arms.

O'Cathain guidance explicitly distinguishes the flexibility of feasibility work from the need to protect the experiment in a full trial.

### Project rule

Real-time formative feedback during a definitive trial must be classified as:

- trial-conduct intervention;
- implementation adaptation;
- process-evaluation feedback;

and its possible effect on trial integrity must be considered.

---

# 10. Qualitative work during a trial

During-trial qualitative components can examine:

- intervention as delivered rather than as written;
- fidelity;
- adaptation;
- dose/receipt;
- participant response;
- provider response;
- adherence problems;
- recruitment/retention;
- context change;
- unintended consequences.

### Important distinction

“During the trial” can mean:

1. data collected during delivery but analysed only later;
2. concurrent analysis with no feedback;
3. concurrent analysis with feedback/adaptation.

These have different risks for trial conduct.

---

# 11. Qualitative work after a trial

Post-trial qualitative work can examine:

- why participants think the intervention helped/did not help;
- what was acceptable;
- sustainment;
- reasons for adherence/nonadherence;
- unexpected outcomes;
- implementation implications;
- theory refinement.

### Critical rule

Post-trial accounts are retrospective.

They may be shaped by:

- knowledge of outcome;
- satisfaction;
- disappointment;
- recall;
- trial experience.

---

# 12. Trial-conduct qualitative research is distinct from intervention-process research

A trial contains at least two systems:

1. the intervention;
2. the research machinery evaluating it.

Qualitative study may target:

## Intervention process

What happened when intervention was delivered?

## Trial process

What happened when participants were:

- approached;
- consented;
- randomized;
- retained;
- followed up?

### Rule

A recruitment study such as QRI is not automatically an intervention process evaluation.

Its target can be trial conduct.

---

# 13. QuinteT Recruitment Intervention (QRI)

QRI demonstrates a special embedded qualitative architecture.

Phase 1:

- interviews with recruiters/trial staff;
- screening/recruitment logs;
- audio-recorded recruitment consultations;
- targeted interaction analysis.

Phase 2:

- feedback/training/action to improve recruitment and informed consent.

### Methodological lesson

Qualitative research can function as:

> diagnostic evidence about the trial itself

and then become part of an intervention to improve trial conduct.

### Critical boundary

Improved recruitment after QRI action is not automatically randomized evidence that QRI caused the improvement.

Separate evaluation is needed for effectiveness claims.

---

# 14. Qualitative evidence can affect trial decisions

The DARTSII example showed qualitative process evidence identifying severe participant confusion in one intervention arm, contributing to discontinuation of that arm.

### Project implication

Qualitative evidence is not limited to “nice context after the trial”.

It can reveal:

- invalid intervention assumptions;
- participant misunderstanding;
- harm/burden;
- protocol failure.

### Rule

The decision role of qualitative evidence must be explicitly reported.

---

# 15. Process evaluation is a purpose architecture, not a qualitative design

MRC process evaluation centers on:

1. implementation;
2. mechanisms of impact;
3. context.

It can use:

- quantitative measures;
- qualitative methods;
- mixed methods;
- routine data;
- observations;
- documents.

### Critical rule

“Qualitative process evaluation” still needs a qualitative methodology/analytic approach.

Process evaluation ≠ phenomenology / QD / case study / framework analysis.

---

# 16. Implementation

Qualitative process data can examine:

- what was delivered;
- how;
- by whom;
- adaptations;
- fidelity;
- reach;
- acceptability;
- barriers/facilitators.

### Strength

Qualitative methods can reveal why measured fidelity/dose metrics look the way they do.

### Example

Same attendance rate may reflect:

- enthusiasm;
- obligation;
- transport;
- family support;
- clinician pressure.

---

# 17. Mechanisms of impact

MRC guidance distinguishes:

- quantitative testing of prehypothesized pathways/moderators;
- qualitative exploration of experiences, emergent changes and unanticipated/complex causal pathways.

### Project rule

Qualitative process evidence can support:

- perceived mechanism;
- experienced mechanism;
- plausible mechanism;
- programme-theory refinement.

It does not by itself estimate:

- causal mediator effect;
- natural indirect effect;
- treatment × mediator estimand.

---

# 18. Programme theory and logic model

Qualitative findings can:

- confirm assumptions;
- reveal missing mechanisms;
- show pathway failure;
- identify contextual conditions;
- refine the logic model.

### Canonical cycle

programme theory
→ trial/process data
→ qualitative interpretation
→ revised programme theory
→ future test.

### Rule

A revised logic model is a scientific product.

It is not equivalent to demonstrated causal mechanism.

---

# 19. Context

Qualitative work is especially useful for intervention–context interaction.

Possible context:

- staffing;
- setting;
- culture;
- policy;
- resources;
- social support;
- physical environment;
- competing services;
- season;
- organizational workflow.

### Critical rule

Context is not merely a list of background variables.

Qualitative process work can examine how context changes what intervention components mean or how they are enacted.

---

# 20. Sampling from a trial

Qualitative sampling can be purposive across:

- intervention/control arms;
- sites;
- age/sex/experience;
- high/low adherence;
- completers/dropouts;
- outcome responders/nonresponders;
- high/low fidelity settings;
- decliners;
- staff/providers.

### Critical rule

Sampling rationale must follow the qualitative question.

The trial's quantitative sample size does not determine qualitative sample adequacy.

---

# 21. Sampling only the intervention arm

This can be coherent for questions like:

- how was the intervention experienced?;
- which components were acceptable?;
- what barriers affected engagement?

### Limitation

It cannot distinguish intervention-specific experience from:

- general disease trajectory;
- study participation;
- contact with researchers;
- usual-care experiences.

### Rule

Do not imply control-arm comparison when only intervention participants were interviewed.

---

# 22. Including the control arm

Control-arm qualitative data can help explore:

- usual care;
- trial experience;
- contamination;
- co-intervention;
- general contextual change;
- experiences unrelated to intervention.

### Critical boundary

A small purposive qualitative arm comparison does not inherit the RCT's quantitative unbiased treatment-effect comparison.

It can support contrastive interpretation, not effect-size estimation.

---

# 23. Sampling by measured trial outcome

Researchers may select:

- responders;
- nonresponders;
- improvers;
- deteriorators;
- high/low adherence.

This can generate useful explanatory contrasts.

### Value

Ask:

> How do experiences differ across measured trajectories?

### Risk

Outcome-conditioned accounts may be shaped by the known outcome.

### Critical rule

This can generate hypotheses about treatment response.

It does not establish:

- treatment-effect heterogeneity;
- causal moderator;
- mediator;
- personalized-treatment rule.

Those require separate quantitative/causal testing.

---

# 24. Sampling dropouts and decliners

People who:

- declined the trial;
- refused randomization;
- discontinued intervention;
- withdrew from follow-up;

may hold information absent from completers.

### Value

Can illuminate:

- recruitment barriers;
- burden;
- acceptability;
- intervention mismatch;
- follow-up problems.

### Critical practical issue

These groups are often hardest to recruit for qualitative follow-up.

### Rule

Completer-only qualitative samples can systematically overstate acceptability.

---

# 25. Sampling providers and implementers

Providers can reveal:

- feasibility;
- fidelity;
- adaptations;
- workability;
- training needs;
- organizational constraints.

### Critical rule

Provider perceptions should not substitute for recipient experience.

Multiple stakeholder groups answer different questions.

---

# 26. Site sampling

Multi-site trials may purposively sample:

- high recruitment sites;
- low recruitment sites;
- high/low fidelity sites;
- urban/rural settings;
- mature/new services.

### Strength

Can identify contextual variation.

### Rule

If site is an important explanatory unit, analysis must preserve site context rather than pool all interviews immediately.

---

# 27. Timing within an intervention

Possible interview points:

## Before exposure

expectations, acceptability, baseline beliefs.

## Early exposure

first-use barriers, learning demands.

## Mid-intervention

adaptation, adherence, emerging mechanisms.

## Post-intervention

overall experience, perceived impact.

## Follow-up

maintenance/sustainability.

### Rule

Timing should follow the process question.

“Interviewed after the trial” is not neutral if the question concerns early implementation problems.

---

# 28. Longitudinal nested qualitative components

A trial may include repeated qualitative interviews.

Example:

REACT:
- parent pragmatic RCT;
- purposive qualitative subsample;
- interviews at 6, 12, 24 months;
- Framework Analysis;
- motivation/support needs tracked through time.

### Project representation

RCT
+
longitudinal qualitative component
+
qualitative methodology/analysis
+
trial integration purpose.

### Rule

Longitudinal qualitative inference remains qualitative.

---

# 29. Embedded vs parallel qualitative component

## Embedded/integrated component

Qualitative work is designed as part of the parent research programme and informs:

- trial design;
- delivery;
- process interpretation;
- integration.

## Parallel/linked component

Qualitative work studies the same intervention/population but has limited operational or analytic integration.

### Critical rule

“Same grant/trial participants” does not prove methodological integration.

---

# 30. Embedded does not automatically mean mixed methods

A study can have:

- RCT dataset;
- qualitative interviews;

and still analyze/report them independently.

### Mixed-method integration requires intentional interdependence.

Examples:

- quantitative results guide qualitative sampling;
- qualitative findings guide quantitative analysis;
- joint displays;
- triangulation protocol;
- data transformation;
- following a thread;
- integrated meta-inference.

### Rule

Co-location ≠ integration.

---

# 31. Mixed-method integration levels

Integration can occur at:

1. design;
2. sampling;
3. data collection;
4. analysis;
5. interpretation/reporting.

### Example: design-level

Qualitative questions preplanned to explain implementation.

### Example: sampling-level

Select interviewees based on quantitative outcome profiles.

### Example: analysis-level

Create joint display linking themes to measured outcomes.

### Example: interpretation-level

Use both strands to refine programme theory.

---

# 32. Joint displays

A joint display places qualitative and quantitative findings side-by-side or in an integrated matrix.

Possible structure:

- outcome change;
- adherence;
- qualitative explanation;
- context;
- inferred hypothesis.

### Strength

Makes:

- convergence;
- divergence;
- silence;
- conditional patterns;

visible.

### Critical rule

A joint display is an integration technique, not proof that resulting hypotheses are causal.

---

# 33. Triangulation protocol

An integration protocol may compare datasets and code relations as:

- agreement;
- partial agreement;
- dissonance;
- silence.

### Scientific value

Dissonance can reveal:

- measurement mismatch;
- overlooked mechanism;
- subgroup differences;
- competing interpretations.

### Rule

Mixed-method dissonance should not be “resolved” automatically by privileging quantitative or qualitative data.

---

# 34. Following a thread

An initial finding in one strand can trigger focused analysis in another.

Example:

Qualitative finding:
participants who dislike intervention philosophy disengage.

Then quantitative data can be inspected for:

- adherence;
- outcome;
- treatment preference;

where appropriately measured.

### Critical rule

Post-hoc following-a-thread analyses are often exploratory.

They require confirmation in independent/future analysis before strong inference.

---

# 35. Data transformation

Qualitative data may be transformed into categorized variables or quantitative data used inside integrated analysis.

Quantitative profiles may also be used to organize qualitative cases.

### Risk

Transformation can strip:

- context;
- ambiguity;
- narrative complexity.

### Rule

Preserve the original qualitative interpretation alongside transformed variables where possible.

---

# 36. Trial outcome + qualitative explanation

A common temptation:

> Trial found no effect. Interviews explain why.

Better calibrated statement:

> Qualitative data identify implementation, experience or contextual findings that may help interpret the null result and generate hypotheses about why the intended pathway was not realized.

### Critical rule

A post-hoc qualitative explanation does not prove the causal reason the trial was null.

---

# 37. Trial effect + perceived mechanism

If trial shows benefit and participants report:

> accountability helped me exercise,

the combined evidence supports:

- benefit occurred in trial;
- accountability was perceived as important;
- accountability is a plausible mechanism.

It does not prove:

- accountability mediated the treatment effect.

Formal mediation requires a causal mediation design/analysis.

---

# 38. Heterogeneity and personalization hypotheses

Integrated qual–quant analysis can identify candidate treatment-response patterns.

Example:

participants whose worldview fits the intervention report better engagement and have better outcomes.

This can generate:

> candidate tailoring hypothesis.

### Critical rule

Small outcome-stratified qualitative groups are exploratory.

Do not derive a clinical treatment-selection rule without prospective validation.

---

# 39. Blinding and qualitative researchers

Qualitative researchers often cannot be blind to:

- intervention identity;
- interview context;
- participant descriptions.

However, some analytic steps can sometimes be organized blind to:

- quantitative outcome;
- measured responder category;
- trial group;

where scientifically useful.

### Value

May reduce outcome-driven interpretive bias.

### Rule

Blinding is optional/context-dependent, not a universal qualitative quality requirement.

---

# 40. Reactivity and contamination

Qualitative interviews can themselves affect participants.

Possible effects:

- reflection;
- motivation;
- behavior change;
- adherence;
- disclosure to clinicians;
- enhanced attention.

### Trial consequence

If interviews are concentrated in one arm during treatment, they may act as an additional intervention.

### Mitigation options

- balanced contact across arms where appropriate;
- post-intervention interviews;
- less intrusive diaries;
- explicit monitoring;
- separation of formative feedback from definitive outcomes.

### Rule

Do not assume qualitative data collection is behaviorally inert.

---

# 41. Team integration and independence

Strong trial–qualitative collaboration helps:

- identify useful questions;
- access trial data;
- time interviews;
- act on feasibility findings.

But excessive dependence can create:

- pressure to defend intervention;
- reluctance to report negative findings;
- selective interpretation.

### Project extraction

Record:

- qualitative team relationship to trial team;
- intervention developers in analysis?;
- independence where relevant;
- pathways for reporting unwelcome findings.

---

# 42. Intervention developer conflict

In feasibility/development work, intervention developers may have strong prior commitment to the intervention.

O'Cathain guidance explicitly notes that teams must remain open to qualitative evidence showing:

- fundamental intervention weakness;
- unacceptable delivery;
- trial infeasibility.

### Rule

Qualitative research loses value if it is allowed only to optimize, never to falsify assumptions.

---

# 43. Feasibility progression decisions

Qualitative findings can inform:

- go;
- amend;
- stop;

decisions.

Examples:

- recruitment acceptable?;
- intervention acceptable?;
- outcome burden tolerable?;
- randomization understandable?;
- delivery feasible?

### Critical rule

If qualitative criteria influence progression, this should be prespecified where possible or transparently justified if emergent.

---

# 44. Intervention refinement

Qualitative findings can trigger:

- language changes;
- new support materials;
- changed delivery frequency;
- improved training;
- removed components;
- adapted recruitment.

### Rule

Refinement evidence supports:

> this version better fits stakeholders/context.

It does not prove:

> this refinement improves health outcome.

---

# 45. Cohort-embedded qualitative studies

Qualitative studies can draw participants from observational cohorts.

Advantages:

- pre-existing longitudinal measurements;
- known exposures/outcomes;
- trajectory-based sampling;
- contextual linkage.

Example selection:

- high vs low activity trajectories;
- injured vs noninjured;
- maintained vs lost function.

### Critical rule

The qualitative component remains a qualitative study.

Cohort measurement does not turn interview themes into epidemiologic effect estimates.

---

# 46. Cohort trajectory sampling

Measured cohort trajectories can guide purposive sampling.

Example:

select:
- persistent high activity;
- declining activity;
- increasing activity;
- persistently inactive.

Then ask:

> How do participants explain and experience these trajectories?

### Strength

Creates meaningful contrast.

### Risk

Outcome-based selection can encourage post-hoc storytelling.

### Rule

Use qualitative accounts to explain/interpret trajectories, not to estimate trajectory causes.

---

# 47. Parent-data linkage

Linking interview data to parent-study variables can enable richer analysis.

Potential linked variables:

- assigned arm;
- attendance;
- adherence;
- outcome trajectory;
- site;
- baseline characteristics.

### Ethical requirement

Participants must be appropriately informed about linkage/use.

### Analytic rule

Linked variables should be used deliberately rather than mining many outcome-based subgroups until a plausible story appears.

---

# 48. Process evaluation before outcome unblinding

Where possible, some qualitative/process hypotheses can be generated before seeing final arm outcome results.

Potential advantage:

- reduces hindsight explanation;
- documents programme theory prospectively.

### Rule

Pre-outcome analysis is not always feasible or desirable, but timing should be reported because it affects interpretation.

---

# 49. Process evaluation after outcome results

Knowing the trial result can help target questions.

But it can also create:

- confirmation bias;
- outcome-driven interpretation.

### Project extraction

Record:

- when qualitative analysis occurred relative to trial results;
- whether analysts knew outcomes;
- whether programme theory was prespecified.

---

# 50. Fitness / exercise crosswalk

| Research structure | Correct routing |
|---|---|
| RCT tests whether exercise programme improves function | parent RCT causal-effect design |
| Interviews explore intervention experience | embedded qualitative component |
| Interviews with intervention arm only about acceptability | valid acceptability study; no arm comparison |
| Interviews from both arms about trial experience/usual care | comparative qualitative interpretation |
| High vs low adherence participants purposively interviewed | outcome/trajectory contrast; hypothesis generation |
| Responders vs nonresponders interviewed | explanatory contrast; not effect-modifier proof |
| Process evaluation measures fidelity + interviews providers/users | process-evaluation layer + mixed/qual methods |
| Focus groups before full exercise trial refine intervention | feasibility/development qualitative study |
| Coaches receive real-time feedback from qualitative findings during pilot | dynamic formative feasibility approach |
| Same feedback introduced mid-definitive RCT | trial-conduct adaptation; potential contamination |
| Participant says accountability caused activity increase | perceived mechanism, not causal mediation |
| RCT benefit + qualitative accountability theme | plausible mechanism requiring formal testing |
| Trial outcome and themes combined in joint display | mixed-method integration |
| Trial and qualitative paper reported separately with no common analysis | parallel/linked, not necessarily mixed methods |
| Cohort members selected by activity trajectories for interviews | cohort-embedded purposive qualitative study |

# 51. Fitness exemplar: HERO trial

The HERO trial compared home-based graded exercise plus usual care versus usual care in older adults with frailty.

Its embedded process evaluation used:

- observations;
- interviews;
- therapy records;
- exercise diaries.

Primary process targets included:

- fidelity;
- acceptability;
- implementation.

### Methodological lesson

Trial outcome and process evidence answer different questions.

Process data can explain:

- how intervention was delivered;
- where adaptation occurred;
- why engagement differed;

without replacing the trial effect estimate.

---

# 52. Fitness exemplar: STRENGTH cardiac rehabilitation trial

A 2026 process evaluation was nested in a two-arm cluster RCT.

Qualitative data included:

- interviews from intervention and control groups;
- intervention focus groups.

Quantitative process data included:

- attendance;
- diaries;
- self-efficacy/PA measures.

### Methodological lesson

Sampling both arms can broaden contextual interpretation.

But qualitative arm comparisons are not equivalent to the randomized outcome comparison.

---

# 53. Fitness exemplar: VEGA exercise RCT

A qualitative interview study was embedded in a multicenter pragmatic RCT of community-based exercise for young adults with severe mental illness.

The RCT evaluates effectiveness.

The qualitative component explores:

- embodied recovery;
- lived experience;
- meaning not captured by outcome scales.

### Critical distinction

# 59. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| parent RCT vs qualitative methodology | separate design layers |
| trial participant vs randomized qualitative evidence | qualitative interpretation does not inherit randomization |
| nested in RCT vs mixed methods | intentional integration is required |
| process evaluation vs qualitative design | process evaluation is purpose architecture |
| intervention development vs efficacy evaluation | different stages/claims |
| feasibility study vs pilot trial | pilot is a subset of feasibility research |
| interviews during trial vs process evaluation | purpose must concern process/implementation/context |
| intervention arm interview study vs arm comparison | no comparator qualitative evidence |
| both arms interviewed vs causal effect comparison | purposive qualitative contrast ≠ randomized effect estimate |
| responders vs nonresponders vs treatment-effect heterogeneity | exploratory contrast ≠ causal moderator |
| perceived mechanism vs causal mediation | participant explanation ≠ indirect-effect identification |
| programme theory vs proven mechanism | theory requires testing |
| fidelity measure vs participant experience | different evidence types |
| real-time qualitative feedback vs passive observation | feedback can change trial/intervention |
| feasibility optimization vs definitive-trial adaptation | contamination risk differs |
| post-trial explanation vs proof of why trial result occurred | often exploratory/hindsight-sensitive |
| trial outcome + matching theme vs mediator proof | consistency does not identify mediation |
| quantitative/qualitative papers side by side vs integration | co-publication ≠ mixed methods |
| joint display vs causal proof | integration organizes evidence, not identification |
| cohort trajectory sampling vs causal trajectory explanation | purposive contrast remains qualitative |
| intervention developer involvement vs neutral interpretation | reflexivity/independence needs consideration |
| completer sample vs intervention acceptability population | dropouts/decliners may differ systematically |
