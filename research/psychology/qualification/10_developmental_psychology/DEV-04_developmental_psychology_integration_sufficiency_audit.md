# DEV-04 — Developmental psychology integration/sufficiency audit

Status: **COMPLETE_FOR_CURRENT_SCOPE — 20/20 PASS**
**Domain decision: DEV sequence COMPLETE_FOR_CURRENT_SCOPE (DEV-01…04).**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public developmental-science evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## 1. Purpose and inputs

This block integrates and audits:

1. **DEV-01** — developmental clocks, chronological age versus maturation, puberty status/timing/tempo, sensitive/critical periods, continuity, plasticity, cohort/period effects, design and measurement;
2. **DEV-02** — person↔environment transactions, cascades, family/peer/school/community contexts, cumulative/timing hypotheses, susceptibility models, equifinality/multifinality and causal identification;
3. **DEV-03** — adolescence, transition to adulthood, midlife, role transitions, retirement, cognitive/social aging, healthy aging, terminal decline and lifespan measurement.

The purpose is not to claim that developmental psychology is complete.

The purpose is to determine whether the current foundation is sufficient to prevent the major developmental-inference errors that would otherwise contaminate psychological reasoning across age and life stage.

## 2. Integrated developmental scaffold

A generic developmental inference can be organized as:

\`developmental clock/timescale -> maturation/exposure/role context -> person↔environment transaction -> repeated state/behavior change -> cross-domain or within-domain trajectory -> measurement/selection process -> functional outcome\`

This is a reasoning scaffold, not a universal causal chain.

Different processes can enter at different points:
- maturation can change opportunity/sensitivity;
- environments can alter exposure;
- persons can select/evoke environments;
- role transitions can reorganize routines;
- practice and attrition can alter observed trajectories;
- disease/terminal processes can overlap with normal aging.

## 3. Chronological age is not a mechanism

DEV-01's strongest invariant remains:

**chronological age is a coordinate/index, not a causal explanation.**

Age can covary with:
- biological maturation;
- accumulated learning;
- schooling;
- role transitions;
- historical period;
- cohort;
- health burden.

A valid explanation names the process.

## 4. Developmental clock must match the process

Possible clocks include:
- chronological age;
- pubertal stage/status;
- pubertal timing;
- pubertal tempo;
- time since exposure;
- time in role;
- distance to death;
- cumulative practice.

Using one clock for every developmental question creates construct error.

## 5. Maturation is not chronological age

DEV-01 showed that:
- pubertal status;
- timing;
- tempo;
- hormonal/physical indicators

can diverge from age.

Current puberty evidence reinforces the need for repeated, multimodal measurement.

Therefore:
**age-adjustment does not replace maturation measurement**.

## 6. Sensitive periods are conditional hypotheses

A sensitive-period claim must specify:
- system;
- exposure;
- timing;
- duration;
- outcome;
- comparison period.

The 2026 systematic review of 43 neuroimaging studies found:
- some earlier-onset/longer-duration associations;
- mixed evidence for discrete sensitive windows;
- substantial method/construct heterogeneity.

Therefore:
**sensitive period ≠ fixed age calendar**.

## 7. Sensitive and critical periods remain distinct

Critical-period claims are stronger:
- narrower;
- more necessary-input-like;
- stronger loss of plasticity outside the window.

Human psychological evidence usually supports more cautious sensitive-period language.

## 8. Plasticity is not benefit

Plasticity describes capacity to change.

It can amplify:
- enrichment;
- adversity.

It does not specify:
- direction;
- adaptiveness;
- reversibility.

Higher plasticity is neither automatically healthier nor unlimited.

## 9. Timing must be separated from duration and dose

Earlier exposure often means:
- longer exposure;
- greater cumulative dose.

Without separation:
**early timing effect**
can be a duration effect.

DEV-02 therefore requires direct comparison of:
- timing;
- recurrence;
- duration;
- cumulative burden;
- exposure type.

## 10. Cumulative-risk counts are summaries, not mechanisms

An ACE/risk count can predict outcomes.

It does not encode:
- severity;
- chronicity;
- developmental timing;
- exposure dependence;
- causal pathway.

Count is a burden index, not biological dose.

## 11. Transaction is the default developmental interaction model

Development is not:
**environment acts on passive person**.

Nor:
**person alone creates environment**.

DEV-02 established reciprocal:
- selection;
- evocation;
- response;
- reinforcement.

Person↔environment transaction is the more defensible default.

## 12. Reciprocal paths are not automatically causal

Cross-lagged evidence can be consistent with reciprocal development.

But shared:
- family context;
- reporter method;
- genetics;
- time-varying stress

can generate apparent reciprocal paths.

Model form does not create causal identification.

## 13. Family effects are multi-process

"Parenting" can refer to:
- sensitivity;
- warmth;
- hostility;
- discipline;
- conflict;
- resources.

DEV-02 therefore prohibits:
**family association = unique parent causal mechanism**.

Child-driven effects can coexist.

## 14. Peer similarity is not peer influence

Similarity can arise from:
- selection;
- socialization;
- common context.

Longitudinal and experimental peer-influence estimates answer different questions and operate on different timescales.

Therefore:
**similar peers ≠ demonstrated socialization**.

## 15. School context is not one level

Separate:
- individual perceived belonging;
- classroom norms;
- school climate;
- institutional composition.

An individual-level perception should not be interpreted as a school-level causal effect.

## 16. Developmental cascades require evidence for each link

The 2025 cascade review sets a high causal bar.

A valid cascade claim needs support for:
- temporal order;
- each link;
- relevant timescale;
- competing paths;
- mechanism/mediation.

A chain of significant regressions is insufficient.

## 17. Cascade and transaction can coexist

A developmental process can be both:
- cross-domain;
- reciprocal.

Example scaffold:
\`skill A -> environment response -> skill B -> changed opportunities -> later A/B\`.

Therefore cascades need not be strictly linear.

## 18. Equifinality blocks backward certainty

Equifinality means:
different developmental histories can produce similar outcomes.

Thus one adult outcome cannot uniquely reconstruct:
- one childhood cause;
- one family pathway;
- one sensitive-period exposure.

Backward inference remains uncertain.

## 19. Multifinality blocks forward destiny

Multifinality means:
the same early exposure can lead to different later outcomes.

Thus:
**early adversity = future pathology**
is invalid.

Risk changes probability, not destiny.

## 20. Differential susceptibility is not a default law

DEV-02 separated:
- diathesis–stress;
- vantage sensitivity;
- differential susceptibility.

The 2025 four-cohort IPD analysis (>30,000 children) more often favored:
- additive/no-interaction models.

Therefore:
**sensitivity interaction must beat simpler models**.

## 21. "Sensitive child" is not a natural type

Sensitivity markers can be:
- temperament;
- reactivity;
- questionnaire scores;
- biological indicators.

No current marker establishes a universal fixed developmental type.

Susceptibility can be:
- outcome-specific;
- environment-specific.

## 22. Protective factor is not mechanism

A factor associated with better outcomes under adversity can be:
- promotive;
- buffering;
- marker of resources;
- causal mechanism.

These are different claims.

Moderation itself does not identify mechanism.

## 23. Developmental stage is not a mechanism

Labels such as:
- adolescence;
- emerging adulthood;
- midlife;
- later adulthood

summarize recurring patterns.

They do not explain them.

The mechanism may involve:
- puberty;
- institutions;
- autonomy;
- role demands;
- health;
- historical context.

## 24. Adolescence is not "unfinished brain"

DEV-03 rejects neural reductionism.

Adolescent behavior depends on:
- maturation;
- peer context;
- norms;
- opportunity;
- reward;
- emotion;
- identity;
- institutions.

Neural findings can contribute but do not replace behavioral/contextual explanation.

## 25. Identity remains developmental and domain-specific

The 2026 identity review emphasizes:
- commitments;
- exploration;
- reconsideration;
- personal/social identity;
- variation across life domains and time.

Therefore:
**identity is not completed once adulthood begins**.

## 26. Psychological adulthood has no single universal age

Adult transition markers include:
- legal;
- biological;
- socioeconomic;
- relational;
- subjective.

They can diverge substantially.

No one age threshold establishes psychological adulthood.

## 27. Emerging adulthood is context-sensitive

It can describe:
- exploration;
- instability;
- partial independence

in some contexts.

It is not a universal biological stage.

Cohort/culture/economy alter:
- housing;
- education;
- employment;
- partnership timing.

## 28. Structural delay is not psychological immaturity

Later:
- employment stability;
- home independence;
- parenthood

can reflect institutional/economic conditions.

Do not individualize structural timing.

## 29. Midlife is not a universal crisis

DEV-03 integrated current cross-national evidence showing:
- role density;
- gains;
- stressors;
- historically varying trends.

Midlife cannot be defined by one crisis narrative.

## 30. Historical context can mimic age effects

If midlife outcomes change in one country/cohort but not another:
- period;
- policy;
- labor;
- inequality

become plausible explanations.

Age alone is insufficient.

## 31. Role transitions are heterogeneous exposures

Career change, caregiving, retirement and partnership transitions vary in:
- voluntariness;
- quality;
- duration;
- resources;
- prior context.

A role label is not a standardized treatment.

## 32. Retirement is not uniformly benefit or harm

DEV-03 showed average improvements in some longitudinal retirement research.

But effects vary by:
- voluntariness;
- timing;
- work quality;
- health selection.

Therefore:
**retirement status alone is not a developmental mechanism**.

## 33. Lifespan development is multidirectional

Across age:
- some capacities increase;
- some stabilize;
- some decline;
- some compensate.

Development is not one monotonic trajectory toward gain or loss.

## 34. Cognitive aging is domain-specific

Cognition should be decomposed into:
- processing speed;
- episodic memory;
- semantic knowledge;
- working memory;
- reasoning;
- executive functions.

One global "cognitive age" is usually too coarse.

## 35. Average cognitive decline is not individual decline

The 2026 healthy-cognitive-aging review emphasizes large interindividual heterogeneity.

Chronological age alone is a poor predictor of cognitive health.

Population curves are priors, not prognoses.

## 36. Cross-sectional aging is not longitudinal aging

Cross-sectional differences can reflect:
- cohort;
- education;
- health selection;
- historical test familiarity.

They do not measure within-person decline.

## 37. Longitudinal aging is also biased unless corrected

Repeated testing can improve performance.
Attrition can remove lower-performing participants.
Selective survival changes the observed sample.

Therefore:
**longitudinal = unbiased age trajectory**
is also invalid.

## 38. Practice effects can be nuisance and signal

Practice effects can:
- inflate apparent maintenance/growth;
- index learning capacity.

They should be modeled, not automatically ignored or removed.

## 39. Attrition is developmental selection

Longitudinal dropout can depend on:
- cognition;
- health;
- mobility;
- resources;
- burden.

Observed later-life samples are therefore selected populations.

## 40. Selective survival is part of late-life inference

Older samples increasingly reflect:
- who survived;
- who remained healthy enough;
- who remained testable.

Late-life averages are conditional on survival/participation.

## 41. Healthy aging is functional, not disease-free

WHO defines healthy ageing through:
**functional ability enabling wellbeing**.

Functional ability arises from:
- intrinsic capacity;
- environment;
- their interaction.

Disease absence is not required.

## 42. Capacity is not function

A person can have:
- reduced intrinsic capacity;
but
- preserved functional ability

through:
- assistive environment;
- social support;
- adaptation.

This is a core person×environment boundary.

## 43. Healthy aging is not one score

Healthy-aging frameworks differ in:
- disease;
- cognition;
- activity;
- autonomy;
- wellbeing;
- functional ability.

One composite should not be reified as the construct.

## 44. Normal cognitive aging is not dementia

NIA and aging science distinguish:
- common mild age-related change;
- MCI;
- dementia.

Dementia is not inevitable aging.

Clinical diagnosis requires separate criteria.

## 45. MCI is not inevitable dementia

MCI increases concern/risk.

It does not guarantee progression.

Individual trajectories can:
- remain stable;
- improve;
- worsen.

Prognosis is probabilistic.

## 46. Subjective complaint is not diagnosis

Memory concern can reflect:
- mood;
- sleep;
- medication;
- stress;
- health;
- normal aging;
- cognitive impairment.

Assessment is required.

## 47. Terminal decline is not ordinary chronological aging

Distance-to-death can explain part of late-life steepening in:
- cognition;
- physical function;
- social/psychological outcomes.

Chronological age and terminal process should be separated where relevant.

## 48. Terminal decline is outcome-specific

Different functions can show:
- different onset;
- different slope.

There is no one universal terminal-decline clock.

## 49. Social aging is not social loss

Network composition can change with age.

Smaller network size can reflect:
- selective retention of close ties;
- loss;
- mobility/health constraints.

Network size alone cannot identify mechanism or loneliness.

## 50. Loneliness is subjective mismatch

Loneliness concerns discrepancy between:
- desired;
- actual connection.

It is not a count of relationships.

A small network can coexist with low loneliness.

## 51. Older age does not guarantee better emotion regulation

Current review evidence is mixed:
- advantages;
- null effects;
- reversals.

Context and strategy matter.

Age is not the regulation mechanism.

## 52. Culture and historical context remain developmental variables

Development occurs within:
- institutions;
- policies;
- family systems;
- education;
- technology;
- cultural norms.

A developmental trajectory estimated in one culture/cohort may not transport.

## 53. Age–period–cohort remains underidentified without assumptions

Identity:

\`age = period - cohort\`.

All three cannot be freely estimated.

Model conclusions depend on identifying restrictions.

This remains a central developmental boundary.

## 54. Measurement invariance is necessary but insufficient

Longitudinal and age-group comparisons require measurement comparability.

But invariance does not prove:
- construct identity;
- mechanistic continuity.

A measure can be invariant and conceptually poor.

## 55. Heterotypic continuity can require changing measures

The same function can manifest differently across age.

Using age-specific measures may improve construct validity.

But it creates linking/calibration problems.

Developmental measurement must balance:
- comparability;
- developmental appropriateness.

## 56. Same behavior can reflect different mechanisms

Across age, one observable behavior can arise from:
- different capacities;
- motives;
- roles;
- constraints.

Surface homotypy does not prove mechanistic continuity.

## 57. Different behaviors can reflect the same function

A stable function can be expressed differently as:
- language;
- motor ability;
- roles;
- social expectations

change.

Heterotypic continuity remains a functional hypothesis.

## 58. Development is not improvement

A developmental change can be:
- adaptive;
- maladaptive;
- neutral.

Direction requires a criterion.

"More mature" should not be used as automatic praise.

## 59. Stability is not success

Stable pathology is not healthy.
Stable function can be adaptive.

Developmental success cannot be inferred from stability alone.

## 60. Cross-domain routing

1. **COG/EXE:** specific cognitive/executive processes.
2. **LEA:** learning mechanisms and experience-dependent updating.
3. **MOT:** goals, incentives, effort and intentional action.
4. **AFF/STR:** emotion/stress dynamics and regulation.
5. **SOC:** roles, norms, identity and social interaction.
6. **PER:** stable individual differences and personality change.
7. **DEV:** timing, developmental clocks, transactions, trajectories, life-stage context and aging.
8. **NEU:** neural/biological mechanisms and neuroscience inference.
9. **PSYCHOMETRICS:** measurement construction, reliability, invariance and score interpretation.
10. **CLN/NDEV:** developmental psychopathology, diagnosis, neurodevelopmental conditions.
11. **BHC:** intervention/behavior-change efficacy.

DEV should not absorb neural or clinical mechanism claims merely because they are age-related.

## 62. Red-team inference cases

**Case A — older adolescents score higher on a task.**
Possible explanations: maturation, schooling, practice, cohort, measurement. Age difference alone does not identify development mechanism.

**Case B — early adversity predicts adult outcome.**
Could reflect timing, duration, recurrence, selection, later exposures, shared causes. Early exposure does not uniquely establish sensitive-period causality.

**Case C — child and parent behaviors predict one another across waves.**
Supports reciprocal temporal association. Does not alone prove causal transaction.

**Case D — similar peers become more similar.**
Could reflect selection, influence, shared context. Similarity is not socialization proof.

**Case E — high-risk trajectory class.**
The class is model-dependent; it is not a natural developmental type.

**Case F — adult transition happens later than historical norm.**
Could reflect education, labor/housing context, culture. Does not establish immaturity.

**Case G — older adult has lower memory score.**
Need age norms, trajectory, practice, health, function and clinical assessment before pathology inference.

**Case H — older person's social network is smaller.**
Does not establish loneliness or social deterioration.

**Case I — sharp decline shortly before death.**
May reflect terminal processes rather than generic age effect.

## 65. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- chronological age is not a developmental mechanism;
- maturation, exposure timing and role timing are distinct developmental clocks;
- sensitive/critical periods are distinct and system-specific;
- development is transactional and multilevel;
- cascades require evidence beyond temporal correlation;
- cumulative burden/timing/type are distinct exposure hypotheses;
- differential susceptibility is not a default law;
- equifinality/multifinality prevent deterministic inference;
- stage labels are descriptive, not causal;
- lifespan development is multidirectional;
- cognitive aging is domain-specific and heterogeneous;
- healthy aging is functional and person×environment;
- normal aging is not dementia;
- practice, attrition, survival and cohort can distort observed trajectories.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- puberty can explain variation beyond chronological age;
- specific sensitive periods can exist for specific systems/exposures;
- parent/child and peer transactions can operate reciprocally;
- cross-domain developmental cascades can occur;
- some susceptibility interactions fit specific outcomes/environments;
- adulthood role transitions can alter trajectories;
- some people maintain high cognitive performance in later life;
- environments can compensate for capacity loss.

### Not established as universal
- one developmental clock;
- one adolescent sensitive period;
- one dominant family/peer pathway;
- one cumulative-risk mechanism;
- one susceptible-child class;
- one adulthood transition sequence;
- one midlife crisis/peak;
- one retirement effect;
- one cognitive-aging trajectory;
- one healthy-aging phenotype;
- one terminal-decline onset.

## Canonical operating rule

**Define the developmental process and timescale -> choose the relevant clock rather than treating chronological age as mechanism -> separate maturation, exposure timing/duration, role transition, cohort and period -> model reciprocal person–environment transactions and competing cumulative/timing/cascade hypotheses -> test measurement comparability, practice, attrition and survival -> distinguish stage labels from mechanisms and healthy variation from pathology -> preserve equifinality/multifinality and individual heterogeneity -> state only the narrowest supported developmental inference.**

Never:
**age = cause; age = maturity; early = destiny; sensitive = critical; cascade = causation; longitudinal = causal; interaction = susceptible type; stage = mechanism; midlife = crisis; older = impaired; decline = dementia; healthy aging = disease-free; population trajectory = personal prognosis.**
