# SOC-04 — Social identity, group norms, conformity, social influence, intergroup contact, cooperation/competition, and measurement boundaries

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public social-psychology evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

SOC-01 established social inference under uncertainty.
SOC-02 separated empathy-related processes.
SOC-03 separated categorization, stereotypes, prejudice, indirect associations, and discrimination.

SOC-04 addresses the group-process layer:

**How do social identity, norms, conformity, social influence, intergroup contact, cooperation, and competition interact, and what can be inferred from group-level behavior without treating public compliance, group identification, contact exposure, or cooperation as transparent evidence of private belief or prosocial character?**

High-risk errors to prevent:
- "social identity = conformity";
- "group identification means agreeing with every group norm";
- "descriptive norm = injunctive norm";
- "norm perception = actual norm";
- "conformity = private belief change";
- "public compliance = internalization";
- "normative influence = irrational";
- "informational influence = objective truth";
- "majority agreement = correctness";
- "minority influence = immediate public conformity";
- "more group identification = always better outcomes";
- "shared identity = cooperation";
- "cooperation = altruism";
- "competition = hostility";
- "intergroup competition always harms cooperation";
- "positive contact always reduces prejudice";
- "contact exposure = causal attitude change";
- "negative contact is just the inverse of positive contact";
- "digital contact works like face-to-face contact by default";
- "contact intervention effects generalize across conflicts";
- "one norm manipulation changes durable private beliefs".

## Core rule

**Group influence is conditional on who the relevant group is, which norm is perceived, whether that norm is descriptive or injunctive, how strongly and situationally the identity is salient, whether behavior is public or private, and what incentives/interdependence/contact conditions are present.**

The canonical analytic scaffold is:

\`social context -> category/identity salience -> perceived referent and norm -> informational/normative/identity-based influence -> public/private response -> interaction feedback -> cooperation/conflict/contact outcome\`

This is a reasoning scaffold, not a fixed serial mechanism.

## 1. Social identity

Social identity concerns self-definition partly in terms of group membership.

Relevant components can include:
- category membership;
- subjective identification;
- importance/centrality;
- affective attachment;
- perceived similarity/prototypicality;
- identity content;
- situational salience.

These are not interchangeable.

## 2. Social identification is not mere category membership

A person can belong to a category while:
- weakly identifying with it;
- rejecting its typical norms;
- seeing another identity as more salient.

Conversely, a temporary or minimal group can become behaviorally relevant if identity is made salient.

Therefore:

**membership ≠ identification ≠ salience**.

## 3. Identity strength versus identity salience

**Strength** concerns relatively enduring subjective identification.

**Salience** concerns whether an identity is psychologically active in a particular situation.

A strongly held identity can be behaviorally irrelevant when another identity is salient.

A weaker identity can become influential when context makes it diagnostic.

Do not infer stable group influence from one situation.

## 4. Identity content matters

Knowing that someone identifies strongly with a group does not reveal:
- which group norm they perceive;
- which prototype they endorse;
- whether the identity is moral, political, professional, relational, or instrumental;
- what action follows.

The content of the identity and norm is required.

## 5. Identification is not conformity

Identification can increase responsiveness to a group's norms.

But high identification can also motivate:
- dissent;
- norm entrepreneurship;
- criticism of deviant leaders;
- attempts to change the group.

A highly identified member can reject a current practice because they see it as inconsistent with the group's "true" values.

Therefore:

**identification ≠ obedience**.

## 6. Social identity and group norms are reciprocally related

A longitudinal field study (N=661; 1,239 observations) found:
- social identification predicted later anticipated adherence to perceived group norms;
- norm adherence in turn predicted later identification;
- reciprocal effects appeared when the relevant identity was salient during the event.

This supports a dynamic relation rather than a one-way "identity causes conformity" model.

## 7. Social identity as moderator of influence

Group messages tend to matter more when the source is:
- perceived as ingroup;
- prototypical;
- normatively diagnostic;
- relevant to the current identity.

However:
- expertise;
- credibility;
- incentives;
- power;
- informational quality

can create influence even without shared identity.

Identity-based influence is one route, not all social influence.

## 8. Social identity can be a "social cure" or "social curse"

A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of 106 studies (N=290,816) on objectively measured physical-health outcomes found several beneficial pathways from social identity processes, including:
- lower stress biomarkers under some identity-salience conditions;
- protective long-term associations of multiple group membership;
- normative effects on health behavior.

But the same review also documents contexts in which identity threat or harmful group norms can worsen outcomes.

Therefore:

**more social identification ≠ universally healthier**.

## 9. Norm content determines direction

If a salient group norm promotes:
- cooperation;
- safety;
- help seeking;
then identification may increase those behaviors.

If it promotes:
- risky behavior;
- hostility;
- overwork;
- exclusion;
identification may increase those instead.

The adaptive value of identification cannot be separated from norm content.

## 10. High-performance norms can become costly

A 2024 study on achievement norms found that high group identification was not universally beneficial:
very high achievement norms could be associated with worse performance and well-being under some conditions.

This illustrates the **social cure/social curse** boundary.

Identity strength is not a context-free protective factor.

## 11. Social norm: broad definition

A social norm concerns shared expectations or perceived standards about behavior, judgment, or values.

Norms can be:
- descriptive;
- injunctive;
- formal;
- informal;
- local/group-specific;
- societal;
- emerging/changing.

"Norm" should not be used as a synonym for any frequent behavior.

## 12. Descriptive norms

A descriptive norm answers:

**What do relevant others actually do?**

Examples:
- most group members cooperate;
- most peers arrive on time;
- most colleagues use a procedure.

Descriptive norms communicate prevalence, not approval.

## 13. Injunctive norms

An injunctive norm answers:

**What do relevant others approve, expect, or believe should be done?**

It concerns:
- approval/disapproval;
- obligation;
- appropriateness;
- expected sanction/reward.

Behavior can be common without being approved.
Approved behavior can be uncommon.

## 14. Descriptive norms ≠ injunctive norms: 2026 meta-analysis

A 2026 meta-analysis synthesized:
- 99 studies;
- N = 80,722;
- tobacco, vaccination, binge drinking, and recreational-drug contexts.

Results:
- descriptive and injunctive norm perceptions correlated only moderately (\`r=.34\`);
- injunctive norms were more strongly related to attitudes and intentions;
- descriptive norms were more closely related to actual behavior;
- injunctive norms amplified descriptive-norm effects across outcomes.

Canonical rule:

**do not collapse descriptive and injunctive norms into one score without evidence.**

## 15. Norm referent matters

A norm needs a referent:
- friends;
- family;
- coworkers;
- profession;
- local community;
- society.

The 2026 meta-analysis found descriptive/injunctive alignment differed by referent proximity, including stronger alignment for friend-based than family-based referents.

Thus:
**"people think X is normal"**
is incomplete unless "which people?" is specified.

## 16. Perceived norm versus actual norm

People can misperceive:
- prevalence;
- approval;
- trend.

A perceived descriptive norm can influence behavior even when objectively wrong.

Therefore separate:
- actual behavior distribution;
- perceived descriptive norm;
- actual approval distribution;
- perceived injunctive norm.

## 17. Pluralistic ignorance boundary

Pluralistic ignorance occurs when individuals privately reject a norm but incorrectly believe others accept it.

This can sustain public conformity despite weak private support.

It is not inferred merely from:
- high public conformity;
- low individual preference.

Both private attitudes and beliefs about others must be measured.

## 18. Descriptive information can change injunctive beliefs

Six studies (N=2,671) found that information about how common a behavior was could shift:
- perceived injunctive norms;
- moral judgments;
- behavioral intentions.

Thus descriptive and injunctive norms are distinct but causally/interpretedly connected.

People can infer:
**common -> probably approved/acceptable**.

This inference can be wrong.

## 19. Social norms can backfire

The 2025 Annual Review emphasizes norm "pitfalls":

Norm messages can:
- threaten autonomy;
- undermine intrinsic motivation;
- reduce trust/sincerity;
- trigger reactance;
- be reconstructed through biased reference groups;
- normalize undesirable behavior if prevalence is highlighted.

A norm intervention must specify the mechanism and possible countereffects.

## 20. Norm internalization versus norm enforcement

People can follow norms because:
- they personally endorse them;
- they expect social approval;
- they fear sanction;
- they want group belonging;
- they infer the behavior is correct;
- they follow rules intrinsically.

Observed conformity alone cannot identify which reason applies.

## 21. Rule-following is not reducible to external enforcement

A 2025 Nature Human Behaviour program of four experimental series (N=14,034) found:
- 55–70% conformed to arbitrary costly rules while anonymous and alone;
- social expectations and intrinsic respect for rules explained substantial conformity;
- extrinsic incentives and prosocial motives added effects.

This challenges a simple model:
**people obey only because others watch them**.

## 22. Conformity

Conformity is change in judgment or behavior toward a group standard or perceived group position.

It can involve:
- public response;
- private belief;
- perceptual judgment;
- opinion;
- behavior.

The outcome channel must be specified.

## 23. Contemporary conformity evidence

A 2024 systematic review identified 48 conformity studies since 2004 using:
- modified Asch paradigms;
- perceptual tasks;
- opinion/decision tasks;
- digital/group contexts.

Effects depend on:
- majority size;
- unanimity;
- task ambiguity;
- incentives;
- group relationship;
- culture/context.

Conformity remains empirically robust but is not one invariant rate.

## 24. Modern Asch replication

A 2023 large replication/extension found:
- about 33% error/conformity under a standard line-judgment condition;
- about 25% under an incentivized condition;
- about 38% conformity for political-opinion trials.

The data show:
- classic majority influence can replicate;
- incentives can reduce conformity;
- influence generalizes beyond perceptual tasks.

They do not imply 33% is a universal conformity constant.

## 25. Public compliance versus private acceptance

**Compliance:** public response moves toward the group while private response does not.

**Acceptance/internalization:** group-consistent response persists privately.

Most simple conformity paradigms do not cleanly distinguish them.

Therefore:
**public conformity ≠ private belief change**.

## 26. Compliance and acceptance need separate measurement

A design seeking the distinction should compare:
- public response;
- private response;
- persistence after group removal;
- delayed retest where relevant.

A single public answer cannot identify internalization.

## 27. Normative influence

Normative influence traditionally concerns changing behavior/judgment to:
- gain approval;
- avoid rejection;
- maintain relationship/group membership.

It can produce public compliance without private acceptance.

But normative motives can also become internalized.

Do not define normative influence solely as "fake agreement."

## 28. Informational influence

Informational influence concerns treating others as evidence about:
- reality;
- correctness;
- uncertainty.

It is especially relevant when:
- task is ambiguous;
- others are perceived as knowledgeable;
- independent evidence is weak.

Informational influence does not guarantee that the group is correct.

## 29. Normative versus informational influence is not always cleanly separable

The same group response can simultaneously provide:
- social approval information;
- evidence about reality;
- identity-relevant guidance.

Self-categorization theory adds:
**referent informational influence**—ingroup norms can be treated as valid information about how "we" should perceive/act.

Mechanisms can coexist.

## 30. Majority agreement is not truth

A unanimous group can be:
- informed;
- mistaken;
- coordinated;
- strategically deceptive;
- responding to the same biased information.

Consensus is evidence about social agreement, not direct evidence of objective correctness.

SOC-01's consensus/accuracy distinction remains canonical.

## 31. Unanimity matters

Classic and modern conformity research shows dissent can reduce majority influence.

A dissenter can:
- break perceived unanimity;
- reduce fear of social isolation;
- provide alternative information.

The dissenter does not need to be objectively correct to alter conformity pressure.

## 32. Majority size has diminishing returns

Increasing majority size can increase conformity up to a point, but effects need not scale linearly.

A group of 100 agreeing individuals is not automatically 10 times more influential than 10.

Perceived independence of sources matters.

## 33. Source independence

Ten people repeating one copied message provide less independent evidence than ten independently informed people.

Modern online environments complicate majority cues because:
- likes;
- shares;
- reposts;
- algorithmic amplification

can create apparent consensus without independent judgments.

Count ≠ independent evidence.

## 34. Minority influence

Minorities can influence majorities through:
- consistency;
- confidence;
- argument quality;
- identity relevance;
- provoking deeper processing.

Minority influence may appear as:
- delayed/private change;
- indirect change;
rather than immediate public conformity.

A lack of immediate public shift does not establish zero influence.

## 35. Deviance and norm change

Groups do not only enforce norms; members can contest them.

The 2025 review of normative transformation emphasizes:
- vertical tensions between societal and group norms;
- horizontal tensions between groups;
- tensions between personal preferences and group norms.

These tensions can motivate:
- collective action;
- norm entrepreneurship;
- social change.

Conformity and change are part of the same normative system.

## 36. Social norms are dynamic

Norms can change when people infer:
- what others increasingly do;
- what others increasingly approve;
- which group is becoming relevant.

A norm trend can influence behavior even before the new behavior becomes the majority.

Static prevalence and perceived direction of change are distinct signals.

## 37. Social identity and norm change

Strong identifiers can:
- enforce old norms;
- champion new norms;
- redefine group prototypes.

Therefore:
**high identification does not imply conservatism**.

Whether it stabilizes or changes a norm depends on identity content and contested prototypicality.

## 38. Cooperation

Cooperation means behavior contributing to a joint or collective outcome, often at some individual cost or foregone benefit.

It can be motivated by:
- altruism;
- reciprocity;
- reputation;
- norm compliance;
- expected partner cooperation;
- identity;
- strategic self-interest.

Therefore:

**cooperation ≠ altruism**.

## 39. Social dilemmas

In social dilemmas:
- individually attractive choices can conflict with collective welfare.

Common paradigms:
- public-goods games;
- prisoner's dilemma;
- common-resource games.

Laboratory cooperation is task-specific behavior, not a global cooperative personality measure.

## 40. Shared identity and cooperation

Shared group identity can increase cooperation through:
- ingroup favoritism;
- expected reciprocity;
- shared norms;
- trust;
- reduced social distance.

But shared identity is not necessary for cooperation.

Institutions, incentives, reputation, repeated interaction, and communication can also sustain it.

## 41. Crossed categories complicate cooperation

A 2024 minimal-group study found multiple shared ingroup memberships additively increased cooperation.

However:
- strength of social identity was not itself associated with cooperation;
- expected cooperation partly mediated the effect.

This shows:
**group membership effects can operate through expectations without requiring stronger subjective identification**.

## 42. Social identification and charitable giving

A 2025 meta-analysis included:
- 109 effect sizes;
- 89,570 participants.

Social identification correlated with charitable giving around \`r=.29\`.

Effects were:
- stronger for self-reported giving than actual behavior;
- dependent on identification target;
- heterogeneous.

Shared identity alone had smaller effects than strength of identification in that literature.

Thus identification can facilitate prosocial behavior without determining it.

## 43. Intergroup competition and intragroup cooperation

A preregistered 2026 meta-analysis of experimental social-dilemma studies included:
- 25 articles;
- 70 effect sizes;
- N = 6,051.

Intergroup competition increased intragroup cooperation with:
- \`g=.677\` overall.

Effects were stronger when intergroup competition involved interdependence.

This supports:
**external competition can increase within-group cooperation**.

## 44. Competition does not imply generalized prosociality

The same process that increases:
- ingroup cooperation

can coexist with:
- outgroup competition;
- hostility;
- resource conflict.

Thus:
**more cooperation inside group ≠ more cooperation across groups**.

Outcome level must be specified.

## 45. Competition is not hostility by definition

Competition means actors/groups have partially incompatible outcomes or comparative incentives.

It does not require:
- hatred;
- prejudice;
- aggression.

Cooperative relationships can contain competition, and competitors can maintain positive attitudes.

Do not infer emotion from payoff structure alone.

## 46. Interdependence

Interdependence concerns how one actor's outcomes depend on others' actions.

It can be:
- positive/cooperative;
- negative/competitive;
- mixed.

Interdependence often predicts behavior more directly than broad identity labels.

MOT principles remain relevant:
payoff structure and goals matter.

## 47. Intergroup contact hypothesis: broad evidence

Intergroup contact research examines whether interaction between group members changes:
- prejudice;
- outgroup attitudes;
- anxiety;
- trust;
- behavioral intentions.

The long-standing literature generally supports:
**positive contact is associated with lower prejudice**.

But effects depend strongly on:
- contact quality;
- valence;
- self-selection;
- context;
- causal design.

## 48. Positive contact is not merely exposure

Contact can vary in:
- quality;
- intimacy;
- equality/status;
- cooperation;
- shared goals;
- institutional support;
- voluntariness;
- duration.

Merely being near outgroup members is not equivalent to positive intergroup contact.

## 49. Facilitative contact conditions are not absolute prerequisites

Classic contact theory emphasized:
- equal status;
- common goals;
- cooperation;
- institutional support.

Modern evidence treats these as conditions that can facilitate stronger effects rather than strict all-or-none requirements.

Contact can sometimes help outside ideal conditions.

## 50. Negative contact matters disproportionately

A 2024 Psychological Bulletin meta-analysis synthesized:
- 238 independent samples;
- 936 effects;
- N = 152,985.

Results:
- positive contact associated with lower prejudice;
- negative contact associated with higher prejudice;
- the detrimental effect of negative contact was significantly larger than the benefit of positive contact.

The negativity bias was especially pronounced under several self-selection/motivational/contextual conditions.

Therefore:
**contact valence must be measured, not averaged away**.

## 51. Positive and negative contact are not simple opposites

A person can have:
- frequent positive contact;
- occasional highly negative contact.

Net effects may depend on:
- intensity;
- recency;
- self-selection;
- group status;
- intimacy;
- expectations.

One contact-frequency score can hide opposing experiences.

## 52. Self-selection is part of contact ecology

People who already hold positive attitudes may seek more contact.

People with high prejudice may avoid it.

Thus cross-sectional:
**contact ↔ attitude**
associations can reflect:
- contact effects;
- selection effects;
- both.

Causal inference needs stronger design.

## 53. 2026 longitudinal re-analysis challenges causal survey inference

A 2026 re-analysis used:
- 31 datasets;
- 21 published studies;
- N = 33,348.

Traditional CLPM models found longitudinal positive/negative contact effects.

Models with stronger control for unobserved stable confounding:
- FF-CLPM;
- RI-CLPM

did not find comparable within-person longitudinal evidence, while substantial between-person correlations remained.

Canonical implication:

**naturalistic longitudinal contact correlations are not automatically causal contact effects**.

## 54. Experimental contact evidence remains distinct

Failure of stronger longitudinal survey models to identify causal within-person effects does not mean:
- contact interventions never work;
- all cross-sectional contact evidence is false.

It means observational causal claims need stronger identification.

Experimental/quasi-experimental contact evidence should be evaluated separately.

## 55. Digital intergroup contact

A 2024 meta-analysis included:
- 88 independent samples;
- N=9,385.

Digital intergroup contact showed a small effect consistent with prejudice reduction:
- \`g=.25\`.

Direct digital contact and computer-mediated communication showed larger effects than some indirect forms.

Digital contact can therefore matter.

It is not automatically equivalent to face-to-face contact.

## 56. Contact in intractable conflict

A 2025 meta-analysis of contact interventions in intractable conflicts included:
- 38 publications;
- 57 samples;
- 143 outcomes.

Contact interventions were associated with improved outgroup attitudes overall.

However:
- heterogeneity was high;
- designs mixed correlational and experimental evidence;
- causal certainty was limited.

Therefore:
**contact intervention ≠ universal conflict-resolution mechanism**.

## 57. Intergroup intervention must fit mechanism/context

A 2024 Nature Reviews Psychology review warns against one-intervention-fits-all approaches.

Interventions may target:
- prejudice;
- conflict resolution;
- reconciliation;
- affective polarization.

Mechanisms and context differ.

An intervention effective for mild bias may fail in active conflict.

## 58. Digital/contact transfer and generalization

Improved attitude toward:
- one person;
- one subgroup;
- one interaction partner

may or may not generalize to:
- the whole outgroup;
- another setting;
- behavior.

Measure:
- target level;
- generalization;
- persistence.

Do not infer generalized prejudice reduction from one pleasant interaction.

## 59. Contact can improve meta-attitudes

A 2025 multi-context correlational program found positive contact related to more accurate beliefs about how outgroups viewed the ingroup in several conflict settings.

This suggests one possible pathway:
contact may correct exaggerated beliefs about outgroup hostility.

Because evidence was correlational, causal direction remains uncertain.

## 60. Norms can mediate contact effects

Intergroup contact can change perceptions of:
- what ingroup members approve;
- what outgroup members are like;
- whether cross-group friendship is normative.

Thus contact and norms are not independent domains.

A contact intervention may work partly through norm updating.

## 63. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- membership, identification, and salience are distinct;
- identity effects depend strongly on group norm content;
- descriptive and injunctive norms are related but distinct;
- perceived norm differs from actual norm;
- public compliance differs from private acceptance;
- majority agreement is not objective truth;
- cooperation is not equivalent to altruism;
- intergroup competition can increase intragroup cooperation under social-dilemma conditions;
- positive and negative intergroup contact have asymmetric effects;
- observational longitudinal contact effects are sensitive to causal model assumptions.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- identification increases sensitivity to group norms;
- norm adherence can reciprocally reinforce identification;
- descriptive norm information can shift injunctive/moral beliefs;
- strong identification can improve or worsen health/performance depending on norm content;
- digital contact can modestly reduce prejudice;
- contact interventions can improve attitudes in intractable conflicts;
- contact can improve meta-attitude accuracy;
- shared identity can facilitate cooperation partly through expectations.

### Not established as universal
- one conformity rate;
- one social-identity outcome direction;
- descriptive/injunctive norm equivalence;
- one normative mechanism for rule following;
- contact as a universally causal prejudice-reduction process;
- equal-status/common-goal conditions as strict necessary conditions;
- cooperation as evidence of prosocial character;
- competition as evidence of hostility.

## Canonical operating rule

**Identify the relevant group and identity salience -> specify the perceived norm and referent -> separate descriptive from injunctive and perceived from actual norms -> determine whether influence changes public behavior or private belief -> distinguish normative, informational, and identity-based routes -> model incentives/interdependence for cooperation -> characterize contact by valence, quality, selection, and causal design -> test persistence/generalization -> infer only the measured group-process outcome.**

Never:
