## 2. Current terminology authority

### Established

FIPAT, within IFAA, maintains the international standard terminology for human anatomical sciences.

Current official adult gross-anatomy framework remains **Terminologia Anatomica, second edition (TA2)**, published online in 2019 and approved by IFAA.

The current IFAA/FIPAT status page still lists TA2 as the active Terminologia Anatomica.

### Important 2026 update

FIPAT's February 2026 report states that work is underway on a planned **TA2.5**, including revision of Latin style. The report describes TA2.5 as a plan **to be published when complete**, not as an already current replacement.

Therefore project rule on 2026-11-18:

**Use TA2-compatible terminology as current standard; do not cite TA2.5 as an adopted/current edition unless IFAA later publishes it.**

### Correction to legacy wording

The legacy ANA-01 finding stated that TA2 was "approved/adopted by IFAA General Assembly in 2020".

Current official IFAA pages support:
- online publication in 2019;
- approval by IFAA / representatives of IFAA Member Societies.

The exact "2020 adoption" wording was not confirmed in the current official pages checked in this pass.

Project must therefore avoid that exact date unless separately sourced.

## 3. Anatomical position

Anatomical position is the reference orientation from which location and movement are described.

Operational project standard:

1. body upright;
2. head and gaze forward;
3. upper limbs alongside the body;
4. palms face anteriorly;
5. lower limbs oriented forward.

The reference matters because terms such as medial/lateral, anterior/posterior and pronation/supination must remain stable even when the actual exercise position changes.

Examples in anatomical position:
- radius is lateral to ulna;
- thumb is lateral;
- palm is anterior;
- forearm is supinated.

The project should not over-specify nonessential details such as exact stance width when they do not change the anatomical reference.

## 4. Directional terms

### Core

- **superior / cranial** — toward the head;
- **inferior / caudal** — toward the feet;
- **anterior / ventral** — toward the front;
- **posterior / dorsal** — toward the back;
- **medial** — toward the median plane;
- **lateral** — away from the median plane;
- **proximal** — nearer the trunk/origin/attachment;
- **distal** — farther from the trunk/origin/attachment;
- **superficial** — nearer the body surface;
- **deep** — farther from the body surface;
- **ipsilateral** — same side;
- **contralateral** — opposite side;
- **unilateral** — one side;
- **bilateral** — both sides.

### Limb-specific terms

Upper limb:
- **radial** — radius/thumb side;
- **ulnar** — ulna/little-finger side.

Lower limb:
- **tibial** — tibia/medial side;
- **fibular** — fibula/lateral side.

Hand:
- **palmar**;
- **dorsal**.

Foot:
- **plantar**;
- **dorsal**.

### Project rule

When limb orientation changes during an exercise, radial/ulnar, palmar/dorsal and plantar/dorsal can be more precise than vague "inside/outside".

## 5. Planes

### Median plane

Single vertical plane passing through the midline and dividing the body into right and left halves.

### Sagittal planes

Planes parallel to the median plane dividing the body into right and left portions.

### Frontal / coronal plane

Vertical plane dividing the body into anterior and posterior portions.

### Transverse / axial / horizontal plane

Plane dividing the body into superior and inferior portions.

### Oblique plane

Any plane not aligned exactly with the standard sagittal, frontal or transverse planes.

### Project rule

Planes are **reference categories**.

Real exercise trajectories can be oblique and multiplanar. Saying "movement is mainly sagittal" is a useful description, not a claim that every point of the joint/segment moves in a perfect geometric plane.

---

## 6. Conventional axes

For elementary description, movement in a plane is conventionally paired with an approximately perpendicular axis:

1. sagittal-plane movement → mediolateral axis;
2. frontal-plane movement → anteroposterior axis;
3. transverse-plane movement → longitudinal/superior-inferior axis.

Typical mappings:
- flexion/extension → sagittal plane;
- abduction/adduction and lateral flexion → frontal plane;
- axial rotation → transverse plane.

### Boundary with biomechanics

This is a teaching/reference coordinate model.

It must **not** be interpreted as proof that a joint rotates around one fixed anatomical axis during the whole movement.

Actual joint axes can shift through ROM and may require local coordinate systems or more advanced 3D descriptions.

Those details belong to MEC.

## 7. Core movement vocabulary

### Flexion / extension

General operational meaning:
- flexion = movement conventionally described toward a flexed/bent position;
- extension = movement toward the opposite/extended position.

The simplistic rule "flexion always decreases the angle, extension always increases it" is not sufficiently robust for all joints and body regions.

Project rule:
**use joint-specific anatomical definitions rather than angle arithmetic when ambiguity exists.**

### Hyperextension

Extension beyond the anatomical/neutral reference range.

This term describes position/motion. It does **not** by itself establish pathology, danger or injury.

### Abduction / adduction

General:
- abduction = away from the relevant reference axis/median plane;
- adduction = toward it.

For digits, the local reference axis matters; therefore "away from body midline" is not universal.

### Medial / lateral rotation

Rotation around the longitudinal axis of a limb/segment.

Common equivalents:
- internal rotation = medial rotation;
- external rotation = lateral rotation.

Project standard prefers **medial/lateral rotation**, with internal/external accepted as common equivalents.

---

## 8. Forearm pronation / supination

These are **radioulnar forearm movements**, not wrist movements.

### Supination

Radius and ulna become relatively parallel; in anatomical position the palm faces anteriorly.

### Pronation

Radius crosses relative to the ulna; in anatomical position the palm faces posteriorly.

When the elbow is flexed about 90°, "palm up / palm down" is a useful teaching shortcut, but it is posture-dependent and not the formal definition.

### Training implication

For curls, rows, presses and grip work, describe:
- elbow movement;
- forearm orientation;
- wrist position

as separate variables.

Do not write "rotate the wrist" when the intended motion is forearm pronation/supination.

---

## 9. Wrist terminology

Core project terms:

1. flexion;
2. extension;
3. radial deviation;
4. ulnar deviation.

"Abduction/adduction of the wrist" may appear in some sources, but **radial/ulnar deviation** is clearer for this project.

---

## 10. Ankle / foot terminology

### Dorsiflexion

Movement bringing the dorsum of the foot toward the anterior leg.

### Plantarflexion

Movement in the plantar direction, e.g. rising onto the forefoot.

### Inversion / eversion

Operationally:
- inversion = sole turns medially;
- eversion = sole turns laterally.

These are useful descriptive terms but do not imply that the whole foot motion occurs at one simple hinge or in one exact plane.

Detailed subtalar/midfoot kinematics → MEC.

---

## 11. Scapular movement vocabulary

For training and later shoulder analysis, keep scapular motion distinct from glenohumeral/humeral motion.

Use:

1. elevation;
2. depression;
3. protraction;
4. retraction;
5. upward rotation;
6. downward rotation;
7. anterior/posterior tilt;
8. internal/external rotation of the scapula when 3D detail is needed.

Project rule:

**"shoulder retraction" is ambiguous. Prefer "scapular retraction" when that is what is meant.**

Likewise, scapular protraction is not identical to glenohumeral horizontal adduction.

Detailed shoulder/scapular kinematics → ANA-04/MEC.

---

## 12. Other movement terms needed later

### Circumduction

Composite sequential movement combining flexion, abduction, extension and adduction (or analogous components), producing a cone-like path of the distal segment.

Not a separate single-axis rotation.

### Lateral flexion

Side-bending of trunk or neck.

### Horizontal abduction / adduction

Useful shoulder terms when the humerus is elevated and moves approximately in the transverse plane.

### Opposition / reposition

Thumb-specific complex movements relevant to later grip/hand anatomy.

---

## 13. Position is not movement

A body segment can have one orientation while another joint motion is occurring.

Examples:
- forearm is **pronated** while elbow is **flexing**;
- humerus is **externally rotated** while shoulder is **abducting**.

Project descriptions must separate:

1. starting position;
2. segment orientation;
3. movement;
4. final position.

---

## 14. Global reference vs local joint coordinates

Anatomical planes describe the body in a global reference framework.

Biomechanical analyses may use:
- segment-local coordinate systems;
- joint coordinate systems;
- sequential rotations;
- moving instantaneous axes.

Therefore:

**anatomical terminology is the communication layer; biomechanics determines the actual kinematic/mechanical model.**

Do not smuggle biomechanical conclusions into anatomy vocabulary.

---

## 15. Training-description standard

For later exercise analysis, describe movement in this order:

1. joint/segment;
2. starting position;
3. segment orientation;
4. main anatomical movement;
5. approximate plane if useful;
6. forearm/grip orientation separately;
7. scapular movement separately;
8. ROM endpoints if decision-relevant.

Example:

"Elbow flexion with the forearm supinated" is preferable to "curl the hand upward".

---

## 16. Common terminology errors to prevent

1. Calling pronation/supination "wrist rotation".
2. Calling scapular retraction "shoulder retraction" without clarification.
3. Saying internal/external rotation without naming the joint/segment.
4. Treating all abduction as movement away from whole-body midline.
5. Treating sagittal/frontal/transverse labels as literal constraints on all real trajectories.
6. Treating "hyperextension" as synonymous with injury.
7. Equating neutral grip with exact 90° forearm rotation.
8. Confusing anatomical position with a required exercise posture.
9. Confusing joint position with direction of current movement.

## 18. Established findings

### HIGH

1. FIPAT/IFAA is the international terminology authority for human anatomical sciences.
2. TA2 remains the current published Terminologia Anatomica framework on the IFAA/FIPAT pages checked.
3. Anatomical position provides the reference for directional terms.
4. Median/sagittal, frontal/coronal, transverse/axial and oblique planes are standard reference descriptions.
5. Anatomical movement terms must be interpreted by joint/segment context.
6. Pronation/supination are forearm/radioulnar movements, not wrist rotation.
7. Reference planes/axes do not prove a fixed real joint axis.
8. Scapular motion must be kept separate from humeral/glenohumeral motion in precise descriptions.

### MODERATE / terminology-dependent

1. Exact preferred English/Russian synonym choice varies by teaching tradition.
2. Some movement labels, especially around scapular 3D kinematics, depend on the coordinate convention used.
