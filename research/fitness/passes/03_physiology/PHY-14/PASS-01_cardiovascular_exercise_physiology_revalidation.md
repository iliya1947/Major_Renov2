## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a bounded cardiovascular model sufficient for later programming, concurrent-training, testing and safety decisions without:

- heart rate = exercise intensity in every context;
- stroke volume always plateauing at moderate intensity;
- cardiac output inferred from HR alone;
- exercise blood pressure interpreted independently of breathing/task;
- total peripheral resistance treated as one local-vessel property;
- blood flow redistributed by complete vasoconstriction of inactive tissue;
- venous return reduced to the skeletal-muscle pump alone;
- resistance exercise described as “anaerobic and therefore not cardiovascular”;
- resistance training assumed to produce the same chronic central adaptations as endurance training;
- postexercise hypotension = chronic BP adaptation;
- capillary density = capillary number;
- normal exercise physiology used to diagnose cardiovascular disease.

### Question type

Integrative acute/chronic cardiovascular physiology during resistance and aerobic exercise.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 13 exact Section 15 items must be covered at content level; HR/SV/CO/BP/TPR and regional flow must remain distinct; resistance vs aerobic acute/chronic responses must be separated; capillary metrics must be interpreted correctly; clinical abnormalities remain MED-owned.

## 3. Heart rate

Heart rate (HR) is one determinant of cardiac output.

During dynamic aerobic exercise, HR generally rises with increasing metabolic/work demand through autonomic adjustment.

During resistance exercise, HR also rises, but the response depends on:

- active muscle mass;
- repetition number/set duration;
- proximity to failure;
- rest;
- breathing/strain;
- load and exercise mode.

A 2025 squat study at matched external intensity showed progressively higher HR with 10, 20 and 30 repetitions.

Breath holding during squats in healthy women modestly increased HR relative to controlled breathing.

Project rule:

**HR is a cardiovascular response variable, not a universal stand-alone measure of internal exercise intensity across modalities.**

---

## 4. Stroke volume

Stroke volume (SV) is the blood ejected per heartbeat.

During dynamic aerobic exercise, SV commonly rises from rest because of:

- greater venous return/preload;
- enhanced contractility;
- reduced end-systolic volume.

A 2025 exercise-CMR study in healthy adults showed increased end-diastolic volume, decreased end-systolic volume and increased SV during exercise.

The traditional claim that SV always plateaus at moderate intensity is too absolute:

- response depends on posture;
- training status;
- blood volume;
- cardiac size;
- exercise intensity and method.

During resistance exercise, SV can be maintained, increased or reduced depending on venous return, afterload and intrathoracic pressure.

Project rule:

**SV response is context dependent and cannot be inferred from HR alone.**

---

## 5. Cardiac output

Cardiac output:

`Q = HR × SV`.

During dynamic aerobic exercise, Q rises substantially to support oxygen delivery.

During resistance exercise, Q can also rise meaningfully.

The 2025 repeated-squat study found higher Q with longer sets at the same relative external load, largely alongside increasing HR.

Project rule:

**resistance exercise has substantial cardiovascular demand even when its primary training goal is strength/hypertrophy.**

---

## 6. Blood pressure

Arterial pressure reflects the interaction of cardiac output and vascular resistance/compliance.

### Dynamic aerobic exercise

Typical pattern:

- SBP rises with increasing work;
- DBP is often relatively stable or may fall slightly because of peripheral vasodilation;
- MAP rises more modestly than SBP.

### Resistance exercise

BP can rise markedly during a set.

Magnitude depends on:

- load;
- active muscle mass;
- set duration/repetition accumulation;
- proximity to failure;
- mechanical vascular compression;
- breathing/Valsalva behavior;
- measurement timing/method.

Recent healthy-adult studies show that repetition accumulation and breath holding materially alter the hemodynamic response.

Project rule:

**“the BP response to resistance exercise” is protocol- and breathing-dependent, not one fixed value.**

---

## 7. Peripheral resistance

Total peripheral/systemic vascular resistance is a whole-circulation construct.

During dynamic aerobic exercise:

- local vasodilation in active muscle is strong;
- systemic TPR generally falls despite sympathetic vasoconstrictor activity elsewhere;
- cardiac output rises enough that MAP is maintained/increased.

During resistance exercise:

- contracting muscle can mechanically compress local vessels;
- sympathetic activation increases;
- vasodilatory/metabolic signals and postcontraction hyperemia coexist;
- systemic resistance therefore varies across contraction/rest phases and protocol.

In the 2025 squat study, longer sets showed lower measured TPR despite higher HR/Q and DBP.

Project rule:

**TPR is an integrated systemic outcome and cannot be equated with local exercising-muscle vascular resistance.**

---

## 8. Blood-flow redistribution

Exercise redistributes cardiac output according to competing demands.

During large-muscle dynamic exercise:

- active skeletal muscle flow increases strongly;
- coronary flow increases;
- renal/splanchnic flow is relatively reduced through sympathetic vasoconstriction;
- skin flow can increase as thermoregulatory demand rises.

This is not complete “shutoff” of inactive tissues.

Regional responses depend on:

- exercise intensity;
- duration;
- body temperature;
- posture;
- hydration;
- active muscle mass.

Project rule:

**exercise hyperemia is regional and regulated; whole-body redistribution is not a simple on/off switch.**

Detailed local vascular mechanics → BIO/MEC as needed.

---

## 9. Venous return

Venous return determines ventricular filling and is supported during exercise by interacting mechanisms:

- skeletal-muscle pump;
- respiratory pump;
- venous valves;
- sympathetic venoconstriction;
- cardiac suction/pressure gradients;
- blood volume and venous compliance.

The 2026 human knee-extension experiment directly showed that rhythmic quadriceps contraction increased femoral venous flow, while expiratory loading attenuated that muscle-pump effect.

Project rule:

**venous return is multi-mechanistic; the muscle pump is important but not sufficient as a complete explanation.**

---

## 10. Acute response to resistance exercise

Integrated model:

`muscle contraction + central command + metaboreflex/mechanoreflex + breathing/strain`
`→ HR/Q increase + BP perturbation + regional vascular compression/vasodilation`.

Key features:

1. HR rises.
2. Q can rise substantially.
3. BP rises during active sets.
4. SV is variable rather than uniformly increased.
5. TPR/local vascular behavior is phase/protocol dependent.
6. longer/fatiguing sets can increase hemodynamic stress even at moderate external load.
7. breath holding/Valsalva changes MAP and cerebrovascular dynamics.
8. BFR can raise BP more than comparable low-load unrestricted exercise.

Project rule:

**acute RT cardiovascular stress depends on more than %1RM.**

No universal “safe” hemodynamic response is inferred here; safety → MED.

---

## 11. Post-resistance-exercise response

After resistance exercise, BP can fall below pre-exercise values in some protocols/populations (postexercise hypotension).

A 2025 ambulatory-BP meta-analysis found small acute reductions in selected ambulatory DBP outcomes.

A November 2026 crossover study in young normotensive men found dynamic resistance exercise produced larger PEH than matched isometric work.

Boundary:

**postexercise hypotension is an acute response, not a direct surrogate for chronic BP reduction.**

---

## 12. Chronic cardiovascular adaptations to resistance training

Resistance training can produce cardiovascular adaptations, including:

- lower resting/ambulatory BP in some populations;
- improved selected vascular/endothelial measures;
- possible improvements in central hemodynamics in some populations;
- improved functional capacity.

Current meta-analyses support BP lowering after dynamic RT, with larger effects often seen in people starting with higher BP, but heterogeneity/certainty limitations are important.

A 2025 RCT in older adults found improvements in resting SV, Q, end-diastolic volume and systemic vascular resistance after 12 weeks of moderate-intensity RT, but this specific result should not be generalized to all healthy adults.

Project rule:

**RT has genuine cardiovascular adaptations, but it should not be assumed to reproduce the full central/peripheral phenotype of endurance training.**

## 13. Acute response to aerobic exercise

During dynamic aerobic exercise:

`metabolic demand ↑`
`→ HR ↑ + SV usually ↑`
`→ Q ↑`
`+ active-muscle vasodilation`
`→ TPR ↓ while SBP/MAP are maintained or increased`.

Additional features:

- venous return rises;
- active-muscle and coronary flow rise;
- regional redistribution occurs;
- heat stress progressively increases skin blood flow;
- at prolonged steady work, cardiovascular drift can alter HR/SV.

Healthy-adult exercise-CMR data confirm large increases in HR, SV and Q from rest to exercise.

Project rule:

**the acute aerobic response is an integrated flow-delivery adjustment, not simply “higher heart rate.”**

---

## 14. Chronic aerobic adaptations

Endurance/aerobic training can increase aerobic capacity through central and peripheral adaptations.

### Central

Potential adaptations include:

- plasma/blood-volume expansion;
- greater ventricular filling;
- greater maximal SV;
- greater maximal Q;
- lower HR at a given absolute submaximal workload;
- cardiac remodeling with sufficient long-term training.

A 2025 randomized 6-week cycling study found higher-intensity groups increased Qmax and some increased blood/plasma volume; larger VO2max gains were primarily supported by central adaptations in that protocol.

A 2026 2-year endurance-training study in sedentary middle-aged adults showed increases in peak VO2, Q and SV, with sex/heart-size differences in magnitude.

### Peripheral

Adaptations can include:

- vascular remodeling/function;
- capillarization;
- mitochondrial/oxidative capacity;
- greater oxygen extraction in some contexts.

Project rule:

**aerobic fitness adaptation is an oxygen-delivery/utilization systems response; central and peripheral shares depend on protocol and person.**

---

## 15. Resting vs exercise HR after aerobic training

A common training adaptation is a lower HR at the same absolute submaximal workload.

Resting HR can also decrease, but magnitude is population/protocol dependent.

Maximal HR is not expected to increase as the main mechanism of higher Qmax; improved SV/Q and peripheral extraction are more important.

Project rule:

**training-related bradycardia/submaximal HR reduction is context-dependent and should not be used alone to quantify fitness gain.**

---

## 16. Blood-pressure adaptation to exercise training

Chronic exercise can reduce resting/ambulatory BP, especially when baseline BP is elevated.

Evidence across exercise modalities is heterogeneous.

A 2026 ambulatory-BP network meta-analysis in hypertension found clear reductions with aerobic, HIIT and combined training, while dynamic/isometric RT estimates were less certain in that specific network.

Other 2026 RT meta-analyses support reductions in resting BP.

Project rule:

**BP adaptation depends strongly on baseline status, measurement method and modality; clinical treatment conclusions belong to MED.**

---

## 17. Capillary adaptations

Skeletal-muscle capillarization is an important peripheral adaptation.

The 2025 systematic review/meta-regression found, after adjustment:

- capillaries per fiber increased with endurance training, HIT and SIT;
- capillary density increased more clearly after ET/HIT;
- capillary adaptations were largest early in training and in lower-fitness participants.

Important measurement boundary:

### Capillary-to-fiber ratio
Reflects the number of capillaries associated with muscle fibers.

### Capillary density
Capillaries per tissue area; this metric is influenced by muscle-fiber CSA.

Therefore hypertrophy can reduce or blunt capillary-density change even when capillary number is maintained/increased.

Resistance-training capillary evidence is less uniform than aerobic-training evidence and is affected by baseline capillarization and hypertrophy.

Project rule:

**capillary density ≠ capillary number; interpret capillarization alongside fiber size.**

---

## 18. Capillary adaptation and oxygen delivery

Capillary remodeling can improve:

- diffusion surface/geometry;
- red-cell transit distribution;
- local oxygen/nutrient delivery.

But “more capillaries” is not the sole determinant of VO2max.

A 2025 study linked training-related changes in VO2max to both Qmax and capillary density/oxidative markers.

A 2026 8-week HIIT/MICT RCT showed VO2max/Qmax improvement with microvascular remodeling but did not detect new capillary formation in its biopsy subset, illustrating that functional vascular adaptation need not equal simple angiogenesis.

Project rule:

**microvascular function/remodeling and capillary number are related but not interchangeable.**

---

## 19. Normal physiology vs clinical abnormality

PHY-14 covers expected physiology in healthy exercise.

It does not diagnose:

- hypertension;
- exercise-induced hypertension;
- arrhythmia;
- ischemia;
- syncope/presyncope;
- heart failure;
- valvular disease;
- autonomic disorders;
- abnormal BP recovery.

Symptoms or abnormal measurements requiring clinical interpretation belong to MED.

Project rule:

**normal exercise physiology provides context but cannot substitute for individual cardiovascular diagnosis/risk assessment.**

## 21. Established project-level model

### Acute dynamic exercise

`task demand`
`→ autonomic + local vascular + mechanical adjustments`
`→ HR/SV/Q + vascular resistance/redistribution + venous return`
`→ oxygen delivery and pressure regulation`.

### Chronic adaptation

`repeated cardiovascular demand`
`→ central blood-volume/cardiac adaptation + vascular/microvascular adaptation`
`→ changed HR/SV/Q/BP response and aerobic capacity`.

Resistance and aerobic exercise share components but produce different weighting and specificity.

## 22. Training-relevant implications

1. **HR alone is not a complete intensity measure across resistance and aerobic exercise.**
2. **Q = HR × SV; neither component should be inferred from the other alone.**
3. **SV does not obey one universal exercise-intensity plateau.**
4. **Resistance exercise can create substantial acute cardiovascular stress.**
5. **Set duration, fatigue and breathing can matter as much as external load for acute RT hemodynamics.**
6. **Dynamic aerobic exercise raises Q while lowering systemic vascular resistance through active-muscle vasodilation.**
7. **Venous return depends on muscle pump, respiratory pump, venous tone and volume status.**
8. **Acute PEH is not a chronic-adaptation surrogate.**
9. **RT can improve BP/vascular/hemodynamic outcomes but is not assumed equivalent to endurance training.**
10. **Aerobic adaptation combines central and peripheral changes.**
11. **Capillary density must be interpreted relative to fiber size.**
12. **Clinical cardiovascular abnormalities remain MED-owned.**

## 24. Confidence / unresolved issues

### HIGH

- Q = HR × SV;
- dynamic aerobic exercise increases HR/Q and usually SV while systemic resistance falls;
- RT can markedly raise BP/HR/Q acutely;
- breathing/Valsalva modifies RT hemodynamics;
- endurance training can increase blood volume/SV/Qmax;
- capillarization is trainable;
- capillary density and capillary-to-fiber ratio are different;
- clinical abnormality interpretation belongs to MED.

- chronic RT lowers BP in many populations;
- RT can improve selected vascular/hemodynamic outcomes;
- central vs peripheral contribution to VO2max adaptation depends on training intensity/protocol.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- exact TPR response during individual resistance sets;
- exact magnitude of chronic RT central cardiac adaptation in healthy young adults;
- exact capillary adaptation from hypertrophy-oriented RT;
