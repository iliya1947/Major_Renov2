# 2. Canonical scientific target

Ethnomethodology asks:

**By what ordinary, locally available methods do competent members produce, recognize, maintain, repair and make accountable the social order of an activity as it unfolds?**

Its scientific object is not primarily:

- hidden beliefs;
- abstract culture;
- attitudes;
- private cognition;
- broad discourse structures;
- externally imposed sociological categories.

The core object is:

> members’ practical methods for accomplishing recognizable social action/order.

### Canonical project representation

naturally organized practical activity
+
members’ methods
+
local accountability
+
indexical meaning
+
reflexive context-production
+
empirical description of order-in-production
+
methodology-specific competence / unique adequacy.

---

# 3. “Ethnomethodology” does not mean a methodology of ethnic groups

The prefix “ethno-” refers to members’ ordinary/common-sense practical methods.

Ethnomethodology is therefore:

> the study of the methods people use to make ordinary social life intelligible and orderly.

### Critical rule

Do not classify EM as:

- cultural anthropology;
- ethnic-group study;
- ethnography by name similarity.

Its genealogy and inferential target are different.

---

# 4. “Members”

A **member** is not merely:

> someone formally enrolled in an organization.

A member is someone competent in the methods/practices by which a social setting or activity is recognizably accomplished.

Examples:

- athlete;
- coach;
- scrub nurse;
- surgeon;
- gym teammate;
- mountain rescuer;
- classroom student.

### Critical rule

Membership is demonstrated through practical competence.

Formal title alone does not establish competence in the relevant local practice.

---

# 5. Members’ methods

Members use ordinary methods to:

- recognize what is happening;
- make actions intelligible;
- coordinate timing;
- interpret instructions;
- repair misunderstandings;
- distinguish normal from deviant conduct;
- make actions recognizable to others.

These methods are often:

- tacit;
- taken for granted;
- embodied;
- locally learned.

### Scientific target

The researcher asks:

> How is this activity actually done so that participants can see it as this activity?

---

# 6. Social order is an ongoing accomplishment

EM rejects the idea that social order can be adequately explained only by externally imposed:

- norms;
- roles;
- structures;
- theories.

Instead, order is produced repeatedly in concrete practices.

### Example

“Coaching” is not simply:

> a role occupied by the coach.

It is continuously accomplished through:

- demonstrations;
- corrections;
- gaze;
- timing;
- instructions;
- athlete response;
- embodied adjustment.

### Rule

Role labels are starting descriptions, not explanations of how order is produced.

---

# 7. Accountability

In EM, **accountability** has a specific meaning.

Actions are designed/performed so that they are:

- observable;
- recognizable;
- describable;
- reportable;
- intelligible;

to competent members.

### Important boundary

Accountability does not mean only:

> moral blame or administrative responsibility.

It refers to the property of actions being available as recognizable instances of what they are.

### Example

A coach's gesture becomes recognizable as:

> correction;
> instruction;
> “watch this”;
> stopping practice;

through local methods shared with athletes.

---

# 8. Accountability is endogenous

A major EM principle:

> the activity itself provides resources for members to recognize what is happening.

Members do not need a sociologist's external theory to identify every local action.

### Project rule

A strong EM analysis should show participants' own displayed orientation to the phenomenon.

Avoid:

> “the researcher interprets this as authority”

without evidence that participants orient to it as such.

---

# 9. Indexicality

**Indexicality** means that the sense of an expression/action depends on its local occasion/context.

Examples:

- “there”;
- “again”;
- “five”;
- “like this”;
- pointing;
- a coach's nod;
- a pause;
- an instruction.

The same expression can mean different things in different activities.

### Sport example

“Five” may mean:

- five repetitions;
- five seconds;
- five rounds;
- score;
- position;

depending on the local training sequence.

### Critical rule

Meaning cannot be fully detached from the situated practice in which it is produced.

---

# 10. Context is not merely external background

EM treats action and context as reflexively related.

Context helps make an action intelligible.

But each action also:

- updates;
- displays;
- constitutes;

what the context now is.

### Example

A coach stopping a drill can transform:

> ongoing practice

into:

> correction episode.

The action does not merely occur “inside” a fixed context; it helps constitute the context.

---

# 11. Ethnomethodological reflexivity

**Reflexivity in EM is not the same concept as researcher reflexivity in generic qualitative research.**

Ethnomethodological reflexivity means:

> actions both depend on and constitute the social setting/order they make intelligible.

### Example

An athlete's response confirms that the previous coach utterance was understood as an instruction.

The response simultaneously:

- interprets;
- displays;
- reproduces;

the instructional context.

### Project rule

Store separately:

1. ethnomethodological reflexivity;
2. researcher methodological reflexivity.

They are different constructs.

---

# 12. Documentary method of interpretation

Garfinkel adapted Mannheim's concept of the **documentary method of interpretation**.

Members treat particular appearances/actions as evidence (“documents”) of an underlying pattern.

At the same time, the presumed pattern is used to interpret new particulars.

### Circular structure

particular action
→ interpreted as document of pattern
→ pattern helps interpret next action
→ pattern may be revised.

### Example

An athlete sees several coach behaviors as documents of:

> “coach is starting a correction sequence.”

That developing pattern changes how subsequent gestures/utterances are understood.

---

# 13. Documentary method is ordinary reasoning

The documentary method is not only a researcher analysis technique.

It describes how members themselves produce coherent understanding from fragmentary, context-dependent evidence.

### Critical rule

Do not confuse:

Garfinkel's documentary method
with
document analysis as a qualitative data method.

They are unrelated uses of “documentary.”

---

# 14. Reciprocity / taken-for-granted background expectancies

Ordinary interaction relies on assumptions that other members:

- share enough background knowledge;
- interpret actions reasonably similarly;
- will repair ambiguity;
- will treat routine meanings as normal unless something disrupts them.

These expectations are usually invisible precisely because they work.

### EM strategy

Study how order becomes visible when expectations:

- fail;
- are challenged;
- require repair.

---

# 15. Breaching experiments

Garfinkel's classic “breaching experiments” deliberately disturbed ordinary expectations to expose the normally invisible methods sustaining social order.

Examples included:

- changing rules during games;
- behaving like a boarder/stranger in one's family home;
- demanding clarification of ordinary statements.

### Scientific purpose

Not:

> prank participants.

But:

> make taken-for-granted background expectancies and repair work empirically visible.

---

# 16. Breaching is not required for ethnomethodology

Breaching is historically famous but is not the defining method of all EM.

Most EM research studies naturally occurring practical action without deliberately disrupting it.

### Critical rule

No breach
≠
not ethnomethodology.

The defining scientific target remains members’ methods for producing order.

---

# 17. Breaching experiment ≠ conventional experiment

Classic breaching demonstrations generally do not share the architecture of randomized experimental science.

Usually absent:

- randomized treatment allocation;
- control groups;
- causal estimands;
- standardized outcomes.

### Correct classification

ethnomethodological demonstration / intervention
+
analysis of repair/accountability/order.

### Rule

Do not infer causal experimental evidence from the word “experiment.”

---

# 18. Modern breaching ethics

Deliberately violating taken-for-granted expectations can create:

- distress;
- embarrassment;
- conflict;
- deception;
- social risk.

Modern research ethics therefore requires substantially more careful justification than early pedagogical demonstrations.

### Current adaptation

Recent “breaching experiment-workshops” use:

- negotiated;
- collaborative;
- sometimes hypothetical;

breaches to examine sociotechnical expectations with lower ethical risk.

### Rule

Classic Garfinkel breaching exercises should not be copied uncritically into modern human-participant research.

---

# 19. Ethnomethodological indifference

**Ethnomethodological indifference** means resisting premature external evaluation/explanation of members' practices.

The researcher tries not to begin by deciding that members are:

- irrational;
- biased;
- inefficient;
- wrong;
- compliant;
- resistant.

Instead:

> first describe how the local practice is made orderly/intelligible on its own terms.

### Critical rule

Indifference ≠ political or moral apathy.

It is an analytic discipline against importing external normative judgments into the description of members’ methods.

---

# 20. EM does not aim primarily to “correct” members’ reasoning

Traditional sociology may explain a practice through theoretical constructs members do not use themselves.

EM instead first asks:

> What practical methods make this activity intelligible to members?

### Rule

External theory can be discussed later.

But an EM account should not replace the phenomenon with theory before describing the endogenous order.

---

# 21. Unique adequacy requirement — weak form

The **unique adequacy requirement of methods** is a distinctive EM quality principle.

Weak form:

> the analyst must be sufficiently competent in the relevant practice to recognize and follow the local phenomena of order.

Often called:

> vulgar competence.

### Examples

To analyze:

- powerlifting team practice;
- surgical handoff;
- football coaching;
- mountain rescue;

the analyst needs enough practical competence to recognize what members are doing.

---

# 22. Unique adequacy is not mere “familiarity”

Knowing vocabulary superficially is insufficient.

Adequacy may require competence in:

- timing;
- embodied cues;
- tools;
- local procedures;
- sequences;
- standards;
- tacit expectations.

### Sport example

A researcher unable to recognize:

- what counts as a failed lift;
- when a set is complete;
- how teammates orient to spotting;
- what a correction means;

may miss the local order completely.

---

# 23. Strong unique adequacy

The strong interpretation is more demanding and debated.

Broadly, it pushes toward producing an account using methods that remain endogenous to the phenomenon rather than importing external explanatory frameworks.

### Critical caveat

The exact interpretation of strong unique adequacy remains contested in the EM literature.

### Project rule

Extract:

- whether weak/strong UA is claimed;
- what competence was acquired;
- how adequacy was demonstrated.

Do not treat “researcher was an insider” as automatically satisfying UA.

---

# 24. Competence may require participation

For specialized practices, field participation may be necessary to acquire the required member competence.

Current work on mountain rescue shows competence being learned/recognized in practical action.

### Critical rule

Participation is sometimes epistemically necessary.

But it also introduces:

- access;
- safety;
- ethics;
- role-conflict;

issues.

---

# 25. Competence can also obscure

A highly experienced insider may stop noticing practices that novices find strange.

Current unique-adequacy literature shows the paradox:

> gaining competence may reduce access to novice perception.

### Project implication

Researcher competence and analytic freshness must both be examined reflexively.

---

# 26. Ethnomethodological ethnography

Some EM studies use prolonged fieldwork and participant observation.

But ethnomethodological ethnography differs from conventional ethnography in its primary aim.

## Conventional ethnography

Often:
- culture;
- meanings;
- norms;
- shared practices.

## EM ethnography

Specifically:
- members' practical methods;
- local accountability;
- endogenous order;
- competent performance.

### Rule

Fieldwork method alone does not determine the parent methodology.

---

# 27. Naturally occurring data

EM strongly values naturally occurring data because the goal is to study order **as members actually produce it**.

Common sources:

- workplace activity;
- meetings;
- sport practice;
- clinical encounters;
- routine interaction;
- video/audio recordings;
- field observation.

### Critical rule

Naturally occurring ≠ researcher-free.

Recording and researcher presence can still influence activity.

---

# 28. Interviews in EM

Interviews can be used, but reports about practice are not identical to the practice itself.

### Key distinction

account of action
≠
action-in-production.

### Project rule

If a claim concerns:

> how an embodied activity is actually accomplished,

direct observation/video is usually stronger evidence than post-hoc interview report.

Interviews may still illuminate:

- members' descriptions;
- terminology;
- practical reasoning.

---

# 29. Video and multimodality

Modern EM/EMCA frequently uses video because practical action involves:

- speech;
- gaze;
- gesture;
- body position;
- movement;
- objects;
- space;
- timing.

### Sport relevance

Coaching and lifting are inherently multimodal.

A transcript of words alone may miss:

- pointing;
- demonstration;
- gaze;
- stance;
- equipment orientation;
- synchronized movement.

---

# 30. Material objects

Practical action often depends on physical objects.

Examples:

- surgical implements;
- barbell;
- platform;
- cones;
- wearable device;
- treatment table.

EM asks:

> how do members make these materials intelligible and consequential in practical action?

### Critical rule

Object presence alone is not analysis.

The key is how members orient to/use it in accomplishing the activity.

---

# 31. Instructed action

A major EM research line studies how people follow:

- rules;
- manuals;
- instructions;
- diagrams;
- protocols.

The key insight:

> formal instructions do not fully specify their own practical use.

Members must interpret them locally.

### Exercise example

“Perform 3×8 at RPE 8” still requires practical methods to decide:

- what counts as RPE 8;
- whether technique is acceptable;
- whether pain changes the instruction;
- when a rep “counts.”

---

# 32. Rule-following is practical accomplishment

EM therefore avoids treating:

> rule exists

as equivalent to:

> rule mechanically determines behavior.

### Rule

Study:

- how rule is invoked;
- how ambiguity is resolved;
- how exceptions are handled;
- how compliance is displayed;
- how repairs occur.

---

# 33. Workplace studies

Garfinkel's later program strongly influenced studies of work and technology.

Typical targets:

- how experts coordinate;
- how work is actually accomplished;
- how tools/documents participate;
- how local expertise differs from formal descriptions.

### Health relevance

Examples include:

- surgery;
- nursing;
- control rooms;
- clinical teamwork.

### Fitness relevance

Analogous targets include:

- coach–athlete correction;
- loading decisions;
- spotting;
- performance review;
- rehabilitation progression.

---

# 34. Work-as-imagined vs work-as-done

A 2025 nursing study using EM explicitly addresses the gap between:

- formal definitions/theories of care;
- everyday practical care work.

The study combined:

- shadowing;
- interviews;
- electronic patient-record review.

### Methodological lesson

EM can reveal multiple locally produced realities/practical logics that one formal model fails to capture.

---

# 35. Accountability in workplace studies

Work becomes visible through members' methods for showing:

- what task is occurring;
- who is responsible;
- whether action is complete;
- whether intervention is needed;
- whether something has gone wrong.

### Example

In an operating theatre, the passing of an instrument is:

- bodily;
- visual;
- sequential;
- materially constrained;

and recognizable to competent participants without verbal explanation.

---

# 36. Ethnomethodology vs Conversation Analysis

CA emerged from the ethnomethodological tradition and shares central commitments to:

- endogenous order;
- accountability;
- indexicality;
- sequential relevance;
- participant orientation.

But EM is broader.

### CA primary target

The organization of:

- talk-in-interaction;
- sequence;
- turn-taking;
- repair;
- action formation.

### Broader EM target

Members’ methods for practical action including:

- embodied work;
- material practices;
- rule-following;
- instruction;
- workplace competence;
- categorization;
- ordinary reasoning.

### Critical rule

All CA is historically/epistemically close to EM.

But not all ethnomethodology is Conversation Analysis.

---

# 37. EMCA as a combined label

Many current health/sport papers use:

> EMCA

when detailed interactional analysis depends on both:

- ethnomethodological commitments;
- CA sequential analysis.

### Project rule

Preserve the authors' EMCA label.

Also identify which analytic engine dominates:

- CA sequence/turn/repair;
- broader EM practical-action/work analysis;
- multimodal interaction analysis.

---

# 38. Broader EM can study nonverbal practical action without full CA

An EM study can focus on:

- embodied movement;
- tool use;
- visual perception;
- practical competence;

without the detailed turn-by-turn conversation architecture that defines classic CA.

### Sport example

Powerlifting “seeing-the-lift” examines:

- assembling;
- suspending other action;
- gaze;
- embodied team membership.

The phenomenon is membership/practical action, not only talk sequence.

MCA is another ethnomethodologically related branch.

It studies how categories such as:

# 40. Ethnomethodology vs conventional ethnography

## Ethnography

Often asks:
- What is this group's culture?
- What shared meanings/norms/practices organize life?

## EM

Asks:
- How do members practically produce the activity/order so it is recognizable here and now?

### Critical rule

Long fieldwork does not automatically make an EM study conventional ethnography.

Likewise, an ordinary ethnography does not become EM because it studies everyday practice.

---

# 41. Ethnomethodology vs phenomenology

EM is historically influenced by phenomenological sociology but has a different empirical target.

## Phenomenology

- lived-experience meaning;
- lifeworld/essence/interpretation.

## EM

- observable practical methods by which members produce social order.

### Rule

First-person subjective experience is not the primary EM endpoint unless used as part of practical action.

---

# 42. Ethnomethodology vs discourse analysis

Discursive approaches may study:

- repertoires;
- subject positions;
- ideology;
- discourse/power.

EM asks:

> how do participants make a situated action recognizable and orderly in practice?

### Critical rule

EM does not infer broad discourse structures from talk merely because language is analyzed.

Participant orientation and practical accomplishment remain central.

---

# 43. Ethnomethodology vs institutional ethnography

## Institutional ethnography

standpoint/work
→ text/ruling relations
→ local-to-translocal coordination.

## Ethnomethodology

members' practical methods
→ local accountable social order.

### Rule

IE tracks institutional/ruling coordination across sites.

EM explicates endogenous practical accomplishment.

They may overlap empirically but have distinct inference architectures.

---

# 44. Ethnomethodology vs organizational ethnography

Organizational ethnography may describe:

- organizational culture;
- identity;
- norms;
- informal practices.

EM requires a finer question:

> exactly how are those practices made recognizable/accomplished by members?

### Rule

“Everyday work” alone does not establish ethnomethodology.

---

# 45. Ethnomethodology vs symbolic interactionism

Both attend to meaning/action.

But EM is more radically focused on:

- local methodic accomplishment;
- accountability;
- indexicality;
- reflexivity;
- members’ endogenous order.

### Rule

Do not collapse EM into generic interactionism.

---

# 46. Ethnomethodology vs thematic analysis

A study may interview members and derive themes.

That is not inherently EM.

Strong EM requires analysis of:

- members’ methods;
- practical reasoning;
- accountability;
- situated order.

### Critical rule

Themes such as:

- communication;
- teamwork;
- trust;

are too abstract unless the study shows **how** those phenomena are accomplished.

---

# 47. Literal/detail-rich description

EM often privileges detailed description over theory-building.

The aim is to **make visible** the methods by which social phenomena are produced.

### Critical rule

Lack of a grand theory is not a methodological weakness when literal explication of practical order is the design objective.

---

# 48. “How” rather than abstract “why”

EM typically respecifies questions from:

> Why do coaches correct athletes?

toward:

> How is a correction recognizably initiated, produced, understood and completed in actual coaching practice?

### Project rule

EM claims should stay close to procedural/practical accomplishment.

---

# 49. Quality architecture

Strong EM should demonstrate:

1. clear practical phenomenon/activity;
2. data close to naturally occurring action where possible;
3. participant/member orientation;
4. indexical/context-sensitive interpretation;
5. accountability shown empirically;
6. reflexive action–context relationship;
7. detailed methodic description;
8. sufficient researcher competence / unique adequacy;
9. ethnomethodological indifference;
10. minimal reliance on externally imposed explanatory categories;
11. multimodal detail where relevant;
12. traceability from data to description;
13. deviant/repair cases where analytically useful;
14. claims calibrated to local practice.

---

# 50. Saturation boundary

Generic thematic saturation is not a defining EM stopping rule.

Adequacy depends more on whether the researcher has:

- identified the phenomenon;
- documented its methodic production;
- shown reproducible/orderly features;
- demonstrated member orientation;
- developed sufficient collections/examples.

### CA overlap

In EMCA/CA, collection-building around recurrent interactional phenomena may be central.

### Rule

“No new themes” is generally a poor fit for EM adequacy.

---

# 51. Unique adequacy vs external validity

Unique adequacy concerns:

> analyst competence relative to the practice.

It does not mean:

- representative sample;
- statistical generalizability;
- external validity in experimental terms.

### Critical rule

A uniquely adequate account can still be local/context-bound.

---

# 52. Generalization

EM often supports:

- procedural generality;
- recurring methodic organization;
- transferable understanding of how an activity is accomplished.

But it does not estimate:

- population frequency;
- prevalence;
- probability.

### Rule

A method found repeatedly in one corpus is not automatically common in all settings.

Cross-setting collections strengthen generality.

---

# 53. Causal inference boundary

EM can reveal:

- sequential consequences;
- practical dependencies;
- how one action makes another relevant;
- how repair changes interaction.

But it does not by itself identify:

- treatment effect;
- causal risk ratio;
- mediation effect.

### Critical rule

Sequential organization ≠ causal estimand.

---

# 54. Health exemplar — acute-care nursing

Healy (2025) used ethnomethodology to study how nurses make sense of competing definitions of nursing practice.

Data included:

- nonparticipant shadowing across eight shifts;
- interviews with six registered nurses;
- review of Electronic Patient Record content.

Finding:

nurses produced multiple practical realities/languages to manage conflicting expectations in everyday work.

### Methodological value

This is a direct current health example of broader EM beyond classic CA.

The central object was:

> how nurses make sense of and accomplish care in practice,

not turn-taking alone.

---

# 55. Health exemplar — workplace learning

Newton et al. studied nursing students and registered nurses across clinical settings using ethnomethodology.

Methods:

- field observations;
- interviews;
- iterative analysis.

The study examined how workplace learning is practically accomplished through:

- doing;
- communication;
- entrustability.

### Appraisal caution

The published analysis is relatively theme-oriented.

- assemble;

This is a direct gym/strength-sport example of ethnomethodology beyond interview themes.

The unit is:

> practical embodied accomplishment of membership.

# 61. “Unmotivated looking”

An EM analytic practice may begin by repeatedly examining recordings without first imposing a predetermined theoretical phenomenon.

The analyst asks:

> What orderly practices are participants themselves displaying?

### Critical caveat

Unmotivated looking does not mean:

- theory-free mind;
- random observation.

Researcher competence still shapes what can be recognized.

### Rule

Pair it with reflexive documentation of member competence and analytic choices.

# 68. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| ethnomethodology vs ethnic-group methodology | “ethno” = members' ordinary methods |
| member vs formal organization member | practical competence is key |
| members’ methods vs researcher methods | target is how participants accomplish order |
| accountability vs blame | recognizability/reportability of action |
| indexicality vs vague contextuality | meaning is locally occasioned |
| EM reflexivity vs researcher reflexivity | action–context constitution vs methodological self-examination |
| documentary method vs document analysis | completely different constructs |
| local order vs externally theorized norm | endogenous accomplishment is primary |
| breaching vs all ethnomethodology | breaches are optional demonstrations |
| breaching experiment vs randomized experiment | no automatic causal design |
| indifference vs moral apathy | analytic non-imposition, not ethical neutrality |
| unique adequacy vs insider identity | competence must be demonstrated |
| competence vs simple familiarity | practical recognition/participation may be required |
| strong UA vs universal settled rule | interpretation remains debated |
| natural data vs researcher-free data | recording/research presence can matter |
| interview account vs action itself | report is not the same evidence as practice |
| video vs ethnomethodology | recording technology alone does not define methodology |
| object presence vs material analysis | members’ orientation/use matters |
| instructions vs deterministic rules | rules need local interpretation |
| EM vs CA | CA is narrower interactional-sequential branch |
| EMCA vs pure CA | combined label can include broader EM commitments |
| EM vs ethnography | practical accomplishment vs culture/shared meanings |
| EM vs phenomenology | observable members' methods vs lived-experience structure |
| EM vs discourse analysis | local accountable action vs broad discourse structures |
| EM vs institutional ethnography | endogenous local methods vs local→translocal ruling relations |
| EM vs thematic analysis | abstract themes can erase methodic detail |
| detailed description vs theoretical weakness | literal explication is often the intended product |
| sequence dependence vs causal effect | sequential relevance ≠ causal estimand |
| recurrent method vs population prevalence | corpus regularity ≠ statistical frequency |
| unique adequacy vs external validity | different quality/inference concepts |

## Established

1. Ethnomethodology is a distinct qualitative methodology centered on members' methods for producing recognizable social order.
2. “Members” are competent practitioners of local methods, not merely formal group members.
3. Accountability means actions are recognizably observable/reportable/intelligible to members.
4. Indexicality means action/expression meaning is occasioned by local context.
5. Ethnomethodological reflexivity means action both depends on and constitutes the setting/context; it differs from generic researcher reflexivity.

7. Breaching experiments expose taken-for-granted expectancies but are optional and are not randomized experiments.
