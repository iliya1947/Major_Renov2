# 2. Canonical scientific target

Critical Incident Technique asks:

**Which specific incidents, actions or events were especially significant for effective/ineffective, helpful/hindering or otherwise consequential performance/experience relative to a clearly defined activity or research purpose, and what recurring categories can be developed from those incidents?**

The analytic center is the **incident**, not the participant as a whole.

### Canonical project representation

defined aim
+
criticality criteria
+
incident eligibility
+
incident-level elicitation/observation
+
context/action/consequence detail
+
incident extraction
+
inductive category development
+
category adequacy/coverage
+
calibrated interpretation.

### Critical rule

CIT is not simply:

> “ask participants to remember something important.”

The study must define what qualifies as a critical incident relative to its purpose.

---

# 3. Flanagan's original definition of an incident

Flanagan defined an incident as an observable human activity sufficiently complete in itself to permit meaningful inference about the action.

For an incident to be **critical**, the original architecture requires:

1. the purpose/intent of the act to be reasonably clear;
2. consequences to be sufficiently definite to judge its effect relative to that purpose.

### Project rule

A vague attitude such as:

> “the gym makes me anxious”

is not, by itself, a full critical incident.

A stronger incident includes:

- situation;
- concrete event/action;
- what happened;
- why it mattered;
- consequence/effect.

---

# 4. “Critical” does not mean catastrophic

CIT terminology is frequently misunderstood.

A critical incident can be:

- positive;
- negative;
- effective;
- ineffective;
- helpful;
- hindering;
- a turning point;
- a significant event.

It does **not** need to be:

- traumatic;
- rare;
- dangerous;
- adverse.

### Example

> A coach gave one specific cue that immediately changed how an athlete understood a lift.

This can be critical if it is significant relative to the study aim.

---

# 5. General aim is foundational

Flanagan's first step is to determine the **general aim of the activity**.

Why?

Because “effective” or “ineffective” behavior cannot be judged without knowing:

> effective for what?

### Example

Activity:
> supporting long-term exercise adherence.

General aim:
> help participants continue meaningful physical activity safely and autonomously.

Only then can an incident be classified as:

- helpful;
- hindering;
- effective;
- ineffective;

relative to that aim.

### Critical rule

CIT without an explicit aim risks arbitrary incident selection.

---

# 6. Flanagan's five-step architecture

The classic procedure has five major stages:

1. **determine the general aim**;
2. **develop plans/specifications/criteria** for incident collection;
3. **collect the data**;
4. **analyze the data**;
5. **interpret and report** the findings.

### Project rule

These are flexible principles, not a rigid survey script.

Flanagan explicitly described CIT as a flexible set of procedures requiring adaptation to the specific problem.

---

# 7. Plans/specifications stage

Before collection, researchers should specify:

- target activity/situation;
- who can provide valid incident information;
- what counts as critical;
- positive/negative or helpful/hindering criteria;
- observation/recall period;
- required incident detail;
- data-collection mode;
- category-analysis plan.

### Critical rule

Criticality criteria should not silently change halfway through analysis unless adaptation is explicitly justified.

---

# 8. Incident as analytic unit

A participant can contribute:

- zero;
- one;
- several;

critical incidents.

Therefore:

**participant N ≠ incident N.**

### Example

20 participants may produce 73 incidents.

The category system is usually developed from the incident corpus, not from treating each participant as one analytic unit.

### Project extraction

Always store separately:

- participant count;
- total incident count;
- incidents per participant if available.

---

# 9. One incident should be sufficiently bounded

A useful critical incident usually contains:

1. **setting/context**;
2. **antecedent/problem/trigger**;
3. **behavior/action/event**;
4. **result/consequence**;
5. **why it mattered** relative to the study aim.

### Critical rule

A broad life period such as:

> “high school sport was difficult”

is not automatically one incident.

CIT works best when the event is bounded enough to reconstruct what happened.

---

# 10. Criticality may be researcher-defined or participant-defined

## Original/functionalist CIT

Criticality is strongly tied to:

- explicit activity aim;
- observable effectiveness/ineffectiveness;
- defined criteria.

## Later qualitative adaptations

Researchers may ask participants to identify events that were:

- especially helpful;
- especially hindering;
- decisive;
- emotionally salient;
- turning points;
- highly significant.

### Project rule

Extract **who defines criticality**:

- investigator;
- expert/observer;
- participant;
- jointly.

This materially affects the claim.

---

# 11. Observer-report CIT

Classic CIT often relied on qualified observers reporting behavior.

Important conditions include:

- direct observation;
- knowledge of the activity;
- ability to judge relevance to the aim;
- enough detail for objective description.

### Critical rule

Observer expertise helps incident interpretation but can introduce:

- professional norms;
- selective attention;
- shared institutional assumptions.

Reflexivity remains relevant.

---

# 12. Participant self-report CIT

Modern qualitative CIT frequently uses participants as observers of their own experience.

Possible prompts:

- Tell me about a time when…
- Describe a specific event that helped…
- Describe a situation that made it harder…
- What happened immediately before/after?
- Why was this event important?

### Strength

Gives access to:

- meaning;
- subjective significance;
- motives;
- perceived consequences.

### Limitation

The incident is reconstructed through memory and narrative.

---

# 13. Data-collection modes

CIT can use:

- individual interviews;
- written incident reports;
- questionnaires/open-ended surveys;
- focus groups;
- direct observation;
- diaries/logs;
- records;
- digital traces;
- combinations of methods.

### Critical rule

Interview is not constitutive.

What makes the study CIT is the incident-centered methodological architecture.

---

# 14. Retrospective recall

Flanagan explicitly noted that CIT often relies on incidents reported from memory and argued recall is more credible when incidents are relatively recent and observers were motivated/able to make detailed observations.

Current sport-methodology literature reiterates:

> retrospective CIT is vulnerable to reconstructive memory.

### Main risks

- forgetting;
- hindsight bias;
- telescoping;
- outcome-based reinterpretation;
- emotional amplification;
- narrative simplification.

---

# 15. Recall aids

Possible aids include:

- timelines;
- diaries;
- training logs;
- video;
- messages;
- written traces;
- calendars;
- competition schedules.

### Value

Can improve:

- event localization;
- sequence reconstruction;
- contextual detail.

### Critical rule

Recall aids reduce some memory problems.

They do not turn retrospective accounts into objective event recordings.

---

# 16. Prospective / near-real-time CIT

CIT can be adapted prospectively.

Participants or observers can document incidents:

- immediately;
- daily;
- weekly;
- after defined events.

### Potential advantage

Reduces long-delay recall distortion.

### Trade-off

Repeated incident reporting can itself change:

- attention;
- reflection;
- behavior.

### Project rule

Record prospective vs retrospective incident capture.

---

# 17. Incident elicitation should avoid generic opinions

Classic CIT was designed partly to move beyond vague impressions/opinions toward concrete events.

Compare:

Weak:
> “Coaches are supportive.”

CIT:
> “Tell me about a specific occasion when a coach did something that made it easier for you to continue training.”

### Critical rule

General evaluations can be collected as contextual data but should not be silently treated as incident evidence.

---

# 18. Helpful vs hindering architecture

A common modern CIT structure asks separately for:

- helping incidents;
- hindering incidents.

This can expose asymmetry.

### Example

Helpful:
> coach offered an easier exercise variation after pain flare.

Hindering:
> coach publicly questioned the athlete's commitment after load reduction.

### Rule

Absence of a helpful factor is not always equivalent to a hindering incident.

---

# 19. Effective vs ineffective architecture

Classic job/performance CIT often focuses on:

- outstandingly effective behavior;
- outstandingly ineffective behavior.

This differs subtly from:

- participant satisfaction;
- emotional salience.

### Project rule

Record the exact criticality dimension:

- effective/ineffective;
- helpful/hindering;
- positive/negative;
- change-triggering;
- anxiety-inducing;
- other significance criterion.

---

# 20. Enhanced Critical Incident Technique (ECIT)

Butterfield et al. developed **Enhanced CIT (ECIT)** from Flanagan's CIT.

Enhancements include:

- more contextual interviewing;
- helping/hindering incidents;
- “wish list” items in some implementations;
- explicit credibility checks.

### Critical rule

ECIT is a named adaptation.

Do not call every modern CIT study “Enhanced CIT.”

---

# 21. Wish-list items

ECIT may collect things that **did not occur** but participants believe would have helped.

These are often called:

> wish-list items.

### Critical distinction

A wish-list item is not an observed/recalled incident.

It is:

- counterfactual;
- desired support/resource/action.

### Project rule

Store separately:

critical incidents
vs
wish-list items.

Do not merge them into one incident frequency table.

---

# 22. ECIT credibility checks

Butterfield et al. describe nine credibility checks:

1. audio recording;
2. interview fidelity;
3. independent incident extraction;
4. exhaustiveness;
5. participation rates;
6. independent placement of incidents into categories;
7. participant cross-checking;
8. expert opinions;
9. theoretical agreement.

### Critical boundary from PASS-06D12

These checks belong to the **ECIT tradition**.

They are not universal requirements for every qualitative methodology.

Even inside CIT, appraisal should ask whether each check fits the study's epistemic adaptation rather than treating nine boxes as automatic validity.

---

# 23. Classic/post-positive vs interpretive adaptations

CIT's history spans different epistemic styles.

## Classic Flanagan architecture

Stronger emphasis on:

- observable behavior;
- objective factual incidents;
- qualified observers;
- effective/ineffective performance;
- category coverage.

## Later qualitative CIT

May emphasize:

- participant meanings;
- subjective significance;
- context;
- interpretation;
- experience.

## ECIT

Current philosophical analysis describes ECIT as containing notable post-positive epistemological elements with some ontological flexibility.

### Project rule

Do not assume all CIT studies share one philosophical stance.

Extract the actual adaptation.

---

# 24. Incident extraction

Interview transcripts often contain:

- true incidents;
- general opinions;
- background;
- explanations;
- hypothetical statements.

The analyst must decide which passages meet incident criteria.

### Strong extraction rule

For each candidate incident, verify:

- specific event?;
- relevant to aim?;
- sufficient context?;
- action/event identifiable?;
- outcome/significance identifiable?

### Critical rule

Incident extraction is an analytic decision and should be documented.

---

# 25. Category development

After incident extraction, incidents are grouped inductively into categories.

Good categories should:

- capture recurring critical behaviors/events;
- remain meaningfully distinct;
- cover the incident corpus sufficiently;
- have clear definitions;
- preserve useful specificity.

### Critical trade-off

Too broad:
> loses practical meaning.

Too narrow:
> produces an unusably fragmented category system.

---

# 26. Category system is the main aggregate output

Typical output:

incidents
→ subcategories
→ higher-level categories
→ practical/theoretical interpretation.

### Example

Anxiety-inducing physical-activity incidents may group into categories such as:

- fragile self-beliefs;
- threat of negative social evaluation;
- competition;
- unfamiliarity/lack of knowledge.

### Rule

The category is not a participant type.

One participant can contribute incidents to several categories.

---

# 27. Exhaustiveness / coverage

Classic Flanagan suggested tracking whether new incidents continue adding new critical behaviors.

His original occupational examples involved very large incident pools.

### Historical heuristic

Flanagan described a practical coverage rule in some settings:

> if 100 additional incidents add only 2–3 new critical behaviors, coverage may be approaching adequacy.

### Critical rule

This is a historical heuristic from specific job-analysis contexts.

It is **not** a universal modern CIT sample-size formula.

---

# 28. Number of participants vs number of incidents

CIT adequacy depends more directly on:

- incident richness;
- incident diversity;
- category coverage;

than participant count alone.

### Example

A participant may report five distinct critical incidents.

Another may report none meeting the criteria.

### Project rule

Never use generic qualitative N rules without also examining incident N and category development.

---

# 29. Participation rate

ECIT studies often calculate:

> number of participants contributing at least one incident to a category / total participants.

Butterfield-derived practice often cites a 25% participation-rate rule for category viability.

### Critical boundary

This is an **internal category-support heuristic**.

It is not:

- population prevalence;
- probability;
- epidemiologic frequency.

### Rule

A 50% participation rate means:

> half of this sample contributed at least one incident in this category.

It does not mean:

> 50% of the target population experiences this phenomenon.

---

# 30. Incident frequency

Researchers may also count:

- incidents per category;
- positive vs negative incidents;
- incident types.

### Critical problem

Incident count can be influenced by:

- number of prompts;
- participant verbosity;
- memory;
- multiple incidents per participant;
- category granularity.

### Rule

Incident frequency is descriptive of the elicited corpus.

It should not be treated as a population rate unless a separate probability-sampling/measurement design supports that inference.

---

# 31. Counts do not make CIT quantitative population research

CIT can legitimately include:

- counts;
- percentages;
- rankings;
- cross-tabulations.

This does not automatically transform category counts into:

- prevalence estimates;
- risk ratios;
- causal effects.

### Project representation

qualitative CIT
+
optional quantitative summaries.

If qualitative and quantitative strands are intentionally integrated beyond simple counts, then evaluate mixed-method architecture separately.

---

# 32. Independent incident extraction / category placement

ECIT may use independent analysts to:

- extract incidents;
- place incidents into categories.

This can assess:

- clarity of incident criteria;
- category usability.

### Critical rule

Independent agreement can be coherent in CIT/ECIT because the methodology often seeks a stable incident/category scheme.

This does **not** imply intercoder agreement is universally appropriate in all qualitative methodologies.

Consistent with PASS-06D12.

---

# 33. Participant cross-checking

ECIT may return:

- extracted incidents;
- category placement;
- interpretations;

to participants.

Possible functions:

- factual correction;
- clarification;
- missing incident addition;
- category resonance.

### Critical rule

Participant cross-checking is an ECIT quality practice, not proof of one objectively true interpretation.

---

# 34. Expert review

Experts may review categories for:

- usefulness;
- missing areas;
- surprising findings;
- domain plausibility.

### Strength

Practical relevance.

### Risk

Experts may reproduce dominant assumptions that participant incidents challenge.

### Rule

Expert review supplements but does not override empirical incidents.

---

# 35. Theoretical agreement

ECIT can compare the category system with existing scholarship.

### Possible outcomes

- agreement;
- extension;
- contradiction.

### Critical rule

Existing theory should contextualize findings.

It should not force incidents into expected categories simply to achieve “agreement.”

---

# 36. CIT vs generic semi-structured interviews

Generic qualitative interview:
> What helps you maintain exercise?

CIT:
> Describe a specific incident when something clearly helped you continue exercise. What happened, who was involved, what did you do, and what changed afterward?

### Distinction

CIT requires:

- event specificity;
- criticality criteria;
- incident-level extraction/category analysis.

### Rule

Adding the phrase “tell me about a time” does not automatically create full CIT.

---

# 37. CIT vs narrative inquiry

Narrative inquiry may preserve:

- chronology;
- plot;
- identity;
- whole life story.

CIT typically extracts bounded incidents from a broader narrative and aggregates them into incident categories.

### Example

Narrative:
> athlete's entire story of leaving organized sport.

CIT:
> specific events that triggered PA decline or increase.

### Rule

If the scientific object is the whole story, narrative inquiry fits better.

If the object is significant incidents and their recurring forms, CIT fits better.

---

# 38. CIT vs turning-point research

A turning point can be a critical incident.

But not all turning-point research uses CIT.

### CIT requires additional architecture

- explicit incident criteria;
- incident extraction;
- category development;
- aim/significance logic.

### Rule

“Turning point” is a possible incident type, not a methodology label by itself.

---

# 39. CIT vs life-event / event-history research

Event-history methods may study:

- timing;
- sequence;
- rates;
- transitions over time.

CIT focuses on:

- significance;
- behavior/action/context;
- helpful/hindering/effective/ineffective incident categories.

### Critical rule

Retrospective critical incidents do not provide population event rates or hazard estimates.

---

# 40. CIT vs adverse-event / safety-incident reporting

Safety systems may collect incidents such as:

- falls;
- injuries;
- medication errors.

But adverse-event reporting ≠ CIT automatically.

Full CIT requires:

- defined aim;
- criticality criteria;
- systematic incident collection;
- analysis/category architecture.

### Rule

A database of adverse events is a data source, not necessarily a CIT study.

---

# 41. CIT vs critical incident reflection in education

In education/professional practice, “critical incident reflection” may mean:

> reflect deeply on a memorable event.

This can be pedagogically valuable but may not implement research CIT.

### Project rule

Classify research CIT only when a systematic research architecture is present.

---

# 42. CIT vs case study

A single incident can be described as a “case,” but CIT usually aggregates multiple incidents across people/situations to build categories.

Case study instead centers:

- bounded case;
- multiple evidence sources;
- within-case context.

### Rule

Incident ≠ case automatically.

---

# 43. CIT vs grounded theory

CIT develops categories of significant incidents.

It does not necessarily require:

- theoretical sampling;
- core category;
- theory generation;
- theoretical saturation.

### Rule

An incident category system is not automatically grounded theory.

---

# 44. CIT vs thematic analysis

CIT can use thematic/category analysis.

But full CIT constrains the analytic unit:

> the critical incident.

Generic thematic analysis may code any meaningful transcript passage.

### Rule

TA of interviews about incidents ≠ CIT unless incident eligibility/criticality is systematic.

---

# 45. CIT inside process evaluation

The “Let's Move It” trial demonstrates CIT inside intervention process evaluation.

CIT can identify:

- intervention-generated insights;
- helpful/hindering episodes;
- events linked by participants to behavior/thinking change.

### Correct design representation

parent trial
+
process-evaluation purpose
+
CIT methodology.

### Critical rule

CIT does not become a process-evaluation design itself.

Process evaluation remains the purpose/parent layer.

---

# 46. Process/mechanism claim boundary

A participant may identify an incident as:

> the moment the intervention changed how I thought about activity.

This supports:

- perceived trigger;
- participant-attributed mechanism;
- process hypothesis.

It does not prove:

- formal causal mediation;
- intervention component effect;
- population mechanism.

### Rule

CIT helps generate/refine mechanism hypotheses, not identify causal indirect effects by itself.

---

# 47. Physical-activity exemplar — adolescent PA change

Kostamo, Vesala & Hankonen analyzed 115 student writings using CIT.

They identified seven critical-incident categories associated with PA change, including:

- promoting one's own well-being;
- body-image awareness;
- finding/losing an inspiring sport;
- health problems;
- life transitions;
- support/lack of support;
- becoming an adult.

### Methodological lesson

CIT was used to identify **participant-attributed triggers/turning events** in activity trajectories.

### Claim boundary

The categories do not estimate the population probability that each event causes PA change.

---

# 48. Physical-activity exemplar — Let's Move It process evaluation

Kostamo et al. used CIT in qualitative process evaluation of the Let's Move It physical-activity intervention.

The analysis identified 39 critical incidents.

One reported process included:

> gaining insight into benefits of light activity and related changes in thinking.

### Methodological lesson

CIT can identify salient process events not captured by aggregate trial outcome measures.

### Critical rule

A reported critical incident is evidence of participant-perceived process significance, not a randomized mediation estimate.

---

# 49. Physical-activity exemplar — anxiety-inducing incidents

Dasinger & Solmon studied 122 young adults using an online CIT survey.

They identified four major sources of anxiety-inducing physical-activity incidents:

- fragile self-beliefs;
- social interaction / negative social evaluation;
- competition;
- lack of knowledge / unfamiliar surroundings.

### Methodological lesson

CIT can be deployed through an open-ended survey rather than interview.

### Critical boundary

The four categories characterize the elicited incident corpus.

They are not prevalence estimates for all exercisers.

---

# 50. Sport exemplar — situational emotional labor

A 2025 Frontiers Sport Psychology methods paper proposes CIT for studying emotional labor among:

- coaches;
- PE teachers;
- other sport interveners.

It explicitly recommends incident reconstruction via:

- questionnaires;
- interviews;

and discusses both qualitative and quantitative incident analysis.

### Current methodological contribution

The paper emphasizes:

- emotionally salient circumscribed situations;
- reconstruction of emotional inducer/experience/regulation;
- longitudinal observation possibilities;
- multiple actors around one incident.

### Important limitation

The paper also highlights retrospective memory reconstruction as a major CIT limitation.

---

# 51. Multi-perspective incident reconstruction

A single incident can be reported by:

- athlete;
- coach;
- teammate;
- observer.

### Strength

Allows comparison of:

- event interpretation;
- action;
- consequence;
- meaning.

### Critical rule

Multiple reports do not necessarily converge on one “true” incident account.

Differences can themselves be analytically meaningful.

---

# 52. Observation-based CIT

When researchers directly observe critical incidents, they can reduce some recall problems.

Potential sources:

- live field observation;
- video;
- competition footage;
- training session coding.

### Limitation

Observation may reveal behavior but not subjective meaning.

### Strong hybrid

observe incident
+
subsequent incident-focused interview.

---

# 53. Incident completeness

A high-quality incident report should be sufficiently complete for interpretation.

Useful fields:

- when/where?;
- who was involved?;
- what happened?;
- what action occurred?;
- what was the immediate result?;
- why was it critical?;
- what happened afterward?;
- how certain is the account?

### Rule

Thin one-sentence reports may not support nuanced incident-level inference.

---

# 54. Incident dependence

Multiple incidents from the same participant are not statistically independent observations.

### Critical rule

Do not treat:

> 100 incidents from 20 participants

as equivalent to:

> N=100 independent people.

If inferential statistics are used, clustering/repeated contributions require appropriate handling.

For purely qualitative categorization, still preserve participant provenance where relevant.

---

# 55. Rare incidents

A rare incident can be practically important.

Examples:

- near-catastrophic injury;
- discriminatory event;
- decisive behavior-change trigger.

### Rule

Low frequency does not automatically mean low importance.

Frequency and criticality are different constructs.

---

# 56. Positive/negative asymmetry

Participants may remember negative events more vividly than routine positive ones.

Prompts can also asymmetrically elicit one class.

### Project extraction

Record:

- positive incident prompt?;
- negative incident prompt?;
- balanced?;
- neutral significant-event prompt?;
- helpful/hindering separately?;

### Rule

Category balance is partly a property of the elicitation design.

---

# 57. Recall-window design

Long recall windows increase access to:

- rare;
- highly salient;
- life-course;

events.

But also increase reconstruction.

Short windows improve event detail but may miss rare incidents.

### Project rule

The recall period is a design choice and should be matched to the research aim.

---

# 58. Emotional salience bias

Critical incidents are intentionally salient.

Therefore CIT may underrepresent:

- gradual change;
- ordinary routines;
- cumulative micro-processes;
- nonconscious influences.

### Example

Exercise dropout may develop through months of friction without one memorable incident.

### Rule

CIT is best for questions where consequential episodes are scientifically meaningful.

It should not replace longitudinal/ethnographic approaches when gradual routine processes are the object.

---

# 59. Verbalizability boundary

Modern sport-methodology work notes that CIT primarily accesses what participants can:

- notice;
- remember;
- verbalize.

It may miss:

- pre-reflective bodily processes;
- automatic affect;
- implicit routines.

### Rule

For embodied/nonconscious mechanisms, combine with:

- observation;
- physiological measures;
- video;
- other methods;

when scientifically needed.

---

# 60. Sampling

Participant sampling can use:

- criterion;
- purposive;
- maximum variation;
- expert/observer sampling.

Incident sampling then occurs within participants' accounts.

### Two-level architecture

1. who can provide relevant incident data?;
2. which reported events satisfy incident criteria?

### Rule

CIT sampling adequacy must be assessed at both levels.

---

# 61. No universal participant N

CIT studies range from:

- small in-depth interview samples;
- large incident-report samples;
- open-ended surveys.

Classic Flanagan studies sometimes used thousands of incidents because the goal was exhaustive occupational requirement mapping.

Modern qualitative CIT can use much smaller samples when the question is narrower.

### Rule

Do not import classic occupational incident counts as modern universal minimums.

# 64. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| “critical” vs catastrophic | critical = significant relative to aim |
| incident vs vague opinion | event/action must be specific enough |
| incident vs whole participant | one person can contribute many incidents |
| participant N vs incident N | different sampling/analytic levels |
| general aim vs broad topic | effectiveness/significance needs a reference aim |
| participant-defined significance vs researcher-defined effectiveness | different criticality architectures |
| effective/ineffective vs positive/negative emotion | not the same criterion |
| helpful/hindering vs wish-list | desired absent factor is not an incident |
| memory account vs objective record | retrospective reconstruction remains interpretive |
| recall aid vs verified truth | improves reconstruction but does not remove memory error |
| incident count vs independent sample size | multiple incidents cluster within participants |
| incident frequency vs prevalence | corpus frequency ≠ population rate |
| participation rate vs prevalence | internal category-support metric |
| rare incident vs unimportant incident | criticality ≠ frequency |
| exhaustive occupational CIT heuristic vs universal sample rule | classic context-specific guidance |
| ECIT nine checks vs universal qualitative quality | adaptation-specific architecture |
| independent coding vs universal rigor | coherent here, not universal elsewhere |
| CIT vs generic interview | incident eligibility/category logic is required |
| CIT vs narrative inquiry | bounded incidents vs whole story/plot |
| CIT vs turning-point study | turning points can be incidents but method architecture differs |
| CIT vs event-history analysis | significance categories ≠ event rates/hazards |
| CIT vs adverse-event database | safety report alone is not research CIT |
| CIT vs reflective practice | systematic research architecture is required |
| CIT vs process evaluation | CIT can sit inside a process-evaluation purpose layer |
| perceived trigger vs causal mechanism | participant attribution ≠ causal identification |
| CIT category vs grounded theory | category system ≠ substantive theory automatically |
| CIT analysis vs generic TA | incident is constrained analytic unit |
| salient events vs gradual process | CIT can miss cumulative/routine mechanisms |
| verbalized experience vs pre-reflective process | CIT accesses what can be noticed/recounted |

# 67. Status after PASS-06D21

## Established

1. CIT is an incident-centered qualitative methodology family with strong Flanagan lineage and multiple later adaptations.
2. A critical incident must be sufficiently specific/complete and significant relative to the study/activity aim.
3. “Critical” does not mean necessarily adverse, rare, traumatic or catastrophic.
4. The classic five stages are aim → specifications → collection → analysis → interpretation/reporting.
5. Participant count and incident count are separate analytic quantities.
6. Criticality may be investigator/expert-defined or participant-defined; that distinction must be recorded.
7. Modern CIT can use interviews, surveys, written reports, observation, logs and multimethod incident reconstruction.
8. Retrospective recall is common and vulnerable to reconstructive bias; recall aids help but do not create objective records.
9. Incident extraction separates specific critical events from general opinions/context.
10. Category development is the main aggregate analytic product.
11. Exhaustiveness/coverage concerns incident/category structure, not a universal participant N.
12. Incident frequency and participation rates are corpus/category-support summaries, not prevalence.
13. ECIT is a specific adaptation with helping/hindering incidents, possible wish-list items and named credibility checks.
14. ECIT checks are methodology-specific and must not be generalized as universal qualitative rigor requirements.
15. CIT must be separated from generic interviews, narrative inquiry, turning-point research, event-history methods, adverse-event databases, reflective practice, GT and generic TA.
16. CIT can be nested within process evaluation but does not itself establish intervention mechanisms or causal mediation.
17. Direct physical-activity/sport evidence confirms applications to PA change, intervention process, anxiety-inducing settings and sport emotional labor.
18. CIT supports incident/category/participant-attributed trigger claims, not prevalence or causal-effect estimates.
