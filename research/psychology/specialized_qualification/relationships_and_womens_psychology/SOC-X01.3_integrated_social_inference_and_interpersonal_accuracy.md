E1. Funder (2012), Accurate Personality Judgment. https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721412445309 ; https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/0963721412445309/ . Full APS author abstract directly read. Realistic Accuracy Model distinguishes relevance/availability of behavior, detection and appropriate use of information, and characteristics of judge, target, trait and information. Conceptual review, not an experiment or a calibrated individual forecast. Original 1995 theoretical paper not independently inspected in full; no additional quantitative claim. Operational implication: adequate inference requires evidence that is informative about the exact construct and available to the particular observer, not merely more observations.

E2. Connelly & Ones (2010), An Other Perspective on Personality: Meta-Analytic Integration of Observers' Accuracy and Predictive Validity. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021212 ; https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ903775 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21038940/ . Full author-provided abstract in ERIC directly inspected; PubMed CAPTCHA. Three meta-analyses totaling 44,178 targets across 263 independent samples distinguished interrater agreement, self–other correspondence and behavior prediction. Greater interaction frequency and closer acquaintance were associated with higher personality-rating validity; gains differed by trait observability and evaluativeness. Observer ratings provided additional prediction of selected educational and occupational outcomes beyond self-ratings. These criterion-specific findings do not establish that familiar observers know private motives better than the target, or that familiarity causally increases accuracy. No pooled coefficients, uncertainty, moderator or publication-bias tables audited.

E3. Kenny & West (2010), Similarity and agreement in self- and other perception: a meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868309353414 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20142435/ . Full PubMed author abstract directly read. 24 studies distinguished self–other agreement from consensus and assumed similarity from assimilation. Group size showed different moderation for self-involving measures; authors report SRM-derived measures appeared less sensitive to the tested moderators. No exact effect size, precision or dependency-method verification. Agreement and similarity are different estimands; neither proves criterion accuracy. Measurement design affects estimates.

E4. Koehler, Brenner, Liberman & Tversky (1996), Confidence and accuracy in trait inference: judgment by similarity. https://doi.org/10.1016/0001-6918(95)00005-4 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8693953/ . Full PubMed author abstract directly read. Participants in the United States and Israel estimated target self-ratings on 50 trait adjectives based on three broad personality dimensions, providing 90% uncertainty intervals. Estimates correlated with the self-rating criterion but were overly extreme; reported confidence covaried negatively with accuracy, particularly when the inferred adjective closely resembled the information provided. This is counterevidence to treating semantic coherence or subjective confidence as sufficient evidence. Self-ratings are the task criterion, not an infallible trait measure. Sample sizes, interval coverage, exact correlations and later replication not audited; no universal inverse confidence–accuracy rule.

E5. Kenny (2021 author documentation), Social Relations Model. https://davidakenny.net/ip/srmip.htm . Directly inspected main text, design notes and source references. Separates general perceiver tendency, consensual target tendency and unique directed relationship component. Distinguishes generalized from dyadic reciprocity. The author explicitly notes that isolated two-person pair designs cannot identify this SRM decomposition. Author methodological documentation, not a new empirical effect or independent replication. Repeated observations of the same dyad do not automatically identify its components. A target effect captures how others tend to judge someone, not automatically true attributes.

E6. Funder (1995), On the accuracy of personality judgment: a realistic approach. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7480467/ . Indexed original author abstract excerpt only (not full abstract directly opened). Foundational theoretical source supporting the accuracy-versus-error research distinction. This is the historical foundation for E1, not additional independent empirical corroboration; no detailed claims beyond that excerpt. 
E7. The Effect of Information on Consensus and Accuracy in Personality Judgment. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022103197913470 . Indexed publisher abstract excerpt; direct 403. Experimental manipulation of available information; agreement across observers was already appreciable at the shortest observation duration and did not rise with longer observation. Only this reported consensus pattern is asserted; self–other accuracy results, authorship/date and exact estimates not independently checked. Illustrates why additional information need not increase consensus, and why consensus and accuracy must be assessed separately. Supporting evidence only.

## 3. Integrated model
Separate (a) what happened, (b) what the actor says or reports experiencing, (c) what the observer infers, (d) what criterion could corroborate the inference, and (e) what future event is predicted. Each requires distinct evidence. Attributions are candidate explanations, not direct observations of causes. Perceived norms, measured group behavior, group approval and individual endorsement differ. A plausible narrative can reflect real cues, observer expectations, actual or assumed similarity, situational constraints, relationship-specific history or shared social assumptions.

Illustrative perception model: rating(i,j,t) = overall level + perceiver tendency(i) + target tendency(j) + directed relationship component(i,j) + occasion and measurement terms. This is a conceptual extension of SRM, not a fitted model. The target tendency is not itself verified accuracy; the relationship component may contain valid relationship-specific information or unique bias. The components cannot be estimated from a single pair of observers and targets without appropriate design. Distinguish target expressivity, cue diagnosticity, observer information and true variation; an attractive explanation is not independent evidence.

Accuracy criteria must match the construct. Self-report can serve as a criterion for a reported experience at the aligned time; it does not establish every underlying cause. Behavior can verify that an event occurred or an agreement was honored, but multiple motives may produce the same action. Observer consensus measures agreement, not necessarily truth. Prediction of observable outcomes is not equivalent to explanation of the reasons for those outcomes. Familiarity can improve trait prediction and also create biased expectations. Do not assume either complete ignorance or privileged access to private mental states.

For repeated judgments, separate directional bias, absolute error, tracking, discrimination and confidence calibration. In a prospective forecasting context, calibration requires stated predictions and independently observed outcomes; it cannot be inferred from an internally consistent explanation. No invented numeric probabilities or claim of calibrated interpersonal forecasting. Perspective-taking effort, concern, ability and accuracy remain distinct.

## 4. Operational procedure
1. Define target, actor, precise proposition, time, context, and intended use. Separate current description, motive hypothesis, causal claim, prediction and decision.
2. Preserve direct evidence, its provenance and limitations; distinguish observed conduct, quoted statements, reports, and interpretations. Identify dependence among cues and repetitions of the same event.
3. Compare at least two genuinely distinct explanations where ambiguity matters: ordinary preference, real constraints, role/norm expectations, temporary state, stable tendency, relationship-specific pattern, shared stimulus, observer bias and measurement error. Do not treat social conformity as endorsement or assume any single behavior diagnoses a trait.
4. Ask what discriminates explanations: which observations would be more likely under one than the other, and which would disconfirm each? Specify a relevant criterion and whether it is available. If all plausible explanations predict the same observation, leave them unresolved.
5. Evaluate observer/target/context factors, cue quality, familiarity, selection, task validity and uncertainty. Separate confidence, consensus, agreement with self-report, behavioral prediction and causal mechanism. Prefer voluntarily supplied clarification and ordinary new information over imaginative elaboration; absence of disclosure does not establish concealment.
6. Revise only the smallest supported claim. No diagnostic labels, invented personal probabilities, manufactured consensus, covert monitoring or tests. Respect explicit choices and autonomy; do not exploit inferred vulnerabilities or social pressure.
7. Record unresolved alternatives and prospective revision conditions. Keep independent validation, cultural transfer and forecasting calibration open; use the appropriate clinical or specialized expertise if needed.

## 5. Internal synthetic transfer review
Rubric set before evaluation: construct separation, credible competing explanation, discriminating evidence, inference boundary. Critical errors: unsupported motive/diagnosis, causal inflation, deterministic group-to-person transfer, disregard of contrary evidence, coercion or privacy breach. Six original generic examples unrelated to private material; self-authored and not held-out.

A. Several observers agree a new group member is unfriendly after watching a single silent meeting. Response: consensus and trait validity differ; role constraints, concentration and shared expectations compete. Compare ordinary observations across opportunities and contexts. No stable trait inference from one episode.
B. A familiar colleague accurately predicts another's preferred working arrangement but misjudges their reason. Response: preference prediction and motive accuracy differ; constraints, convenience and interest are alternatives. The actual choice establishes predictive criterion, voluntary reasons may clarify explanation. No hidden motive inferred.
C. A person feels highly certain about a trait because it resembles another known trait. Response: semantic similarity and empirical diagnosticity differ; overgeneralization and true association compete. Seek independent trait-relevant evidence and record possible error. No increased confidence justified solely by coherence.
D. Two observers describe everyone in a group quite differently, but agree about one target. Response: perceiver tendencies, consensus and relationship-specific contributions differ. Crossed ratings and suitable criterion measures clarify them; single-pair data insufficient. No identification of true target trait from agreement.
E. A norm is widespread in public behavior but rarely privately endorsed in representative reports. Response: actual prevalence, perceived approval and personal endorsement differ; role constraints and sanctions may contribute. Compare report validity and ordinary choices. No inference of private endorsement from conformity.
F. A forecast about a collaborator's action succeeds twice, but several explanations predict both events. Response: observed predictive hits differ from causal discrimination and calibration. Alternative explanations and selection of trials remain relevant. Record future testable predictions across ordinary contexts, including failures. No claim of validated accuracy or cause.
