## 1. Qualification purpose / Question Card

A fitness trainer needs this model to avoid:

- diagnosing every hard-set failure as “lactate”;
- treating PCr depletion as synonymous with ATP depletion;
- treating glycogen depletion as the universal immediate cause of task failure;
- treating Pi, H+, K+ or any other metabolite/ion as a single universal fatigue switch;
- assuming one fixed pH, Pi, glycogen or PCr threshold predicts failure across tasks;
- confusing peripheral metabolic effects with central fatigue;
- confusing O2 delivery with mitochondrial utilization;
- assuming low VO2/performance is always “cardio-limited” or always “mitochondria-limited”;
- assuming dehydration and heat are interchangeable exposures;
- assuming one recovered metabolite means the athlete is functionally recovered;
- inferring a mechanism merely from blood lactate, blood glucose, sweat loss or one MRS value;
- prescribing training based on a universal hierarchy of fatigue mechanisms.

Primary trainer-level decisions supported later:

- identify plausible limiting-factor families from task structure rather than from slogans;
- understand why rest changes repeated-bout performance;
- understand why a short maximal set, repeated sprint, high-rep resistance set and prolonged endurance bout can fail for different reasons;
- interpret substrate state, environmental stress and oxygen transport without overclaiming causality;
- preserve mechanism uncertainty when selecting programming, recovery and measurement strategies.

Question type:

- **mechanism + causal-boundary mapping + task-specific interpretation**.

Target population/context:

- healthy adults and athletes;
- resistance, sprint/HIIT and endurance exercise;
- normoxia/hypoxia and temperate/hot environments where relevant;
- acute exercise fatigue/task failure rather than chronic disease.

Closure criterion for PASS-01:

- all 10 exact Section 11 items covered at content/scope level;
- interaction among energetic, ionic, E–C, oxygen and environmental limitations explicit;
- no content-level blocker preventing independent closure audit.

## 2. Core model — fatigue is not a metabolite

The governing integrated model remains:

`task demand > momentary sustainable capacity`

BIO-11 narrows that architecture to the metabolic/energetic contribution.

Momentary metabolic support depends on interacting state variables including:

- ATP turnover;
- PCr availability;
- Pi/ADP/AMP state;
- carbohydrate availability;
- ion gradients and membrane transport;
- intracellular pH;
- Ca2+ handling / E–C coupling;
- O2 delivery/distribution;
- mitochondrial oxidative capacity/control;
- temperature and hydration state.

Canonical rule:

**metabolic fatigue is a family of interacting limitations, not a single substance accumulating until a universal threshold is reached.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 3. Limiter vs correlate vs consequence

Three categories must remain separate.

### Limiter
A process whose alteration causally constrains performance in the task/context.

### Correlate
A variable that changes with fatigue but is not proven to be the decisive cause.

### Consequence
A variable altered because the task is fatiguing, without evidence that reversing it would restore performance.

Examples:

- blood lactate can correlate with high glycolytic work without being the fatigue toxin;
- low PCr can reduce rapid ATP-buffering capacity but does not by itself identify the whole cause of failure;
- high Pi can impair contractile processes and has stronger mechanistic support than a simple “lactate causes fatigue” model;
- low glycogen can impair some high-intensity and prolonged tasks but can coexist with different downstream failure mechanisms;
- rising core temperature/dehydration can increase strain without allowing one universal performance threshold.

Canonical rule:

**a marker that changes during fatigue is not automatically the limiting factor.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 4. PCr depletion — content PASS

PCr falls rapidly during high ATP-turnover work.

Relevant consequences:

- reduced immediate phosphate-transfer reserve through CK;
- lower ability to buffer ATP/ADP during abrupt high-rate demand;
- altered Pi/Cr state as phosphagen use proceeds;
- less restored PCr before a repeated effort when recovery is short.

But preserve all upstream boundaries:

- PCr depletion ≠ ATP depletion;
- PCr depletion ≠ task failure by definition;
- PCr recovery ≠ complete performance recovery;
- no one PCr level defines fatigue across tasks.

Repeated-sprint evidence already retained in BIO-02/BIO-10 (PMID **8964751**) supports incomplete PCr restoration before repeated maximal work and reduced subsequent high-rate performance.

The 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of 31P-MRS PCr recovery kinetics (PMID **40189235**, 128 studies) reinforces large protocol/biological heterogeneity.

Canonical rule:

**PCr depletion can materially constrain repeated high-power work, especially when ATP turnover is abrupt and recovery is incomplete, but it is one contributor rather than a complete fatigue diagnosis.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 5. PCr depletion and Pi accumulation are linked but non-equivalent

PCr breakdown through the CK system changes the high-energy-phosphate state and contributes to Pi accumulation.

However:

- PCr and Pi are different variables;
- low PCr and high Pi can influence performance through different mechanisms;
- the degree of PCr depletion does not uniquely specify Pi effects;
- oxidative/glycolytic ATP turnover, ATP hydrolysis and recovery state alter the relationship.

Canonical rule:

**PCr depletion and Pi accumulation are coupled energetic events but must not be collapsed into one fatigue variable.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 6. Glycogen depletion — content PASS

Muscle glycogen availability can constrain performance.

Current human evidence strengthens this beyond a generic substrate statement.

PMID **40895431**:

- 10 healthy young men;
- one leg glycogen depleted and the other glycogen loaded;
- high-intensity one-legged knee-extensor task;
- time to task failure was ~40% shorter with low glycogen;
- resting MVC/twitch/RFD were not impaired by low glycogen;
- both conditions reached similar MVC reductions at task failure;
- results therefore do not support a simple model in which low glycogen directly lowers resting force capacity.

Canonical interpretation:

**low glycogen can reduce high-intensity exercise capacity without behaving as a simple on/off contractile fuel threshold.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH** for this task-specific causal effect; generalization requires caution because the study was small and male-only.

---

## 7. Glycogen mechanisms — more than ATP substrate shortage

Possible fatigue-relevant pathways include:

- reduced carbohydrate-derived ATP supply under high demand;
- altered local/subcellular glycogen availability;
- altered SR Ca2+ handling;
- altered membrane/ion-regulatory processes;
- changed substrate selection and metabolic flux.

Human evidence retained upstream:

PMID **24091991** in trained male triathletes found lower glycogen associated with depressed SR Ca2+ release after glycogen-depleting exercise, with restoration alongside carbohydrate-supported glycogen recovery.

PMID **40895431** suggests low glycogen can impair ~5-min high-intensity performance while not simply reducing resting contractile function.

Boundary:

- whole-muscle glycogen ≠ every fibre/subcellular pool;
- correlation between glycogen and Ca2+ handling ≠ proof of one universal causal path;
- glycogen depletion ≠ zero glycogen;
- glycogen depletion ≠ universal short-set failure mechanism.

Canonical rule:

**glycogen availability can influence performance through energetic and E–C/ion-linked pathways, but the dominant pathway is task- and state-dependent.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 8. Inorganic phosphate — content PASS

During intense ATP turnover, Pi rises substantially.

Mechanistically relevant effects include:

- reduced cross-bridge force;
- reduced myofilament Ca2+ sensitivity;
- effects on Ca2+ handling;
- interaction with H+/acid-base state.

Current evidence:

### Human in vivo
PMID **40961462** found that in both women and men, intramuscular Pi was more consistently related to fatigue than H+ during the studied high-intensity contraction model.

### Human isolated fibres
PMID **41348575** showed fatigue-like high Pi + low pH markedly depressed Ca2+ sensitivity in skeletal-muscle fibres from young and older adults.

PMID **40047118** showed high Pi + low pH reduced force and altered cross-bridge kinetics in fibres from older adults.

### Animal in vivo
PMID **40686424** found a more consistent force–Pi than force–H+ relationship in rat triceps surae across the studied stimulation protocols.

Evidence boundary:

- human isolated-fibre conditions are mechanistic, not whole-task quantitative proof;
- animal in-vivo thresholds cannot be imported numerically into humans;
- no universal Pi threshold is retained.

Canonical rule:

**Pi is a major fatigue-relevant metabolite with evidence for contractile/Ca2+-related effects, but no single universal human Pi failure threshold is established.**

Confidence: **HIGH** for fatigue relevance; **MODERATE** for exact causal share in a given human task.

---

## 9. Pi vs H+ — current hierarchy is not “H+ explains everything”

Current evidence rejects the old single-variable acid model.

PMID **40088272** concludes that severe intracellular acidosis can reduce force, shortening velocity and power, while also interacting with Pi and ionic effects.

PMID **40961462** found Pi more consistently related to fatigue than H+ in the studied human in-vivo model.

PMID **40686424** likewise showed stronger consistency for Pi than H+ in rats.

Canonical rule:

**H+ can contribute, especially when intracellular acidosis is severe, but current evidence does not support H+ as the universal primary cause of exercise fatigue.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 10. Ionic disturbances — content PASS

Fatiguing muscle contractions perturb:

- K+;
- Na+;
- Cl−;
- Ca2+;

across intracellular, interstitial and extracellular compartments.

These changes can alter:

- sarcolemmal excitability;
- action-potential propagation;
- T-tubular function;
- SR Ca2+ release;
- contractile activation.

Current review evidence:

PMID **38206444** documents large exercise-related K+/Na+ shifts and the central regulatory role of Na+/K+-ATPase.

PMID **34190344** and PMID **37584745** support an interacting ion-gradient model rather than a one-ion switch.

Current human intervention evidence:

PMID **39580613** found acute digoxin in healthy adults hastened fatigue and increased exercise-related plasma K+ perturbation despite preserved measured skeletal-muscle Na+/K+-ATPase content.

Canonical rule:

**ionic fatigue is an interacting excitability/homeostasis problem; K+ is important but is not a universal isolated fatigue switch.**

Confidence: **HIGH** for ionic relevance; **MODERATE** for exact ion-by-ion causal partition in a specific task.

---

## 11. K+ interpretation boundary

Exercise-related extracellular/interstitial K+ can:

- reduce membrane excitability at sufficiently large disturbance;
- interact with Na+, Cl− and channel regulation;
- show context-dependent potentiating as well as depressing effects at smaller changes.

Therefore reject:

- “any rise in K+ causes fatigue”;
- “plasma K+ directly equals muscle interstitial K+”;
- “one K+ concentration defines task failure.”

Canonical rule:

**the fatigue relevance of K+ depends on compartment, magnitude, accompanying ion/metabolic state and membrane/channel regulation.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 12. H+ / pH changes — content PASS

BIO-05 remains authoritative for acid–base mechanism.

BIO-11 retains only fatigue integration.

Established:

- muscle pHi can fall substantially during intense exercise;
- severe intracellular acidosis can reduce force, shortening velocity and power;
- acidification interacts with Pi, ion-channel function and sensory feedback;
- blood pH/lactate cannot substitute numerically for local muscle pHi.

Reject:

- lactate = H+;
- lactate = acid;
- lower pH = all fatigue;
- acidosis is irrelevant;
- one pH threshold = universal failure point.

Canonical rule:

**acid-base disturbance can contribute to fatigue, especially at severe intracellular acidosis, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient to explain all task failure.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 13. H+ and Pi interaction

Human fibre evidence supports combined effects.

PMID **41348575**:

- fatigue-like Pi + low pH depressed Ca2+ sensitivity.

PMID **40047118**:

- high Pi + low pH reduced force and altered cross-bridge behavior.

PMID **42306919** at 37°C in older-adult fibres found fibre-type-specific contractile effects under fatigue-like Pi/acidosis conditions.

Canonical rule:

**Pi and H+ should be modeled as interacting perturbations rather than independent universal percentages of fatigue.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH** mechanistically.

---

## 14. Excitation–contraction effects — content PASS with PHY ownership

BIO-11 must recognize but not duplicate detailed PHY-10 ownership.

Metabolic/ionic changes can influence:

- membrane/T-tubule excitability;
- SR Ca2+ release;
- myoplasmic Ca2+;
- myofilament Ca2+ sensitivity;
- cross-bridge force.

Current 2026 mechanistic study PMID **42932903**:

- intact single fibres from female mice;
- task-dependent fatigue differences were strongly related to intracellular Ca2+ handling;
- contraction type itself was not sufficient to explain the fatigue pattern when task demands were matched differently.

Translation boundary:

**this is animal single-fibre mechanistic evidence; it supports mechanism plausibility, not a quantitative human resistance-training mechanism share.**

Canonical rule:

**metabolic fatigue can be expressed through E–C/Ca2+ impairment; the detailed physiology remains PHY-10/NEU ownership.**

Confidence: **HIGH** for E–C relevance; **MODERATE** for exact mechanism share in human tasks.

---

## 15. Metabolic disturbance can reduce force without primary central failure

Peripheral metabolic/E–C impairment can reduce force while voluntary neural drive is not the primary limiting event.

This does not imply central fatigue is absent.

Canonical rule:

**central and peripheral limitations can coexist; identifying a metabolic contributor does not prove central contribution is zero.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 16. Oxygen delivery limitations — content PASS

Oxygen supply to active muscle depends on the full cascade:

- pulmonary gas exchange;
- cardiac output;
- arterial O2 content;
- muscle blood flow/perfusion;
- diffusion;
- local distribution.

Current 2026 review PMID **41845944** states that systemic O2 delivery is the primary VO2max limiter in elite endurance athletes, while also emphasizing strong peripheral extraction/capillarization/mitochondrial requirements.

Current 2026 hypoxia review PMID **42028867** confirms that reduced inspired O2 availability progressively reduces VO2max with altitude.

Boundary:

- this does not mean O2 delivery is the primary limiter in every task;
- short maximal work can fail despite substantial non-oxidative ATP contribution;
- local muscle perfusion/diffusion can differ from whole-body delivery;
- VO2max limitation ≠ every endurance-performance limitation.

Canonical rule:

**O2 delivery can be a major or primary limitation in maximal/prolonged aerobic contexts, but its importance is task-, population- and environment-dependent.**

Confidence: **HIGH** for aerobic limitation relevance; **MODERATE-HIGH** for primary VO2max limitation in young/elite endurance contexts.

---

## 17. O2 delivery ≠ mitochondrial utilization

Keep three layers separate:

1. systemic delivery;
2. peripheral distribution/extraction;
3. mitochondrial utilization.

A low whole-body exercise capacity can emerge from limitation at one or several layers.

Canonical rule:

**“oxygen-limited” must specify whether the constraint is delivery, diffusion/distribution, extraction or mitochondrial utilization.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 18. Mitochondrial limitations — content PASS

Mitochondrial oxidative capacity/function can constrain the rate at which oxidative ATP demand is supported.

Relevant variables include:

- mitochondrial content/volume density;
- respiratory capacity;
- intrinsic mitochondrial function;
- substrate/ADP/Pi control;
- coupling;
- cristae/inner-membrane architecture;
- local O2 availability.

But mitochondrial characteristics do not uniquely determine endurance performance.

Current 2026 review PMID **42730845**:

- better endurance performers commonly have greater mitochondrial content/function;
- training/intervention changes often track performance markers;
- relationships are not universal;
- increasing O2 delivery can improve performance, showing that performance is not always mitochondria-limited;
- mitochondrial characteristics are important/necessary in many contexts but not sufficient.

Canonical rule:

**mitochondrial capacity is an important peripheral determinant, not a universal sole endurance limiter.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 19. Mitochondrial limitation ≠ mitochondrial dysfunction

In a healthy athlete, “mitochondrial limitation” can simply mean oxidative machinery is one rate-limiting component relative to task demand.

It does not imply disease or pathological mitochondrial dysfunction.

Canonical rule:

**physiological rate limitation ≠ pathology.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 20. Mitochondrial content ≠ intrinsic function ≠ whole-body performance

Preserve BIO-06 non-equivalences:

- mitochondrial content ≠ intrinsic mitochondrial respiratory function;
- citrate synthase ≠ complete mitochondrial function;
- O2 consumption ≠ ATP production;
- VO2max ≠ isolated mitochondrial capacity.

Canonical rule:

**no single mitochondrial marker can certify the limiting factor of a whole-body task.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 21. Thermal effects — content PASS

Heat can impair exercise capacity through interacting mechanisms including:

- greater cardiovascular strain;
- competition between skin and active-muscle circulatory demands;
- rising core/skin temperature;
- altered perception/thermal discomfort;
- accelerated fluid loss;
- altered substrate use;
- central and peripheral changes.

Current 2026 network meta-analysis PMID **41826976** found heat and combined heat+hypoxia impaired exercise performance and increased physiological strain across included studies.

PMID **41734094** in elite athletes found substantial performance decrement in hot/humid exercise, with interindividual variation linked to several physiological and perceptual variables.

Canonical rule:

**heat-related performance loss is multisystem; it is not reducible to one core-temperature or dehydration number.**

Confidence: **HIGH** for heat as a performance stressor; **MODERATE** for exact causal partition.

---

## 22. Dehydration / hypohydration — content PASS

Hypohydration can alter:

- plasma volume/hemodynamics;
- thermoregulation;
- cardiovascular strain;
- perception;
- neuromuscular performance in some contexts.

Current RCT PMID **41572530**:

- 11 male soccer players;
- intermittent exercise in heat;
- hypohydration reduced several early RTD measures and worsened some neuromuscular outcomes.

However, current meta-analytic evidence requires strong caution.

PMID **43010950**:

- systematic review/meta-analysis of hydration during ≥60-min endurance exercise;
- no clear fixed-duration/time-trial benefit on average;
- time-to-exhaustion estimate favored hydration but prediction interval crossed no benefit;
- physiological averages favored hydration;
- certainty for critical/important estimates was **very low**;
- programmed/individualized drinking did not clearly outperform self-regulated drinking for performance in the pooled evidence.

Canonical rule:

**hypohydration can impair performance, especially under heat/high-strain contexts, but no universal body-mass-loss threshold or hydration-performance effect size is retained from this evidence.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH** for context-dependent impairment; **LOW-MODERATE** for a universal quantitative threshold/effect.

---

## 23. Heat ≠ dehydration

Heat and dehydration commonly interact but are distinct exposures.

Possible combinations:

- heat with maintained hydration;
- dehydration in temperate conditions;
- combined heat + dehydration.

Current 2025 meta-analysis PMID **40835807** found heat and dehydration both influenced carbohydrate use during prolonged endurance exercise, with context-specific effects.

Canonical rule:

**thermal strain and hydration state must be separated analytically before they are recombined as interacting stressors.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 24. Cooling/hydration effects do not prove one fatigue mechanism

Current cooling meta-analysis PMID **42987616** found small average improvements in self-paced and intermittent/repeated-sprint performance and larger time-to-exhaustion effects in hot conditions.

This supports heat strain as modifiable.

It does not prove:

- one core-temperature threshold caused all failure;
- dehydration was the sole pathway;
- cooling benefit identifies the dominant intracellular fatigue mechanism.

Canonical rule:

**successful environmental intervention does not automatically identify one molecular fatigue mechanism.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

## 25. Task-specific nature of metabolic limitation — content PASS

The dominant limitation changes with:

- required force/power;
- duration;
- contraction pattern;
- duty cycle;
- muscle mass;
- rest interval;
- exercise mode;
- starting substrate state;
- training status;
- oxygen availability;
- heat/hydration;
- pacing strategy.

Canonical rule:

**always ask: what must this task sustain, for how long, with what recovery and under what environment?**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 26. Short maximal / near-maximal efforts

Likely important contributors can include:

- rapid PCr depletion;
- Pi accumulation;
- ionic disturbance;
- E–C/Ca2+ effects;
- rapid force/power decline.

Oxidative ATP production is still active.

Glycogen may be used substantially, but its total-store depletion is not automatically the immediate cause of failure in a brief set/sprint.

Canonical profile:

**brief high-power failure is typically better modeled as rapid high-energy-phosphate + ionic/E–C disturbance than as “running out of glycogen.”**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH** as a profile, not a universal law.

---

## 27. Repeated sprints / repeated high-power bouts

Important contributors can shift toward:

- incomplete PCr restoration;
- progressive Pi/ionic disturbance;
- rising oxidative contribution;
- local glycogen use;
- recovery-interval dependence;
- thermal strain if prolonged.

Canonical profile:

**repeated-bout limitation is dynamic: the metabolic state before bout 2+ is not the state before bout 1.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 28. High-repetition / high-density resistance work

Potential limiting architecture:

- high ATP turnover;
- PCr use;
- increasing glycolytic ATP contribution;
- Pi/H+/ion disturbance;
- local glycogen use;
- E–C impairment;
- incomplete between-set recovery.

Do not infer:

- metabolite accumulation is the desired hypertrophy mechanism;
- more burn = more effective training;
- one lactate level specifies the useful stimulus.

Canonical profile:

**high-density resistance work can become strongly metabolically constrained, but training value must be evaluated separately from fatigue magnitude.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 29. Prolonged endurance work

Potential limiting architecture includes:

- systemic O2 delivery;
- local O2 distribution/extraction;
- mitochondrial oxidative capacity;
- muscle/liver carbohydrate availability;
- blood-glucose availability/flux;
- thermal/hydration strain;
- neuromuscular and perceptual factors.

Current evidence does not support one universal glycogen-only or O2-only termination model.

Canonical profile:

**prolonged endurance limitation is an integrated oxygen–substrate–environment–neuromuscular problem.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 30. Maximal aerobic exercise / VO2max context

For VO2max-like tasks:

- systemic O2 delivery is often a major/primary limiter in healthy fit/elite endurance populations;
- peripheral extraction and mitochondrial capacity remain essential;
- pulmonary limitation can become more relevant in some athletes/environmental states;
- training status can shift the dominant step.

Canonical profile:

**VO2max limitation is an oxygen-cascade problem, not a single-organ diagnosis.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 31. Hypoxia

Hypoxia changes:

- arterial O2 availability;
- ventilatory/cardiovascular responses;
- VO2max;
- perceived effort;
- substrate/metabolic state.

Current 2026 review PMID **42028867** supports progressive reduction in VO2max with increasing altitude.

Canonical rule:

**hypoxia makes O2 availability more limiting, but the downstream performance effect still depends on task intensity, duration and acclimatization/state.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 32. No universal metabolic-limiter ranking

Reject a universal ordering such as:

`PCr → Pi → H+ → glycogen → O2 → mitochondria`.

The ordering can change by:

- task;
- participant;
- time within task;
- environment;
- recovery state.

Canonical rule:

**metabolic fatigue has no fixed universal hierarchy.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 33. Limiter can change during the same task

During one continuous or repeated-bout task:

- initial high-rate ATP support may rely heavily on PCr;
- Pi/ion effects may accumulate;
- oxidative contribution rises;
- glycogen/blood-glucose relevance can grow with duration;
- heat/dehydration can become progressively important;
- neural/perceptual factors can change simultaneously.

Canonical rule:

**the dominant limiting factor can migrate over time even when the external task is unchanged.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 34. Limitation is state dependent

The same protocol can produce different limiting architecture with changes in:

- preexercise glycogen;
- creatine/PCr status;
- prior exercise;
- training status;
- temperature;
- hydration;
- altitude/O2 availability;
- recovery interval.

Canonical rule:

**a task does not have one immutable fatigue mechanism independent of starting state.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 35. Interaction architecture

Useful trainer-level interaction map:

`ATP demand`
→ PCr use + glycolytic/oxidative ATP production  
→ Pi/ADP/H+/ion changes  
↔ membrane excitability / transport  
↔ SR Ca2+ release + myofilament function  
↔ force/power output  

while:

`O2 delivery/distribution ↔ mitochondrial utilization`

and:

`glycogen/glucose availability ↔ substrate flux + ion/E–C support`

and:

`heat/hydration ↔ cardiovascular + thermal + metabolic + perceptual strain`.

Canonical rule:

**fatigue emerges from coupled systems; arrows are interactions, not a deterministic single causal chain.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

## 36. Measurement boundaries

### PCr / Pi / pH
31P-MRS can measure local high-energy-phosphate/pH behavior but results depend on muscle, protocol, acquisition and modeling.

### Glycogen
Biopsy / histochemistry / 13C-MRS; whole-muscle values can hide fibre/subcellular heterogeneity.

### Ions
Blood/plasma values do not equal muscle interstitial or intracellular concentrations.

### VO2
Pulmonary VO2 is integrated whole-body oxygen consumption, not direct local mitochondrial ATP output.

### Mitochondria
Content, mass-specific respiration and intrinsic function are different constructs.

### Hydration
Body-mass change, urine indices, plasma osmolality and total-body water are related but non-equivalent measures.

### Temperature
Core, skin and local muscle temperature are not interchangeable.

Canonical rule:

**limiting-factor inference is only as valid as the physiological compartment and construct actually measured.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 38. Practical trainer implications — non-prescriptive layer

1. For a short high-power effort, do not default to “glycogen depletion”; inspect high-energy-phosphate, rest and E–C/ion plausibility first.
2. For repeated sets/sprints, recognize that incomplete PCr/ionic recovery changes the starting state of later bouts.
3. For prolonged endurance tasks, evaluate oxygen cascade, carbohydrate availability, heat/hydration and pacing together.
4. Do not use blood lactate as a standalone fatigue diagnosis.
5. Do not use one glycogen, PCr or pH measurement as a readiness certificate.
6. Do not infer “weak mitochondria” from low endurance performance without separating O2 delivery/distribution and task factors.
7. Do not prescribe a universal hydration or cooling threshold from BIO-11.
8. Treat environmental heat as a modifier of the whole fatigue system, not just a sweating problem.
9. Use task structure to generate mechanism hypotheses, then use measurements/performance data to test them.
10. Programming should manage fatigue relative to the training goal rather than maximize metabolite accumulation.

## 41. Evidence-quality interpretation

### Stronger trainer-level evidence

- fatigue is task specific and multi-mechanistic;
- PCr depletion matters for rapid/repeated high-power ATP support;
- Pi is fatigue relevant;
- severe acidosis can contribute but is not the sole cause;
- ionic disturbances affect excitability;
- E–C/Ca2+ handling is fatigue relevant;
- low glycogen can impair some high-intensity exercise capacity;
- O2 delivery is a major aerobic limitation;
- mitochondrial characteristics matter but are not sufficient alone;
- heat can impair performance;
- hydration effects are context dependent.

### Translation cautions

- Pi mechanistic literature includes animal in-vivo and isolated human-fibre models;
- 2026 Ca2+ task-dependence study PMID 42932903 is mouse single-fibre evidence;
- low-glycogen 2025 study PMID 40895431 is small and male-only;
- elite endurance O2 limitation evidence should not be generalized to every population/task;
- hydration meta-analysis PMID 43010950 reports very-low-certainty critical estimates;
- environmental studies frequently combine heat, fluid loss, pacing and perceptual effects.

## 42. Established content-level findings

### HIGH

1. Fatigue is task specific and cannot be reduced to one metabolite.
2. PCr depletion can constrain rapid/repeated high-power ATP buffering.
3. PCr depletion is not identical to ATP depletion or complete task-failure causality.
4. Pi is a major fatigue-relevant metabolite.
5. H+ / severe acidosis can contribute to force/power loss but is not the universal sole cause.
6. Ionic disturbances can impair membrane excitability and E–C processes.
7. Metabolic/ionic changes can impair E–C coupling and Ca2+ handling.
8. Glycogen availability can limit exercise capacity in some tasks.
9. O2 delivery, extraction/distribution and mitochondrial utilization are distinct.
10. O2 delivery is a major limitation in maximal aerobic/endurance contexts.
11. Mitochondrial capacity is important but not sufficient to explain whole-body performance.
12. Heat stress can impair exercise performance.
13. Heat and dehydration are non-equivalent exposures.
14. The dominant metabolic limiter can change across tasks and across time within one task.
15. No universal metabolic-limiter hierarchy is retained.

### MODERATE-HIGH

16. Pi is often more consistently linked to contractile fatigue than H+ in current in-vivo evidence.
17. Low glycogen can impair high-intensity ~5-min exercise capacity independent of a simple resting-force deficit.
18. Glycogen can influence fatigue partly through E–C/Ca2+/ion-related mechanisms.
19. Hypohydration can worsen neuromuscular/endurance performance in high-strain/heat contexts.
20. systemic O2 delivery is often the primary VO2max limiter in young/elite endurance athletes.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT DEPENDENT

21. Exact causal share of PCr vs Pi vs ions vs H+ in one set/sprint.
22. Exact glycogen level that becomes limiting.
23. Exact Pi concentration that becomes limiting in humans.
24. Exact K+/Na+/Cl− contribution to task failure.
25. Exact pH contribution at a given intensity.
26. Whether O2 delivery or mitochondrial capacity dominates for one individual.
27. Exact dehydration/body-mass-loss level that impairs one athlete's performance.
28. Exact core-temperature level at which a given task becomes performance limited.
29. Exact dominant limiter in a mixed or self-paced task.
