# REL-02 — Conflict and communication architecture and REL sufficiency audit: reciprocal sequences, demand–withdraw, validation, repair, attribution, power, boundaries, coercion, measurement, and causal limits

Status: **COMPLETE_FOR_CURRENT_SCOPE — 20/20 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Close the remaining REL qualification dependency after REL-01.

REL-01 established:

- adult attachment is better treated dimensionally than as four natural person types;
- attachment measures target different constructs and are not interchangeable;
- global, relationship-specific, trait, and state attachment must be separated;
- dyadic actor, partner, and shared-couple effects differ;
- early attachment predicts adult relationships probabilistically, not deterministically;
- attachment labels do not explain coercion, power, or safety.

REL-02 adds the missing conflict/communication layer:

1. conflict as a reciprocal sequence rather than a trait;
2. demand–withdraw, demand–demand, and withdraw–withdraw patterns;
3. escalation and de-escalation;
4. validation/invalidation;
5. responsiveness and accurate versus biased partner perception;
6. repair/recovery after conflict or transgression;
7. attribution/blame;
8. power/asymmetry;
9. boundaries and autonomy;
10. ordinary conflict versus psychological aggression/coercive control;
11. self-report, observational, diary, and dyadic measurement;
12. causal limits.

The goal is not to produce communication tips.

The goal is to prevent high-impact interpersonal inference errors.

Canonical rule:

`conflict target -> sequence -> each partner's behavior/function -> state/arousal/context -> actor/partner/shared effects -> attribution/perception -> power/safety -> repair/recovery -> repeated pattern -> narrow conclusion`.

Never:

`one behavior -> one relationship style`

or:

`both partners participated -> equal power/equal responsibility`.

# I. Question Card

## 1. Concrete dependency

Reliable individualized psychological reasoning must distinguish:

- ordinary disagreement from destructive repeated interaction;
- withdrawal from coercive silence, overload, or deliberate de-escalation;
- demand from legitimate problem pursuit versus controlling pressure;
- validation from agreement/submission;
- relationship attribution from objective partner character;
- reciprocal conflict from coercive control;
- mutual acts from symmetric power/responsibility;
- one conflict episode from stable relational pattern;
- communication correlation from causal mechanism.

Without this layer, likely errors include:

- "pursuer/withdrawer" becoming fixed personality labels;
- conflict frequency treated as relationship quality;
- emotional validation treated as agreement;
- repair reduced to apology wording;
- negative attributions treated as accurate facts;
- self-report treated as objective communication observation;
- observational coding treated as culturally universal;
- attachment used to excuse coercion;
- "both did it" used to infer equal coercive power;
- ordinary boundary-setting confused with control;
- coercive control reframed as communication mismatch.

# II. Conflict is not one construct

## 12. Conflict can refer to different targets

Possible meanings:

- disagreement;
- negative affect;
- hostile behavior;
- unresolved issue;
- frequency of arguments;
- intensity;
- duration;
- aggression;
- coercion.

These are not interchangeable.

## 13. Conflict frequency is not relationship quality

A couple may:

- disagree often but repair effectively;
- disagree rarely because topics are avoided;
- show low overt conflict under coercive control;
- have high satisfaction despite some disagreement.

Therefore:

`more conflict -> worse relationship`

is too crude.

## 14. Conflict content and conflict process differ

Content:

- money;
- sex;
- chores;
- family;
- time;
- values.

Process:

- demanding;
- withdrawing;
- validating;
- attacking;
- problem solving;
- repairing.

One content area does not imply one process.

## 15. One conflict is not a relationship trait

Stable inference requires repeated/contextual evidence.

---

# III. Sequence is the basic unit

## 16. Interaction is reciprocal

Partner A's behavior alters the context for Partner B, whose response alters the next context for A.

A minimal sequence:

`trigger -> A interpretation -> A behavior -> B interpretation -> B behavior -> next A state/behavior`.

## 17. Static labels discard order

"Both were hostile" does not reveal:

- who initiated;
- what preceded it;
- whether hostility escalated;
- whether one partner attempted repair;
- whether one partner was responding to coercion/threat.

## 18. Recursive cycles can emerge without stable malicious intent

A repeated loop may be maintained by:

- threat appraisals;
- learned expectations;
- reinforcement;
- exhaustion;
- incompatible regulation strategies.

This does not imply moral equivalence or causal symmetry.

## 19. Within-conflict and between-conflict patterns differ

One conversation can show a cycle.

A stable relationship pattern requires recurrence across episodes/time.

---

# IV. Demand–withdraw

## 20. Demand–withdraw is a dyadic pattern

One partner pressures for:

- discussion;
- change;
- engagement;
- resolution,

while the other:

- disengages;
- avoids;
- becomes silent;
- exits/limits discussion.

## 21. Demand and withdrawal are complementary positions

2024 observational dyadic evidence found demand by one partner associated with withdrawal by the other and vice versa.

Therefore the unit is often the **pairing/sequence**, not isolated "demanding personality" or "withdrawing personality."

## 22. Demand–withdraw predicts poorer outcomes in several contexts

A 2025 observational prospective study of 151 couples discussing sexual conflict found higher observed demand–withdraw associated with:

- lower relationship satisfaction;
- lower sexual satisfaction;
- higher sexual distress at baseline;
- lower relationship satisfaction one year later.

This supports prognostic relevance in that context.

## 23. 2026 longitudinal evidence extends the pattern

A study of 263 couples followed across one year found attachment insecurity associated with later:

- demand–withdraw;
- demand–demand;
- withdraw–withdraw

patterns, which were in turn associated with lower later relationship satisfaction.

## 24. Statistical indirect effects do not prove unique mediation

Longitudinal ordering strengthens inference.

It does not eliminate:

- omitted variables;
- reciprocal influence;
- prior satisfaction effects;
- measurement overlap.

## 25. Demand is not automatically unhealthy

Problem pursuit can be adaptive when it is:

- specific;
- noncoercive;
- responsive;
- open to negotiation.

## 26. Withdrawal is not automatically unhealthy

Withdrawal can function as:

- avoidance;
- deactivation;
- overload;
- self-regulation;
- time-limited de-escalation;
- safety behavior.

Function and re-engagement matter.

## 27. "Time-out" and withdrawal are not equivalent

A negotiated pause with:

- clear reason;
- bounded duration;
- plan to return

differs functionally from indefinite refusal, punishment, or coercive silence.

This is an operational distinction, not a diagnostic label.

## 28. Demand–withdraw is not inherently gendered

Some older heterosexual-couple literature emphasized woman-demand/man-withdraw patterns.

Recent evidence shows direction depends on:

- whose issue is discussed;
- attachment;
- topic;
- context.

Do not infer gender destiny.

---

# V. Demand–demand and withdraw–withdraw

## 29. Demand–demand

Both partners may intensify pressure/argument.

This can create reciprocal escalation.

## 30. Withdraw–withdraw

Both may disengage.

This can represent:

- mutual de-escalation;
- avoidance;
- resignation;
- low investment;
- overload.

The observable pattern alone does not determine function.

## 31. Conflict patterns can shift within one episode

A dyad may move:

`demand–withdraw -> demand–demand -> mutual withdrawal -> repair`.

Static classification loses this dynamic structure.

---

# VI. Escalation

## 32. Escalation is a temporal process

Escalation means some dimension increases over interaction, for example:

- hostility;
- intensity;
- arousal;
- threat;
- coercion;
- reciprocal negativity.

## 33. Escalation can be behavioral and physiological

Naturalistic 2026 couple-conflict evidence shows pre-conflict stress/anger can alter physiological linkage during conflict.

This supports state/context sensitivity.

## 34. Physiological synchrony is not inherently healthy

In-phase linkage can occur during:

- positive connection;
- mutual escalation;
- shared stress.

Therefore:

`physiological synchrony -> intimacy/healthy co-regulation`

is invalid.

## 35. Pre-conflict state matters

Stress, fatigue, prior anger, and external demands can shape conflict before the first sentence is spoken.

STR/AFF control interpretation.

---

# VII. De-escalation and recovery

## 36. De-escalation is not simply stopping speech

Possible de-escalation processes include:

- lowering arousal;
- shifting affect;
- increasing validation;
- changing topic structure;
- pausing with planned re-engagement;
- moving from accusation to problem specification.

## 37. Recovery matters after peak conflict

A couple can show strong negative affect yet recover.

A low-intensity conflict can remain unresolved and accumulate.

## 38. Validation can contribute to immediate affective transition

A 2026 observational study of 93 expectant-parent couples coded conflict behavior in real time.

When one partner displayed validation—acknowledgment, interest, sincere understanding—the other was more likely to transition from negative affect toward neutral/positive affect.

## 39. Validation also predicted later adjustment in that sample

Observed validation during pregnancy was associated with later relationship quality; some partner-specific stress associations also emerged.

This supports a plausible co-regulatory process.

It is not universal mechanism proof.

## 40. Repair is broader than one phrase

Repair can include:

- acknowledgment;
- responsibility;
- clarification;
- validation;
- apology/remorse;
- reconnection;
- changed behavior;
- restitution where relevant;
- constructive co-reflection.

## 41. Repair success depends on the breach

A minor misunderstanding and a serious betrayal require different evidence of repair.

One generic "sorry" variable cannot capture all repair.

## 42. Co-reflection after transgression has prospective evidence

Three-wave longitudinal work found constructive dyadic co-reflection prospectively associated with post-transgression relationship growth, controlling baseline relationship qualities.

This is supportive, not proof that co-reflection causes growth in every case.

## 43. Recovery after conflict should be measured over time

Immediate calm may reflect:

- resolution;
- suppression;
- avoidance;
- exhaustion.

Later recurrence distinguishes these possibilities better.

---

# VIII. Validation

## 44. Validation is not agreement

In current observational coding, validation includes:

- acknowledgment;
- interest;
- communicating sincere understanding.

A partner can validate the other's emotion/experience without endorsing every factual claim.

## 45. Validation is not capitulation

Understanding another person's perspective does not require:

- surrendering one's own needs;
- accepting blame;
- consenting;
- abandoning a boundary.

## 46. Validation is not guaranteed to resolve conflict

Some conflicts involve:

- incompatible goals;
- material constraints;
- safety problems;
- trust breaches.

Validation may improve process without solving the substantive issue.

---

# IX. Invalidation

## 47. Invalidation can be experienced or observed

Possible forms:

- dismissing emotion;
- minimizing;
- ridicule;
- denying meaningful experience;
- treating expression as illegitimate.

## 48. Perceived invalidation is not identical to objectively coded invalidation

Perception can reflect:

- actual behavior;
- state distress;
- prior history;
- expectations;
- attachment;
- communication mismatch.

## 49. 2026 dyadic evidence

A cross-sectional APIM study of 240 mixed-gender couples found perceived emotional invalidation related to psychological distress and some actor/partner relationship-satisfaction pathways.

## 50. Cross-sectional mediation is not causal mediation

The study cannot establish:

`partner invalidates -> distress -> relationship decline`

as a proven temporal causal chain.

---

# X. Responsiveness

## 51. Perceived partner responsiveness

Typically concerns the sense that a partner:

- understands;
- validates;
- cares.

## 52. Responsiveness is broader than conflict behavior

It can occur during:

- support;
- ordinary conversation;
- disclosure;
- celebration;
- conflict.

## 53. Perceived responsiveness and objective behavior differ

The PRI measure-development work found responsiveness/insensitivity scales related to partner-reported behaviors while remaining partly perceptual constructs.

## 54. Responsiveness should not be inferred from one interaction

Repeated evidence is preferable.

---

# XI. Empathic accuracy and communication

## 55. Understanding a partner's internal state is imperfect

A 2025 35-day diary study of 327 couples found partners generally tracked changes in each other's relationship-related emotions reasonably well, especially when feelings were shared verbally.

## 56. Assumed similarity contributes

People also use their own emotion as a cue to infer the partner's.

Therefore perceived understanding can contain:

- accuracy;
- projection/assumed similarity;
- bias.

## 57. Accuracy does not guarantee responsiveness

In the 2025 study, emotion intensity/perception often related more strongly to perceived responsiveness than accuracy itself.

So:

`accurately reading partner -> partner necessarily feels understood`

is not universal.

---

# XII. Relationship attributions

## 58. Attribution concerns explanations for partner behavior

Examples:

- temporary versus stable cause;
- situation versus character;
- intentional versus accidental;
- selfish versus benign motive.

## 59. Attribution can shape emotional response

The same behavior can elicit different responses depending on inferred:

- intent;
- responsibility;
- controllability.

## 60. Negative attributions are associated with lower satisfaction

A preregistered 2026 cross-cultural study of 2,347 partnered adults across:

- Spain;
- Taiwan;
- Turkey;
- United States

found higher causal/responsibility attributions associated with lower relationship satisfaction across countries.

## 61. The association is culturally patterned

Strength differed by country.

Therefore:

`one attribution model applies identically across cultures`

is invalid.

## 62. Attribution is not objective motive detection

A person can be:

- accurately identifying harmful intent;
- misattributing intent;
- uncertain.

SOC person-perception rules remain controlling.

## 63. Relationship distress can itself alter attribution

Low satisfaction may increase negative interpretations.

Cross-sectional attribution–satisfaction associations cannot identify direction.

---

# XIII. Hostility

## 64. Hostility is not simply conflict

Hostility includes behavior such as:

- contemptuous/derogatory tone;
- insult;
- aggression;
- antagonistic attack.

## 65. Naturalistic prospective evidence

A 2024 study sampled approximately one day of natural couple communication in 106 dating couples.

Own and partner hostility predicted:

- more later relationship aggression;
- lower later relationship satisfaction,

controlling baseline outcome.

## 66. Naturalistic observation improves ecological evidence

But one day remains a sample.

Repeated sampling is preferable for person/relationship-level inference.

---

# XIV. Communication quality versus quantity

## 67. More communication is not automatically better

Frequency and quality are distinct.

## 68. Domain-specific evidence illustrates this

A meta-analysis of sexual communication found stronger associations of satisfaction with communication **quality** than with communication frequency/self-disclosure alone.

This does not generalize numerically to all communication domains.

## 69. Repeated discussion can be productive or ruminative/coercive

Need:

- function;
- tone;
- responsiveness;
- outcome;
- repetition pattern.

---

# XV. Power

## 70. Relationship power is not one variable

Power can involve control over:

- resources;
- decisions;
- access;
- social connections;
- sex;
- information;
- relationship continuation.

## 71. Subjective and objective power can differ

A 2026 truth-and-bias study across 1,304 dyads found systematic underestimation of one's own power on average, with motivational correlates.

Therefore:

`felt powerless -> objectively has no influence`

is not guaranteed.

## 72. Power asymmetry can shape conflict behavior

Recent review evidence emphasizes that structural and within-relationship power interact with:

- gender ideology;
- aggression;
- accommodation;
- need expression.

## 73. Power is not inherently abuse

Some asymmetry can arise from:

- expertise;
- income;
- caregiving roles;
- temporary dependence.

The question is how power is used.

## 74. Power can become safety-relevant when used to dominate/control

Examples:

- monitoring;
- isolation;
- threats;
- deprivation;
- forced decision control.

This moves beyond ordinary communication style.

---

# XVI. Boundaries

## 75. "Boundary" is not one standardized relationship-science construct

In practical reasoning, operationalize the exact behavior/limit rather than treating "boundary" as a psychometric label.

## 76. A boundary concerns one's participation/access/conditions

Operational examples:

- what one will discuss now;
- what access one permits to privacy/resources/body/time;
- what one will do if a condition is violated.

## 77. Boundary and control are not synonyms

Compare:

- "I will leave the conversation if I am threatened";
- "You are not allowed to speak to your friends."

The first specifies one's response.

The second may restrict another person's autonomy and can become coercive depending on pattern/context.

## 78. Consequence and threat require contextual analysis

A consequence can be:

- self-protective;
- retaliatory;
- manipulative;
- coercive.

Do not classify from wording alone.

# XVII. Ordinary conflict versus IPV/coercive control

## 79. Intimate partner violence is not synonymous with "bad communication"

CDC's current definition includes:

- physical violence;
- sexual violence;
- stalking;
- psychological aggression.

## 80. Psychological aggression includes control

CDC defines psychological aggression as verbal/nonverbal communication intended to:

- harm a partner mentally/emotionally;
- exert control over a partner.

## 81. Current NISVS operationalization

CDC's 2026 NISVS brief distinguishes expressive aggression from coercive control/entrapment.

Coercive-control items include behaviors intended to:

- monitor;
- control;
- threaten

an intimate partner.

## 82. Conflict and coercive control differ in structure

Ordinary conflict centers on disagreement and negotiation.

Coercive control centers on domination/restriction/fear/entrapment patterns.

They can coexist, but they are not interchangeable constructs.

## 83. Coercive control is pattern-based

A 2026 C-CAS validation study emphasizes that:

- severity;
- frequency;
- pattern

must be considered collectively.

## 84. Counting acts alone can miss relational structure

Two partners may each perform aggressive acts while differing in:

- motive;
- fear;
- impact;
- control;
- injury;
- freedom to disengage.

## 85. "Bidirectional" does not automatically mean symmetric

A 2026 scoping review of 82 publications found substantial conceptual/operational heterogeneity around bidirectional violence and highlighted:

- motivational asymmetry;
- escalation;
- relational context.

Therefore:

`both partners used aggression -> equal coercive power/responsibility`

is invalid.

## 86. Coercive-control assessment itself has measurement limitations

A 2026 systematic review identified:

- 157 studies;
- 77 measures

of psychological IPV/general IPV.

Most instruments covered less than half of a broad psychological-abuse taxonomy.

Measurement is still incomplete.

## 87. Safety overrides couple-process symmetry

If there are:

- credible threats;
- stalking;
- sexual coercion;
- severe intimidation;
- isolation/control;
- physical violence,

ordinary dyadic "both sides" process analysis is insufficient and can be unsafe.

Route to SAF.

# XVIII. WHO/CDC public-health boundary

## 88. IPV has major health relevance

WHO's 2026 fact sheet classifies intimate-partner violence as a major public-health/human-rights problem.

## 89. Prevalence should not be used to infer a private case

Population prevalence establishes importance.

It does not establish that a particular relationship is abusive.

## 90. Conversely, base-rate caution must not erase direct evidence

If direct evidence of coercion/violence exists, a low prior/base rate cannot dismiss it.

# XIX. Communication measurement channels

## 91. Self-report

Captures:

- subjective perception;
- remembered pattern;
- meaning.

Limitations:

- recall;
- attribution;
- mood;
- shared-method variance.

## 92. Partner report

Adds another perspective.

Still not objective ground truth.

## 93. Laboratory observation

Can code:

- sequence;
- affect;
- demand/withdraw;
- validation;
- hostility.

Limitations:

- short sample;
- selected topic;
- observation reactivity;
- artificial context.

## 94. Naturalistic observation

Can improve ecological validity.

The 2024 one-day study captured communication in ordinary life and predicted later outcomes.

Still limited by:

- sampling window;
- privacy constraints;
- coding validity.

## 95. Daily diary/EMA

Useful for:

- within-person fluctuation;
- conflict occurrence;
- context;
- temporal ordering.

Limitations:

- self-report;
- compliance;
- time resolution.

## 96. Behavioral coding is not universal ground truth

Observer codes are operationalizations.

They require:

- reliability;
- construct validity;
- cultural/language validity.

# XX. Cross-language/cultural measurement

## 97. 2026 communication-coding invariance evidence

A 2026 study tested two observational coding systems across English- and Spanish-speaking couples in samples of:

- N = 416;
- N = 107.

## 98. Results were mixed

One sample supported invariance across codes.

The other supported invariance primarily for positive codes, with nonequivalence for some negative behavior codes.

## 99. Therefore

`observer-coded negative communication means exactly the same thing across language/culture`

is unsafe without validation.

## 100. Attribution evidence shows similar cultural boundary

2026 cross-cultural attribution–satisfaction links differed in strength across countries.

Relationship cognition/communication models need cultural transport checks.

# XXI. Communication and relationship satisfaction: directionality

## 101. Communication predicts later outcomes in several studies

Examples include:

- naturalistic hostility/withdrawal;
- longitudinal demand/withdraw;
- attachment-linked communication.

## 102. Satisfaction can also affect communication

Distressed partners may:

- interpret more negatively;
- communicate less constructively;
- select different topics;
- reduce investment.

## 103. Relationship deterioration can precede separation for years

Longitudinal evidence shows relationship satisfaction often declines before dissolution.

Thus communication measured late in decline may partly be consequence/marker of deteriorating relationship rather than sole cause.

## 104. Reciprocal models are usually more realistic than one-way stories

Use:

`communication ↔ satisfaction/stress/context`

unless stronger design establishes direction.

---

# XXII. Validation/invalidation versus truth

## 105. Validating emotion does not certify factual accuracy

A partner can say:

- "I understand why that felt threatening"

without agreeing:

- "your interpretation of my motive is correct."

## 106. Invalidating a false factual claim and invalidating emotion differ

Disagreeing with an interpretation is not automatically emotional invalidation.

Operationalize:

- what was acknowledged;
- what was disputed;
- how.

## 107. This distinction prevents epistemic collapse

Otherwise "validation" could mean:

- never correcting;
- never disagreeing;
- surrendering one's own perspective.

That is not supported by the construct.

---

# XXIII. Repair and responsibility

## 108. Repair requires identifying what is being repaired

Possibilities:

- hurt;
- misunderstanding;
- trust breach;
- practical failure;
- threat;
- coercion.

## 109. Responsibility is not necessarily 50/50

Dyadic process analysis does not require symmetric responsibility.

## 110. A repair attempt can fail

Possible reasons:

- insufficient acknowledgment;
- repeated behavior continues;
- no behavioral change;
- timing;
- low credibility;
- safety remains absent.

## 111. Forgiveness and repair are not identical

A person can:

- forgive without reconciling;
- reconcile without full trust recovery;
- repair some domains but not others.

Do not collapse outcomes.

---

# XXIV. Mentalization and conflict

## 112. Mentalization is relevant but not magic

A 2026 systematic review included:

- 72 publications;
- 24 studies in meta-analysis for satisfaction.

Higher mentalization correlated moderately with relationship satisfaction:

- ρ ≈ .31,

with high heterogeneity.

## 113. Intervention evidence remains limited

Eight intervention studies were identified, with:

- small samples;
- inconsistent measures;
- heterogeneous effects.

Therefore:

`mentalization training -> established universal conflict solution`

is unsupported.

## 114. Mentalization can fail under threat

Conflict arousal may reduce accurate inference.

This should be treated as a state/context issue, not fixed incapacity.

---

# XXV. Reciprocal escalation and reinforcement

## 115. Conflict behavior can be reinforced

Example:

Partner A escalates demand.

Partner B withdraws.

A increases pressure because withdrawal blocks resolution.

B withdraws further because pressure becomes aversive.

The loop can stabilize even if neither partner intended the final pattern.

## 116. Reinforcement account is a hypothesis, not automatic mechanism

Need temporal/contingency evidence.

LEA controls reinforcement inference.

## 117. Stopping the loop can have different meanings

If B withdraws and A stops demanding:

- withdrawal may be negatively reinforced;
- or a negotiated pause may successfully regulate arousal.

Context/function decides.

---

# XXVI. Power and reciprocity

## 118. Equal turn-taking does not imply equal influence

One partner may control:

- resources;
- housing;
- money;
- social access;
- immigration status;
- threat capacity.

## 119. Visible communication can understate power

A person may appear calm/compliant because:

- disagreement feels unsafe;
- consequences are asymmetric.

## 120. Low overt conflict can coexist with coercive control

Therefore:

`they rarely argue -> relationship is safe`

is invalid.

## 121. High conflict does not automatically imply coercive control

Frequent reciprocal disagreement can occur without domination/fear.

Need pattern-specific evidence.

# XXVII. Relationship boundaries and autonomy

## 122. Autonomy and interdependence can coexist

Healthy closeness need not eliminate:

- privacy;
- individual goals;
- separate relationships;
- self-determination.

## 123. Boundary inference must specify the protected domain

Examples:

- body/sexual consent;
- privacy;
- time;
- money;
- communication;
- social contact.

## 124. "Boundary" can be misused as a control label

A demand restricting another's independent choices does not become noncoercive merely by being called a boundary.

## 125. Context and proportionality matter

A request or limit may be reasonable in one context and controlling in another.

No universal wording test exists.

# XXVIII. Causal evidence hierarchy for conflict/communication

## 126. Level 0 — one recalled episode

Supports description only.

## 127. Level 1 — repeated self-report pattern

Supports perceived relational pattern.

## 128. Level 2 — convergent partner/observer evidence

Strengthens pattern identification.

## 129. Level 3 — temporal/daily sequence

Supports ordering.

## 130. Level 4 — longitudinal prediction controlling baseline

Stronger prognostic/process evidence.

## 131. Level 5 — experimental/causal perturbation

Stronger causal process evidence.

## 132. Level 6 — replicated intervention mechanism

Requires:

- intervention changes process;
- process changes outcome;
- temporal/counterfactual evidence;
- replication.

# XXIX. Common conflict-analysis errors

## Failure A — conflict frequency = relationship quality

Invalid.

## Failure B — demand = unhealthy personality

# XXXII. Evidence-strength map

## High-confidence practical boundaries

1. conflict frequency is not equivalent to relationship quality;
2. conflict should be analyzed as a reciprocal temporal sequence;
3. demand–withdraw is a dyadic pattern rather than one person's fixed trait;
4. demand/withdraw function depends on context and topic;
5. demand–withdraw and hostility predict poorer outcomes in several longitudinal/naturalistic studies;
6. validation can facilitate de-escalation but is not the same as agreement;
7. perceived invalidation is associated with distress but cross-sectional mediation is not causal proof;
8. partner responsiveness is perceptual and behavioral, not pure objective truth;
9. relationship attributions are associated with satisfaction but are culturally patterned;
10. partner motive cannot be read directly from attribution;
11. power perception and actual partner-reported power can differ;
12. power asymmetry is not identical to abuse;
13. coercive control is distinct from ordinary disagreement;
14. psychological aggression/coercive control require pattern/context, not only act counts;
15. bidirectional aggression does not automatically mean symmetrical power/responsibility;
16. self-report, partner report, observation and diary measure different channels;
17. observational communication codes require cultural/language validity;
18. APIM/mediation does not automatically identify causal interpersonal mechanisms;
19. safety/power analysis overrides symmetric communication framing when coercion/violence is present;
20. one conflict episode cannot establish a stable relational style.

## Supported but context-sensitive

1. demand–withdraw as longitudinal risk marker;
2. validation as a co-regulatory process;
3. constructive co-reflection as repair-related process;
4. mentalization as relationship-functioning correlate;
5. responsiveness as a relationship-protective process;
6. attribution patterns as conflict-relevant cognition;
7. physiological coregulation during conflict;

## Not established as universal

1. one healthy conflict style;
2. one unhealthy withdrawal behavior;
3. one universal demand–withdraw gender direction;

## Source-boundary note

Demand–withdraw evidence is treated as a dyadic process association/prognostic pattern, not as a fixed personality type or universal causal mechanism.

The 2026 validation study provides real-time observational evidence for affect transitions during conflict in expectant-parent couples; it does not establish validation as a universal sufficient mechanism.

The emotional-invalidation APIM study is cross-sectional and its indirect effects are not interpreted causally.

Relationship-attribution evidence is explicitly culturally variable; no single Western attribution model is treated as universal.

The 2026 observational-coding study shows that language/cultural measurement invariance can hold for some coding systems/codes and fail for others.

CDC/WHO materials define IPV/public-health boundaries. The 2026 CDC NISVS PDF was text-accessible and relevant definition/table content was inspected. Mandatory screenshot calls were attempted, but the renderer returned cache-miss errors; no claim is made that the PDF was visually reviewed page-by-page.

The IPV/coercive-control literature is used to prevent symmetric communication framing in potentially unsafe contexts, not to perform forensic/legal classification.
