## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a bounded physiology model of strength adaptation sufficient to interpret later programming/testing decisions without collapsing strength into muscle size.

The model must distinguish:

1. muscle size from neural activation/coordination;
2. architecture from hypertrophy;
3. tendon adaptation from muscle adaptation;
4. task skill from general force capacity;
5. dynamic 1RM from isometric MVC and isokinetic torque;
6. joint-angle, velocity and contraction-mode specificity;
7. maximal force from rate of force development;
8. absolute from body-size-normalized strength;
9. maximal strength from strength endurance;
10. trained-task gain from transfer to another task.

### Question type

Chronic neuromuscular / musculoskeletal adaptation / task specificity.

### Evidence rule

“Strength” is not one biological variable.

It is an observed performance outcome produced by:

`muscle force capacity × neural activation/coordination × architecture/MTU mechanics × task skill × test mechanics`

Therefore the test defines part of the construct.

## 3. Why strength can increase without proportional hypertrophy

Strength gain can exceed muscle-size gain because training can improve:

- motor-unit recruitment/discharge behavior;
- agonist activation;
- intermuscular coordination;
- antagonist behavior;
- task-specific skill/technique;
- force expression at the trained joint angle;
- force expression at the trained velocity;
- contraction-mode-specific capacity;
- muscle architecture;
- tendon/MTU force transmission;
- tolerance/familiarization with maximal effort.

This is especially important early in training and in highly task-specific lifts.

Project rule:

**strength gain is not a pure readout of hypertrophy.**

---

## 4. Why hypertrophy can increase without proportional strength gain

A larger muscle usually has greater potential force capacity, but realized strength depends on how effectively that capacity is expressed in the test/task.

Hypertrophy can exceed measured strength gain when:

- training/testing tasks differ;
- neural/skill adaptation is limited;
- the hypertrophy occurs in regions/fibers that do not translate equally to the test;
- architecture changes differ;
- body-size normalization changes the metric;
- the chosen strength test has ceiling/measurement limitations.

Recent within-participant lower-body data in young men show a very strong association between muscle growth and strength gain, supporting muscle size as a major contributor, but this does not make hypertrophy sufficient for all strength outcomes.

Project rule:

**hypertrophy raises force-producing capacity; task-specific neural/mechanical expression determines how much appears in a given strength test.**

---

## 5. Neural adaptation

Resistance training can increase strength before large hypertrophic change is detectable.

Relevant neural mechanisms include:

- voluntary activation;
- motor-unit recruitment;
- discharge rate;
- synchronization/common drive;
- reduced inappropriate antagonist activity;
- improved intermuscular coordination;
- cortical/spinal adaptation.

The 2025 neuromuscular meta-analysis supports RT-related changes in strength, muscle size and EMG-related outcomes, but publication bias/heterogeneity and the limitations of surface EMG prevent treating EMG amplitude as a direct neural-strength mechanism measure.

Project rule:

**neural adaptation is real; surface EMG amplitude is not a complete quantitative explanation of strength gain.**

Detailed mechanisms → NEU.

---

## 6. Hypertrophy contribution

Muscle size is an important determinant of maximal force capacity.

Current evidence:

- RT increases muscle thickness/size and maximal strength;
- within-person lower-body analyses show a strong association between growth and strength gains;
- specific tension/force per PCSA can also differ/change, so size is not the entire explanation.

Canonical rule:

**larger muscle generally increases strength potential, but muscle size does not uniquely determine measured strength.**

---

## 7. Muscle architecture contribution

Architecture affects how muscle volume is translated into force/velocity.

Relevant variables include:

- PCSA;
- pennation angle;
- fascicle length;
- moment-arm interaction at the joint.

Resistance training can alter architecture, but effects are muscle/protocol dependent.

Potential roles:

- greater pennation can permit more contractile material in parallel;
- fascicle length can influence shortening capacity/velocity;
- PCSA relates to force potential.

Project rule:

**architecture modifies force expression but should not be reduced to “more pennation = more strength” or “longer fascicles = stronger.”**

Detailed external mechanics → MEC.

---

## 8. Tendon stiffness contribution

Tendon stiffness can adapt to chronic mechanical loading.

Potential functional roles include:

- force transmission;
- muscle-fascicle operating behavior;
- electromechanical delay;
- rapid force expression.

The 2026 paired meta-analysis found both strength and tendon stiffness increased under chronic mechanical loading, but causal/functional interpretation of their parallel changes remained uncertain.

A 2026 RCT in older men also found high-intensity RT increased both 1RM and patellar-tendon stiffness, whereas BFR produced different tendon adaptation.

Project rule:

**tendon stiffness is a plausible contributor to force transmission/RFD, but it is not established as a simple proportional cause of maximal-strength gain.**

Detailed tendon adaptation → PHY-12.

---

## 9. Intermuscular coordination

Multi-joint strength requires coordinated contribution of multiple muscles.

Training can improve:

- timing;
- relative activation;
- stabilization;
- agonist/synergist coordination;
- reduction of task-irrelevant co-contraction.

This is one reason the trained lift can improve more than an isolated non-trained force test.

Project rule:

**intermuscular coordination belongs to strength expression and task transfer, not to muscle size.**

Detailed control → NEU.

---

## 10. Skill / technique contribution

A 1RM squat, bench press, deadlift or machine lift is partly a motor skill.

Repeated practice can improve:

- setup;
- bar path;
- bracing;
- sequencing;
- timing;
- use of advantageous joint positions;
- confidence/familiarization with maximal effort.

Therefore:

**1RM strength is not a pure physiological muscle-force assay.**

Skill/technique detail → NEU/TEC.

---

## 11. Specificity of force production

The strongest current synthesis is task specificity.

The 2025 meta-analysis of dynamic RT found:

- dynamic strength improved with a moderate-to-large effect;
- transfer to non-trained isometric strength was substantially smaller;
- hypertrophy and muscle-activity changes did not explain the full task-specific difference.

Canonical rule:

**strength transfer is graded, not absent: the closer the test is to the trained task, the larger the expected gain.**

---

## 12. Joint-angle specificity

Strength is joint-angle dependent because of:

- force–length behavior;
- moment arms;
- muscle architecture;
- neural/task specificity.

Isometric training often produces the largest gains near the trained angle, with transfer to nearby angles.

The 2019 controlled isometric study found joint-angle-specific torque gains, while activation changes did not show the same clear angle-specific pattern.

Project rule:

**joint-angle specificity exists, especially for isometric training, but adaptation is not restricted to one exact angle.**

---

## 13. Velocity specificity

Strength is velocity-specific because force capacity and coordination depend on shortening/lengthening velocity and task intent.

Evidence supports:

- high-load/maximal-strength training strongly improving high-force tasks;
- explosive/ballistic training preferentially improving high-velocity performance;
- maximal intended acceleration can matter even when actual movement is slow under heavy load;
- transfer exists across velocities but is incomplete.

A 2026 lower-body trial showed maximal-strength and hypertrophy training improved squat 1RM more than unloaded explosive training, while explosive training had greater specificity to low-load/high-velocity jumping.

Canonical rule:

**training velocity/intent changes which part of the force–velocity spectrum is practiced; no single velocity maximizes every strength expression.**

---

## 14. Contraction-mode specificity

PHY-04 established contraction-mode physiology.

Current strength evidence adds:

- eccentric-only training preferentially improves eccentric MVC;
- concentric and eccentric training both transfer to concentric/isometric outcomes;
- isometric training improves isometric strength more than dynamic training on average;
- dynamic/isokinetic transfer can still occur.

Canonical rule:

**strength adaptation is contraction-mode specific but not mode-exclusive.**

---

## 15. Rate of force development

RFD describes how rapidly force rises from contraction onset.

It is not equivalent to maximal force.

Determinants depend on time window:

- very early RFD is strongly influenced by rapid neural activation/motor-unit discharge;
- later RFD increasingly reflects maximal force capacity and muscle properties;
- tendon/MTU mechanics can contribute.

The 2020 meta-analysis showed RT increases early, late and maximal RFD; faster training, explosive intent and movement-pattern similarity were favorable for peak RFD.

Project rule:

**RFD is a distinct rapid-force outcome; it must be measured and trained with time/task specificity.**

Detailed explosive-power physiology → PHY-09.

---

## 16. Maximal voluntary force

Maximal voluntary force (MVF/MVC) is the maximum force/torque voluntarily produced under a defined test condition.

It depends on:

- contractile capacity;
- voluntary activation;
- joint angle;
- contraction mode;
- task setup;
- familiarization/motivation.

Therefore:

**MVC is not interchangeable with 1RM.**

1RM includes dynamic movement skill and external mechanics; MVC can isolate a defined joint/contraction condition more tightly.

Project rule:

**always state the strength test before interpreting a strength change.**

---

## 17. Absolute vs relative strength

### Absolute strength
Unnormalized force/torque or external load.

Examples:
- 180 kg squat 1RM;
- 300 N·m knee-extension MVC.

### Relative strength
Strength scaled to body size.

Common simple form:
`strength / body mass`.

But simple ratio scaling can be biased when strength does not scale linearly with body mass.

Allometric or lean-mass/PCSA normalization may answer different questions.

Project rule:

**absolute and relative strength are different outcomes; the denominator must be stated.**

A person can gain absolute strength while relative strength changes little if body mass rises substantially.

---

## 18. Strength endurance

Strength endurance is the ability to sustain/repeat force production over repeated contractions or time.

It is influenced by:

- maximal strength reserve;
- local metabolic capacity;
- fatigue resistance;
- task/load;
- pacing;
- repetition strategy.

RT improves muscular endurance in the ACSM 2026 synthesis.

However maximal strength and strength endurance are not proportional:

- greater maximal strength can make a fixed absolute load relatively easier;
- endurance at a fixed %1RM also depends strongly on metabolic/fatigue properties.

The 2026 failure-vs-nonfailure meta-analysis found no clear muscular-endurance advantage of training to failure.

Canonical rule:

**strength endurance is a distinct task-specific outcome, not simply “more maximal strength.”**

---

## 19. Transfer of strength between tasks

Transfer depends on similarity across:

- movement pattern;
- joint angles/ROM;
- contraction mode;
- velocity;
- muscle groups;
- external mechanics;
- coordination/skill.

Current meta-analytic evidence shows dynamic RT has clear but limited transfer to untrained isometric strength.

Therefore:

`general force capacity + task-specific skill/coordination = observed performance`

Canonical rule:

**more general adaptations transfer; more task-specific adaptations transfer less as task distance increases.**

---

## 20. Strength prescription implications from current evidence

For voluntary dynamic strength in healthy adults:

- higher loads (approximately ≥80% 1RM in the ACSM review classification) generally enhance maximal strength more than lighter-load prescriptions;
- multiple sets and repeated weekly exposure improve strength;
- a 2026 meta-analysis identified non-linear dosage–strength relationships and muscle-group-specific predictors;
- there is no evidence that simply maximizing total dosage indefinitely continues to increase the rate of strength gain.

Important boundary:

**prescription effects partly reflect specificity to the strength test (often 1RM), not only deeper “general strength physiology.”**

Detailed dose prescription remains PRO.

---

## 21. Why size and strength can dissociate

Integrated explanation:

### Strength > hypertrophy
Possible when:
- neural/skill adaptation is large;
- test is highly practiced;
- tendon/architecture changes improve force expression;
- baseline muscle size is already adequate;
- early training phase.

### Hypertrophy > strength
Possible when:
- muscle grows but test skill changes little;
- test differs from training;
- body mass rises and relative strength is used;
- architecture/neural transfer is limited;
- strength metric is poorly matched to the hypertrophied muscle/region.

Canonical rule:

**hypertrophy changes capacity; neural/skill/mechanical specificity changes expression.**

## 23. Established project-level model

Observed strength in a specific test can be represented as:

`contractile capacity (size/PCSA/specific tension)`
`× neural activation/coordination`
`× architecture + muscle–tendon mechanics`
`× task skill/specificity`
`× test conditions`.

This is not a literal multiplicative equation for calculation; it is a causal organization model.

Therefore:

**strength is a task-specific system output rather than a direct proxy for muscle size.**

## 24. Training-relevant implications

1. **Muscle size matters strongly, but strength is not muscle size alone.**
2. **Early or task-specific strength gains can occur with little measurable hypertrophy.**
3. **1RM includes skill/technique and should not be treated as isolated muscle-force capacity.**
4. **MVC, 1RM, isokinetic torque, RFD and endurance are different strength constructs.**
5. **Specificity exists across task, joint angle, velocity and contraction mode.**
6. **Transfer exists but usually weakens as task similarity decreases.**
7. **Heavy loading has stronger specificity for maximal dynamic strength.**
8. **Explosive intent/velocity is especially relevant to rapid force expression.**
9. **Tendon stiffness can adapt, but its causal contribution to maximal-strength gains is not a fixed proportion.**
10. **Absolute and relative strength require different interpretation.**
11. **Strength endurance requires separate measurement from maximal strength.**
12. **Program effectiveness must be judged against the exact target strength outcome.**
