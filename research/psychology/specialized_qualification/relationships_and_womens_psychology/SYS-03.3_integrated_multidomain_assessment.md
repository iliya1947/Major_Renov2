## 4. Multidomain scenario A — public library volunteer program
Fictional task: Over six scheduled sorting sessions, a volunteer arrives at four; two nonattendance entries are summaries of one original attendance log. The volunteer reports enjoying the work and difficulty arranging transport. A coordinator interprets a brief tense conversation as disinterest. A population study in a different context associates conscientiousness with attendance. A free additional role is offered, with higher time demands. Later the booking record is corrected: one listed absence occurred when the building was closed, while the other session was open.

Response:
C1. Separate scheduled dates, actual building access, original attendance entries, copied summaries, reported enjoyment/transport problem and the coordinator's interpretation. The two summaries do not add independent observations. Establish dates and closure in the stipulated corrected booking record.
C2. Trait standing, current affect, stated motivation, available transport, participation, interpersonal perception and willingness to accept a new commitment differ. A tense expression is not a measure of motive.
C3. Compare stable task preference with logistical constraints, fatigue or other demands, changing goals, interaction-specific discomfort and misreading of tone. More than one can coexist.
C4. Neither population association nor the earlier descriptive sequence identifies the cause of nonattendance. The relevant group construct, target and task differ; no numeric individual probability or personality classification.
C5. Prospective qualitative test: for the next two actually open sessions with ordinary transport available and a voluntarily accepted invitation, record whether the volunteer attends. If sessions are closed, access unknown or attendance unobserved, record respectively ineligible or unresolved. Repeated nonattendance with verified access weakens a pure transport-only account, but does not establish loss of interest; attendance is compatible with several models. No manufactured opportunity or covert observation.
C6. Recalculate eligible prior attendance: four attendances among five known open sessions, one absence; original four of six scheduled sessions remains historical description, not comparable denominator. Reopen claims about absence frequency, transport, prediction and role suitability derived from the incorrect opportunity count. Preserve other observations and historical predictions. Do not count the duplicate summaries twice.
C7. Compare current volunteering, declining the extra role, or an independently chosen lower-demand role if available. Extra duties may serve program staffing while imposing time and travel costs. The volunteer's own preferences and schedule determine suitability; the coordinator's staffing goal is not their goal. No assumed benefit from a change.
C8. Respect voluntary choice and refusal. No pressure, surveillance or attempts to use perceived personality vulnerabilities. Legitimate records and optional clarification are enough for the bounded decision.
Self-review: C1–C8 explicitly addressed. 8/8 checklist coverage; no critical error identified.

## 5. Multidomain scenario B — community choir scheduling
Fictional task: A choir committee chooses between venue options. After rehearsals were moved to a noisier location, two singers report irritation and fewer completed rehearsals. An observer claims each singer causes the other's irritation. A later notice shows the noise began at the same time as an independent schedule change. Venue A has better acoustics but less flexible access; B has adequate acoustics and more flexibility; C is unavailable due to a booking constraint. The committee can wait for a free official availability update before committing, but delay may lose the current booking.

Response:
C1. Distinguish reported irritation, completed rehearsal counts, actual venue/schedule records and an observer's causal narrative. The concurrent changes and shared source notice are not independent demonstrations of interpersonal influence.
C2. Own-state persistence, cross-person association, common input, within-person changes, group averages, feasibility, satisfaction and outcome are different. Acoustics and access represent distinct objectives.
C3. Compare shared environmental effect, schedule fatigue, independent preferences, reciprocal influence and measurement/selection changes. The same initial sequence fits multiple graphs.
C4. Draw candidate time-indexed common-input and partner-coupling paths, not a known causal graph. With two simultaneous changes and selected observations, lagged association does not identify an effect. A simple common-input model can compete with a more complex feedback model; no inferred effect size.
C5. Define ordinary subsequent rehearsals with comparable schedule/access, contemporaneous voluntary reports and completed-rehearsal counts. A decrease in irritation when the noise is absent is compatible with environmental effects but is not discriminating if scheduling also changes. A comparable contrast or suitable independent design would be needed for causal inference; missing reports remain missing.
C6. The notice reopens any noise-only causal explanation, dependent venue forecasts and decision comparison; it does not disprove all noise effects. Preserve actual counts and earlier reports. Record both alternative causes and revise scope; retain historical forecast versions.
C7. Exclude unavailable C; compare A and B by acoustic quality, accessible time, participant preferences, cost, reversibility and possibility of lost booking. Neither is automatically best. The update is instrumentally useful if it can alter feasible choice enough to justify waiting; no probabilities or weights are supplied, so use a conditional comparison and discuss actual priority rather than fabricate expected utilities.
C8. Use ordinary consensual scheduling and voluntary reports, not experimental distress or assigning blame. An individual may opt out. Safety/access constraints precede optimization.
Self-review: 8/8 checklist coverage; no critical error identified.

## 6. Multidomain scenario C — forecast and correction
Fictional task: A training-center analyst issues a conditional forecast of passing a short equipment qualification test for each of five scheduled sessions: stipulated model probability .7 and stipulated benchmark .5, only if a test is offered. Synthetic resolution records: [{"opportunity":true,"outcome":1,"p":0.7,"baseline":0.5},{"opportunity":false,"outcome":null,"p":0.7,"baseline":0.5},{"opportunity":true,"outcome":0,"p":0.7,"baseline":0.5},{"opportunity":true,"outcome":null,"p":0.7,"baseline":0.5},{"opportunity":true,"outcome":1,"p":0.7,"baseline":0.5}]. The analyst proposes dropping the failed test because it contradicts a prior hypothesis of increasing skill. Later the record of the third session is corrected: the original failure outcome is independently confirmed, but the analyst's explanation citing unfamiliar equipment was mistaken; the standard equipment was used. The center must decide whether to keep using this forecast as a planning aid.

Response:
C1. Preserve the frozen five forecast entries, actual eligibility, recorded outcomes, missing observation and independent correction. The third session remains a failure; correction concerns explanatory evidence, not outcome.
C2. Test offered, pass/fail, skill level/change, explanation, subjective confidence, probability calibration and decision usefulness differ.
C3. Competing explanations include actual skill variation, test conditions, imperfect model, measurement noise and prior overconfidence. Unfamiliar equipment loses support for the third session; other explanations remain hypotheses.
C4. Forecast probabilities are deliberately stipulated toy inputs, not derived from data or validated for real learners. This small constructed series establishes neither a causal skill mechanism nor calibration. No population-to-person inference.
C5. Resolution rule specified in task: score only offered tests with known outcomes. One canceled test is ineligible, one offered but unobserved remains unresolved. Keep all five in ledger. Three scored outcomes, one failure. Executed JavaScript using binary Brier convention: model 0.223333, baseline 0.250000. Model has lower loss in this deliberately constructed subset; no claim of statistically significant improvement, prospective validation or calibrated probabilities. The failure cannot be excluded to improve results.
C6. Reopen equipment-context premise, dependent explanation, model revision and decision analysis, not the confirmed failure. Preserve original prediction and scoring. Record revised version for future issues rather than retroactively editing history.
C7. Compare continued restricted use with a simple benchmark, further evaluation, or suspension of forecast-based planning if errors have substantial costs. The choice depends on real consequences, validation data and alternatives; no evidence here establishes production readiness. Use independent future resolved outcomes and a pre-specified benchmark/criterion before consequential use.
C8. The learner's participation is voluntary; do not label ability from a forecast, compel a test or collect private information. No safety decisions on an unvalidated score.
Self-review: 8/8 checklist coverage; no critical error identified. Numerical exercise executed, not an independent test.
