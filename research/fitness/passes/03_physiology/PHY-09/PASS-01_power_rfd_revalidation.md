## 3. Power as force × velocity

Mechanical power is the rate of doing mechanical work.

For translational motion:

`P = F × v`

where force and velocity are instantaneous/vector-dependent quantities.

For rotational motion:

`P = torque × angular velocity`.

This has a critical implication:

- maximal force can occur at very low/zero velocity;
- maximal velocity can occur at low force;
- maximal power generally occurs where meaningful force and velocity coexist.

Project rule:

**power is not force alone and not velocity alone.**

Detailed external/system mechanics → MEC.

---

## 4. Measurement boundary: muscle power vs external mechanical power

In practical resistance exercise, reported power can differ depending on:

- whether force is measured or estimated;
- whether velocity refers to bar/load, body center of mass or joint angular velocity;
- whether body mass is included;
- whether only concentric/propulsive phase is analyzed;
- device/software filtering and sampling;
- exercise technique.

The 2026 methodological review argues that mechanical power estimates during complex multijoint lifts can have limited physiological specificity.

Project rule:

**a “watts” value is not universal across devices/exercises; define what system, phase and signal produced the value.**

This is a TST/MEC boundary.

---

## 5. Rate of force development

RFD describes the slope of force/torque rise over time:

`RFD = ΔF / Δt`.

It is a rapid-force construct rather than a power metric.

RFD can be quantified in multiple windows:

- early RFD;
- later RFD;
- peak RFD;
- impulse/force at defined time points.

Physiological determinants vary with the time window.

Current model:

- early RFD: strongly constrained by rapid voluntary activation and motor-unit discharge behavior;
- later RFD: increasingly influenced by maximal force capacity, muscle contractile properties and MTU mechanics.

Project rule:

**RFD ≠ maximal strength and RFD ≠ mechanical power.**

---

## 6. RFD trainability

RFD can improve after:

- explosive/ballistic training;
- heavy resistance training;
- protocols emphasizing rapid intended force production.

The RFD meta-analysis found improvements in early, late and maximal RFD, with faster movement, explosive intent and movement-pattern similarity tending to favor peak RFD adaptations.

Important boundary:

**the exact RFD result depends heavily on the test method/time window.**

A program may improve one RFD metric without identically improving another.

Detailed neural mechanisms → NEU.

---

## 7. Peak power

Peak power is the highest power value reached during a movement or defined analysis window.

It is sensitive to:

- exercise;
- load;
- technique;
- measurement system;
- sampling/filtering;
- whether body mass is included;
- phase definition.

Peak power captures a short maximal expression.

Project rule:

**peak power is not equivalent to sustained/mean power and cannot be compared blindly across tests.**

---

## 8. Mean power

Mean power averages power over a specified movement/phase/time interval.

It can differ from peak power because:

- a brief high-power spike raises peak power without necessarily raising average power equally;
- fatigue/velocity loss can reduce mean power across repetitions;
- different phase definitions alter the denominator.

The 2026 rest-redistribution meta-analysis illustrates that interventions can improve mean power without a statistically clear peak-power difference.

Project rule:

**peak and mean power are separate test outcomes and must be named explicitly.**

---

## 9. Velocity of contraction / movement

Muscle shortening velocity, fascicle velocity, joint angular velocity and external-load velocity are not identical.

In gym/research practice, bar/device velocity is often an external task measure.

It can be useful for:

- monitoring repetition speed;
- inferring task force–velocity behavior under standardized conditions;
- managing velocity loss.

But:

**external movement velocity is not a direct measurement of every muscle fascicle's shortening velocity.**

This preserves the PHY-04 internal-vs-external movement boundary.

---

## 10. Load effect on power

Changing external load changes both force and movement velocity.

General task-level pattern:

- very high loads → high force, low velocity;
- very low loads → high velocity, low external force;
- intermediate regions can maximize measured power in many tasks.

However, there is **no universal percentage of 1RM that maximizes power across all exercises**.

Current evidence examples:

1. ACSM 2026 synthesis: chronic power outcomes were enhanced by moderate loads, classified broadly as **30–70% 1RM**, power RT and fast concentric intent.
2. 2024 dose-response modeling: power outcomes were modeled as highest around low-to-moderate loads, roughly **40–70% 1RM**.
3. 2026 power-clean meta-analysis: acute peak power was lower at ≤40% 1RM, but there was no clear superiority among >40–70%, >70–85% and >85% zones.

These are not contradictory because:

- exercises differ;
- power calculation differs;
- acute power expression is not the same estimand as chronic power adaptation.

Canonical rule:

**load–power relation is exercise/test specific; broad moderate-load guidance does not define one universal optimal power load.**

---

## 11. Acute optimal-load fallacy

An acute load that maximizes measured peak power in one exercise does not automatically maximize chronic power gains.

Reasons:

- acute expression ≠ chronic adaptation;
- force–velocity deficits differ among individuals;
- sport/task target can require high-force or high-velocity emphasis rather than maximal instantaneous power;
- measurement conventions alter the apparent optimum;
- strength base can limit power development.

The 2026 review specifically argues that a single optimal-power-load approach neglects task specificity and individual force–velocity characteristics.

Project rule:

**do not prescribe chronic power training solely from the load that gives the highest acute watt value.**

---

## 12. Neuromuscular determinants of power

Power expression depends on both force-producing and velocity-producing capacities.

Relevant neural factors include:

- rapid motor-unit recruitment/discharge;
- high intended acceleration;
- intermuscular coordination;
- task timing/skill;
- reduced unnecessary co-contraction.

Relevant muscular/MTU factors include:

- maximal force capacity;
- muscle size/PCSA;
- fiber/contractile phenotype;
- architecture/fascicle behavior;
- tendon/elastic behavior;
- force–velocity properties.

Project rule:

**power is a systems outcome of rapid neural activation plus force/velocity capacity and task mechanics.**

Detailed neural control → NEU.  
Detailed mechanics → MEC.

## 13. Maximal strength as a power foundation

Higher maximal strength can raise the force side of the force–velocity continuum and increase power potential.

This is especially relevant when force capacity is limiting.

But strength alone does not guarantee maximal power because the person must also express force rapidly and at meaningful movement velocity.

The current 2026 review identifies maximal strength as a major predictor/foundation of power production while still recommending ballistic/speed-oriented exposure for power development.

Project rule:

**maximal strength expands power potential; velocity-specific/ballistic practice helps express that potential rapidly.**

---

## 14. Power training vs maximal-strength training

### Maximal-strength training

Primary characteristics:

- relatively high external load;
- high force;
- low actual movement velocity under heavy loads;
- often maximal intended acceleration;
- outcome specificity to maximal force/1RM.

### Power-oriented training

Primary characteristics:

- intent to produce force rapidly;
- preserve high movement velocity;
- select loads/exercises allowing high force–velocity product;
- often ballistic/Olympic-derived/jump/throw or fast-concentric resistance tasks;
- avoid excessive within-set velocity degradation when quality is the target.

ACSM 2026 finds power improved particularly with:

- moderate loads;
- low-to-moderate volume;
- Olympic-style weightlifting;
- power RT / fast concentric phase.

Canonical rule:

**maximal-strength training emphasizes force capacity; power training emphasizes rapid force expression at meaningful velocity.**

They overlap and can complement each other.

---

## 15. Ballistic vs non-ballistic boundary

Ballistic tasks allow continued acceleration through the movement and often release/project the body or implement.

This can reduce the need for deliberate end-range deceleration seen in some conventional lifts.

Examples can include:

- jumps;
- throws;
- Olympic-weightlifting derivatives;
- some jump squats.

Project rule:

**ballistic intent/exercise selection can improve high-velocity specificity, but no single ballistic method is universally superior.**

The 2026 advanced-method network meta-analysis found no clear universal superiority of advanced RT methods for power adaptations.

---

## 16. Force–velocity profile

A force–velocity profile can describe a person's task-specific force and velocity capacities.

Potential uses:

- identify whether a task appears more force- or velocity-limited;
- monitor shifts in F0/V0/Pmax;
- inform hypothesis generation for load selection.

But evidence is not sufficient to treat individualized F–V profiling as a universally superior prescription system.

A 2026 meta-analysis found individualized F–V training favored some profile variables and vertical jump but not Pmax or sprint, with high heterogeneity.

A November 2026 RCT in velocity-deficient older adults found different F0/V0 adaptations from matched vs deliberately mismatched loading but no group difference in power or functional performance.

Project rule:

**F–V profiling can describe adaptation and guide hypotheses; it is not yet a universal personalization rule.**

---

## 17. Power adaptation is task specific

A person can improve:

- maximal force;
- jump power;
- barbell peak power;
- RFD;
- sprint acceleration

to different degrees.

Why:

- movement pattern differs;
- load/velocity differs;
- stretch-shortening-cycle use differs;
- coordination/skill differs;
- test calculation differs.

Project rule:

**power is not one transferable scalar capacity; test/task specificity remains substantial.**

## 19. Established project-level model

Power adaptation is best organized as:

`force capacity`
`+`
`rapid neural activation / intent`
`+`
`velocity capacity`
`+`
`muscle–tendon architecture/mechanics`
`+`
`task skill/specificity`
`→ task-specific mechanical power`.

RFD is related but separate:

`rapid rise in force over time`

rather than:

`force × movement velocity`.

Therefore:

**maximal strength, RFD and mechanical power overlap but are not interchangeable outcomes.**

## 20. Training-relevant implications

1. **Power requires force and velocity simultaneously.**
2. **RFD is not power.**
3. **Peak power and mean power are separate outcomes.**
4. **A watt value is test/device/exercise specific.**
5. **Moderate-load fast-concentric training is broadly supported for power, but no universal optimal %1RM exists.**
6. **Acute maximal power load is not automatically the best chronic prescription.**
7. **Maximal strength is an important foundation, especially for force-deficient individuals.**
8. **Ballistic/high-velocity intent is important for expressing force rapidly.**
9. **Power adaptations are specific to movement pattern and force–velocity region.**
10. **F–V profiling can inform hypotheses but is not a universally validated personalization algorithm.**
11. **Power quality can be degraded by excessive within-set fatigue/velocity loss; detailed stopping rules remain PRO/TST.**
12. **Program effectiveness must be judged against the exact target power/RFD task.**
