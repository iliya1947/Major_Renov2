# RES-20 — Appraisal of guidelines and position stands

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**  
Дата: 2026-11-16  
Область: 13 — Методология исследований

## 0. Question Card

### Decision
Как оценивать профессиональные guidelines/position stands так, чтобы не путать авторитет организации с качеством методологии и силой конкретной рекомендации.

### Question type
Guideline appraisal / evidence-to-recommendation.

### Critical outcomes
1. Transparent question/evidence methods.
2. Certainty grading.
3. Recommendation strength.
4. Evidence-to-decision process.
5. Conflicts/funding.
6. Recency/update policy.
7. Applicability.

### Closure criterion
Есть project guideline-appraisal card sufficient to evaluate ACSM/NSCA/IOC/clinical documents later.

---

## 1. Guideline is not primary evidence

Guideline/position stand:
- summarizes;
- interprets;
- converts evidence into recommendations.

It can be highly useful for:
1. broad map;
2. safety standards;
3. consensus;
4. implementation.

But it is not automatically stronger than the underlying evidence.

A recommendation can be:
- strong with high certainty;
- conditional with high certainty because trade-offs/preferences vary;
- occasionally strong with lower certainty for exceptional reasons.

Need inspect the pathway.

## 2. Organization prestige is not methodology

“ACSM says”, “NSCA says”, “IOC says” is useful context, but not full appraisal.

Questions:
1. How was evidence searched?
2. Was systematic review used?
3. How was certainty graded?
4. How were recommendations formulated?
5. Who voted/decided?
6. Conflicts?
7. Update date?
8. Does recommendation actually apply to target population?

## 3. Scope and target population

A guideline should specify:
- population;
- setting;
- users;
- intervention/issue;
- outcomes.

A recommendation for:
- athletes;
- cardiac patients;
- older adults
may be indirect for healthy detrained adult.

Need recommendation-level applicability, not document-level label.

## 4. Evidence identification

Stronger guideline process includes:
1. explicit questions/PICO;
2. systematic searches;
3. eligibility criteria;
4. evidence tables;
5. risk-of-bias assessment;
6. certainty grading.

Weak:
- narrative expert selection;
- unclear literature search;
- no evidence tables;
- recommendation not traceable to sources.

## 5. GRADE recommendation logic

GRADE separates:
1. certainty of evidence;
2. magnitude/balance of benefits and harms;
3. values/preferences;
4. resources/costs;
5. equity;
6. acceptability;
7. feasibility.

Recommendation is therefore broader than effect estimate.

## 6. Strength of recommendation

Current GRADE guidance emphasizes:
- direction: for/against;
- strength: strong/conditional (and related categories where appropriate).

Strong usually means panel believes most people should follow recommendation.

Conditional:
reasonable alternatives may fit different individuals/context.

For fitness:
conditional recommendation can be ideal when multiple programs produce similar outcomes.

## 7. Certainty vs recommendation strength

Not identical.

High-certainty evidence that:
two routines produce similar hypertrophy
may lead to:
- conditional choice based on preference/time.

Low-certainty evidence can sometimes support stronger safety recommendation if potential harm severe and alternative low burden, but this requires explicit justification.

## 8. Evidence-to-Decision process

Good guideline should explain:
1. benefits;
2. harms;
3. certainty;
4. values/preferences;
5. resource implications;
6. feasibility;
7. acceptability;
8. equity where relevant.

For our one-person project, priority later:
- efficacy;
- safety;
- time;
- cost;
- equipment;
- ADHD adherence;
- reversibility.

## 9. Conflict of interest

Need:
1. declarations;
2. financial/nonfinancial conflicts;
3. management;
4. recusal when relevant;
5. funder role.

Conflict ≠ automatic invalidity.
Unmanaged conflict reduces confidence.

## 10. Panel composition

Look for:
- subject experts;
- methodologists;
- clinicians where needed;
- stakeholders/users;
- patient/public input in clinical guidelines.

Position stands may be expert-focused; that is acceptable if transparent, but should not be mistaken for independent systematic review.

## 11. External review / quality assurance

RIGHT reporting framework asks whether guideline:
- underwent external review;
- underwent quality assurance.

External review does not guarantee correctness but improves transparency/error detection.

## 12. Update policy / recency

Check:
1. publication date;
2. evidence search end date;
3. planned update;
4. major new studies after search;
5. superseded version.

For fast-moving sports science:
search date can matter more than publication date.

## 13. RIGHT Statement

RIGHT is a **reporting guideline**, not a quality-assessment tool.

It checks reporting of:
- background;
- evidence;
- recommendations;
- review/QA;
- funding/conflicts;
- limitations.

Project rule:
RIGHT completeness helps transparency, but does not prove recommendation quality.

## 14. AGREE II concept

AGREE II is widely used for guideline methodological quality/applicability.

Core domains include:
1. scope/purpose;
2. stakeholder involvement;
3. rigor of development;
4. clarity;
5. applicability;
6. editorial independence.

Project use:
for high-stakes clinical/medical guidelines when deeper appraisal needed.

## 15. Position stand vs clinical practice guideline

### Position stand
Often:
- expert synthesis;
- professional scope;
- practical recommendations;
- may vary in formal grading.

### Clinical practice guideline
Ideally:
- formal evidence review;
- certainty grading;
- explicit recommendation process.

Do not judge one solely by title.
Inspect methods.

## 16. Consensus statement

Consensus can be useful when:
- evidence sparse;
- standardized definitions needed;
- practical policy required.

But:
consensus strength is not evidence certainty.

Project wording:
“expert consensus recommends…” not “evidence proves…”

## 17. Recommendation-level appraisal

A document can have:
- strong evidence for one recommendation;
- weak consensus for another.

Never give one global trust score.

Assess recommendation/outcome-specific basis.

## 18. Citation chain

For any high-impact recommendation:
1. guideline recommendation;
2. supporting systematic review;
3. key primary studies.

If recommendation wording seems stronger than evidence:
trace chain.

This is especially important for:
- exact sets/reps;
- contraindications;
- supplements;
- ADHD claims.

## 19. Exercise-science red flags

1. exact prescription with no uncertainty;
2. old references despite new evidence;
3. mechanistic evidence used as direct outcome;
4. no distinction trained/untrained;
5. no adherence consideration;
6. no evidence grading;
7. conflicts undisclosed;
8. recommendations stronger than cited meta-analysis.

## 20. Recommendation applicability to Ilya

For each recommendation:
1. population match;
2. training status;
3. health status;
4. equipment;
5. setting;
6. goal;
7. burden;
8. ADHD/adherence;
9. alternatives;
10. certainty.

A strong general recommendation can still require personalization in implementation.

## 21. Guideline hierarchy in project

For current technical/medical questions prefer:

1. current official evidence-based guideline/position stand from relevant authority;
2. transparent systematic evidence underlying it;
3. primary evidence for disputed/new details.

Not:
- blog;
- influencer summary;
- certification prep page
when primary guideline exists.

Professional educational materials may still help map scope.

## 22. Project guideline appraisal card

1. Document/version/date.
2. Developer.
3. Question/scope.
4. Target population.
5. Search end date.
6. Systematic review method?
7. RoB assessment?
8. Certainty system?
9. Recommendation strength labeled?
10. EtD/consensus process?
11. Benefits/harms considered?
12. Values/preferences?
13. Feasibility/resources?
14. Conflicts/funding?
15. External review?
16. Update policy?
17. Recommendation directly applicable?
18. Newer conflicting evidence?
19. Overall confidence in this **specific recommendation**.
20. Need to inspect underlying studies?

## 23. Trust classes

Project-specific:

### G-A
Transparent systematic evidence + certainty grading + EtD + managed conflicts + current.

### G-B
Good evidence synthesis but some methodological/reporting limitations.

### G-C
Expert consensus/position with useful scope but weak formal grading.

### G-D
Opaque recommendation, outdated, severe methodological/conflict concerns.

Not official AGREE/GRADE categories.

## 24. Project rules

1. Authority is not substitute for method.
2. Assess recommendation, not just document.
3. Certainty ≠ recommendation strength.
4. RIGHT is reporting, not quality appraisal.
5. Consensus ≠ high certainty.
6. Search end date matters.
7. Conflicts must be disclosed/managed.
8. Trace important recommendations to underlying evidence.
9. New guideline does not automatically beat older stronger methodology.
10. Apply to Ilya only after applicability check.

## 25. Confidence

### HIGH
- GRADE separates certainty and recommendation strength;
- recommendation process should consider net effects and contextual factors;
- reporting guidelines like RIGHT are not themselves methodological quality instruments;
- conflicts, search methods, evidence grading and update recency are important appraisal dimensions.

### MODERATE
- AGREE II remains a widely used guideline-appraisal framework; exact project use will depend on high-stakes need.

### PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC
- G-A to G-D trust classes and project appraisal card.
