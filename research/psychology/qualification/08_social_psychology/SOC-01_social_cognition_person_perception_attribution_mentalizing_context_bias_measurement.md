# SOC-01 — Social cognition foundations: person perception, attribution, mentalizing, social inference, context, bias, and measurement boundaries

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public social-cognition evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

STR established stress, coping, and resilience without treating social context as a transparent causal explanation. SOC begins the next dependency:

**How do people infer other people's traits, goals, beliefs, intentions, emotions, and social meaning from incomplete behavior and contextual information, and what limits the accuracy of those inferences?**

High-risk errors to prevent:
- "behavior reveals personality";
- "one act reveals intention";
- "facial appearance reveals trustworthiness";
- "first impression = accurate personality judgment";
- "the situation is irrelevant if behavior is strong";
- "fundamental attribution error is a universal law";
- "actor–observer bias is robust and general";
- "mentalizing = empathy";
- "perspective taking = knowing what another person thinks";
- "Theory of Mind is one unitary trait";
- "Reading the Mind in the Eyes measures global mentalizing ability";
- "high self-reported empathy = high social-cognitive accuracy";
- "observer agreement = truth";
- "group stereotype = individual trait";
- "cultural norm differences = measurement error";
- "algorithmic social inference = objective mind reading";
- "neural activation = another person's mental state decoded".

## Core rule

**Social cognition is inference under uncertainty. Observable behavior, appearance, language, context, social roles, prior knowledge, and cultural norms provide evidence about another person, but none uniquely identifies hidden traits or mental states.**

Valid reasoning must separate:

\`observation -> social cue/context -> candidate mental/trait explanations -> situational constraints -> inference uncertainty -> accuracy criterion -> measurement validity\`.

## 1. RDoC social-process scaffold

Current NIMH RDoC defines **Systems for Social Processes** as systems mediating responses in interpersonal settings, including perception and interpretation of others' actions.

Relevant constructs include:
- Social Communication;
- Perception and Understanding of Self;
- Perception and Understanding of Others.

Within **Perception and Understanding of Others**, RDoC distinguishes:
- Animacy Perception;
- Action Perception;
- Understanding Mental States.

This is a useful functional scaffold, not a complete taxonomy of social psychology.

## 2. Understanding mental states

RDoC defines **Understanding Mental States** as making judgments/attributions about another animate entity's mental state in order to predict or interpret behavior.

Mental-state targets include:
- intentions;
- beliefs;
- desires;
- emotions.

This is broader than emotion recognition and narrower than all interpersonal understanding.

## 3. Social cognition is not one ability

"Social cognition" can refer to:
- emotion perception;
- action understanding;
- mental-state inference;
- trait inference;
- social attribution;
- perspective taking;
- empathy;
- moral/social reasoning;
- social decision making;
- metaperception.

A score in one subdomain does not establish global social-cognitive competence.

## 4. Observation versus inference

Observable:
- words;
- actions;
- timing;
- gaze;
- facial/vocal behavior;
- physical context;
- role;
- consequences.

Inferred:
- intention;
- belief;
- desire;
- emotion;
- trait;
- motive;
- relationship meaning.

The observer never measures the inferred state directly merely by seeing behavior.

**Observation and social explanation are distinct levels.**

## 5. Behavior is multiply determined

The same behavior can arise from:
- stable dispositions;
- immediate goals;
- social roles;
- incentives;
- threat;
- norms;
- misunderstanding;
- fatigue;
- skill constraints;
- habit;
- deliberate impression management;
- situational coercion.

Therefore:
**behavior -> trait**
is not a valid one-step inference.

## 6. Traits versus states versus roles

A trait claim concerns relatively stable cross-situational individual differences.

A state claim concerns current condition.

A role claim concerns behavior shaped by social/institutional position.

Examples:
- assertive behavior can reflect trait dominance, task authority, defensive state, or assigned role;
- quiet behavior can reflect introversion, uncertainty, norm compliance, fatigue, or low opportunity.

Repeated cross-context evidence is required for stronger trait inference.

## 7. Person perception is multimodal

First impressions can use:
- face;
- voice;
- name;
- body;
- clothing;
- speech content;
- interaction behavior;
- context.

A 2026 study sampled ~45,000 spontaneous descriptors and found recurring dimensions resembling approachability and competence across multimodal, voice, and name conditions, with additional dimensions in multimodal judgments.

This demonstrates structured first impressions.

It does **not** prove that those impressions are accurate traits or universal across all cultures/populations.

## 8. Appearance-based impressions are judgments, not measurements

Observers can rapidly agree that a face looks:
- trustworthy;
- dominant;
- competent;
- attractive;
- threatening.

Agreement demonstrates shared perception/conventions.

It does not establish that the target actually possesses the inferred trait.

**Consensus ≠ accuracy.**

## 9. Facial trustworthiness accuracy is modest

A meta-analysis of facial trustworthiness impressions found statistically above-chance but modest accuracy:
- face-level correlation about r=.14;
- perceiver-level correlation about r=.27.

Accuracy varied by target domain and the evidence base was heavily Western.

Thus:
**face-based trustworthiness impressions contain limited information at best and should not be treated as reliable individual diagnosis or moral assessment.**

## 10. Physiognomic overreach

Inferring:
- criminality;
- honesty;
- intelligence;
- moral worth;
- psychiatric status

from static facial appearance is scientifically unsafe unless a specific criterion and validated predictive evidence exist.

Even modest group-level associations do not justify high-confidence individual inference.

Base rates, cue validity, selection, and confounding must be examined.

## 11. Context changes person perception

A 2026 study manipulating background context around life-sized targets found strong effects of positive versus negative contexts on several trait impressions.

This shows that impressions attributed to the "person" can partly arise from:
- surrounding scene;
- nearby people;
- interpersonal distance;
- affective context.

Person perception therefore includes context integration, not only target decoding.

## 12. Face context from AFF remains relevant

AFF established that emotional judgments about faces depend strongly on visual context.

SOC extends the same logic to trait/social inference:

**target cue + situation + prior belief -> social judgment**.

Do not interpret a target cue independently when context is available and causally relevant.

## 13. Trait inference from behavior can be spontaneous

The systematic meta-analysis of spontaneous trait inference included:
- 86 publications;
- N=13,630.

Average effect was moderate-to-large (\`dz≈.59\`) with substantial heterogeneity and paradigm moderation.

This supports the claim that observers can spontaneously encode behavior in trait terms.

It does not establish:
- inferred trait is correct;
- trait caused the behavior;
- spontaneous inference is immutable;
- every behavior produces trait inference.

## 14. Spontaneous trait inference versus explicit attribution

Spontaneous trait inference:
- may occur without deliberate causal explanation.

Explicit attribution:
- asks why an action occurred.

These processes overlap but are not identical.

A person may automatically activate a trait concept and later correct for situational constraints.

Do not treat implicit memory effects as full conscious explanations.

## 15. Attribution concerns explanations

Attribution research asks how people explain:
- intentional actions;
- outcomes;
- success/failure;
- interpersonal events.

Possible explanation categories include:
- reasons/goals;
- beliefs;
- dispositions;
- situational causes;
- enabling conditions;
- causal history.

A simple "person versus situation" dichotomy can be too coarse for intentional behavior.

## 16. Reasons differ from causes

For intentional actions, explanations can invoke:
- the person's reasons;
- beliefs about outcomes;
- desires/goals.

These are different from:
- personality trait explanations;
- external situational causes;
- accidental/mechanical causes.

Attribution frameworks that collapse all person-related explanations into "disposition" can lose important structure.

## 17. Fundamental attribution error: use cautiously

The "fundamental attribution error" traditionally refers to overemphasizing dispositional explanations while underweighting situational constraints.

The label is historically influential but contested.

Correspondence bias—inferring a corresponding attitude/trait despite situational constraint—has stronger specific experimental support than a universal claim that people always ignore situations.

Use:
**context-sensitive correspondence/dispositional inference**
rather than assuming one universal error.

## 18. Actor–observer asymmetry is not a general law

The classic hypothesis:
- actors explain own behavior situationally;
- observers explain others dispositionally.

A meta-analysis of **173 studies** found average effects from approximately \`d=-.016\` to \`.095\`, essentially near zero overall.

Effects appeared under specific moderators and valence conditions.

Canonical rule:

**do not teach actor–observer asymmetry as a pervasive universal bias.**

## 19. Self-serving attribution is different

Self-serving attribution refers to explanations protecting or enhancing self-evaluation, such as asymmetries for success/failure.

It is not equivalent to actor–observer bias.

Valence, motivation, relationship, and accountability can influence attribution.

Different biases must not be merged into one "egocentric bias."

## 20. Situational information can be underused or integrated

Social perceivers sometimes:
- underweight constraints;
- adjust for them;
- use them strongly;
- infer traits from how someone behaves within constraints.

The relevant question is not:
"Do people ignore situations?"

It is:
**Which situational information is represented, believed, weighted, and integrated in this task?**

## 21. Social roles are causal information

Behavior under:
- occupational roles;
- institutional authority;
- assigned tasks;
- group norms;
- power asymmetries

can reflect role requirements.

A 2026 theoretical account reframed part of attribution bias as neglect of institutional stance/role structure.

This is a theoretical proposal rather than a definitive replacement for attribution theory.

Nevertheless, role constraints should be explicitly checked before trait inference.

## 22. Intentionality inference

Inferring intentional action requires separating:
- action outcome;
- desire for outcome;
- belief that action would produce outcome;
- awareness;
- ability/control.

Harmful outcome ≠ harmful intention by definition.

Successful outcome ≠ prior intent by definition.

Intentionality judgments can be influenced by moral evaluation and outcome valence.

## 23. Goal inference versus trait inference

Behavior may be better explained by a current goal than a stable trait.

Example structure:
- behavior: interrupts;
- possible goal: prevent error;
- possible state: urgency;
- possible trait: dominance.

Without repeated cross-context evidence, the goal/state explanation often remains viable.

## 24. Mentalizing / Theory of Mind

Theory of Mind (ToM) commonly refers to reasoning about:
- beliefs;
- intentions;
- desires;
- knowledge;
- emotions.

"Mentalizing" is often used more broadly for holding in mind, appraising, and reappraising mental states of self and/or others.

Terminology varies across literatures.

Therefore:
**ToM score ≠ all mentalizing**.

## 25. Cognitive and affective mental-state inference

Some tasks emphasize:
- beliefs/knowledge/intention;
others emphasize:
- emotional states.

This distinction resembles but is not identical to:
- cognitive empathy;
- affective empathy.

A task should be classified by what participants must infer, not by its label.

## 26. Mentalizing is not empathy

Mentalizing can involve understanding another person's state without sharing it emotionally.

Empathy can include:
- cognitive understanding;
- affective resonance;
- concern/other-oriented response.

A person can accurately infer another's intent without feeling concern.

A person can feel emotional contagion without accurately representing the other's belief.

SOC-02 will treat empathy in depth.

## 27. Perspective taking is not mind reading

Perspective taking is an attempt/ability to represent another perspective.

Accuracy depends on:
- available information;
- target expressiveness;
- familiarity;
- stereotypes/prior beliefs;
- attention;
- language;
- context.

Taking a perspective can produce a plausible but wrong inference.

Therefore:
**perspective-taking effort ≠ interpersonal accuracy**.

## 28. Adult ToM measurement is highly heterogeneous

The 2023 systematic review identified:
- 273 studies;
- 75 adult ToM measures.

Findings included:
- frequent ceiling effects;
- limited reporting of reliability/validity;
- inconsistent interrelations between measures.

This is strong evidence against treating adult ToM as a single well-measured trait.

## 29. 2024 ToM reliability meta-analysis

A reliability-generalization meta-analysis examined:
- 90 studies;
- 27 ToM tasks;
- 2,771 schizophrenia participants;
- 690 autistic participants;
- 15,599 nonclinical participants.

In nonclinical populations, roughly half of tasks lacked satisfactory internal consistency.

The commonly used RMET and Hinting Task had notable reliability limitations, while only a small subset of tasks showed satisfactory evidence across populations.

**Task popularity ≠ psychometric adequacy.**

## 30. RMET is not a global mentalizing test

The Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test asks participants to infer mental-state terms from eye-region photographs.

Performance can depend on:
- emotion/mental-state vocabulary;
- visual processing;
- cultural familiarity;
- test-taking strategy;
- language.

Even if reliable in a given population, it cannot establish global everyday Theory of Mind ability without convergent/ecological evidence.

## 31. Hinting and narrative tasks include language demands

Narrative ToM tasks can require:
- vocabulary;
- reading comprehension;
- working memory;
- inference;
- narrative integration.

A 2024 adult ToM study found generally weak associations among several ToM tasks and a protagonist-perspective factor related more strongly to vocabulary than executive function.

Thus task performance can mix social reasoning and general cognitive demands.

## 32. Mentalizing measurement construct validity is divergent

A 2025 systematic review of mentalizing measures found substantial differences in:
- dimensions operationalized;
- self versus other relational context;
- hypothetical versus relational targets;
- construct validity.

The review argues that measure selection should follow the specific mentalizing component of interest.

Canonical rule:

**choose measure from construct definition, not construct name.**

## 33. Clinical social-cognition tasks are population-specific

A 2026 psychometric meta-analysis synthesized 55 validation studies:
- 3,845 patients;
- 3,807 controls.

Some tasks showed useful known-group validity for specific conditions, but heterogeneity/inconsistency was considerable elsewhere.

A task validated to discriminate one clinical population is not automatically:
- valid for healthy individual differences;
- diagnostic for an individual;
- transferable to another condition.

## 34. Ceiling effects matter

A task designed to detect severe impairment can show little variance in healthy adults.

High average accuracy can create:
- restricted range;
- unreliable rank ordering;
- false null correlations.

Absence of association may therefore reflect measurement ceiling rather than absence of the construct.

## 35. Internal consistency is not the only reliability

For social cognition consider:
- internal consistency;
- test–retest reliability;
- interrater reliability;
- alternate-form reliability;
- measurement invariance.

A heterogeneous task can legitimately have lower item homogeneity while still measuring meaningful performance.

Interpret reliability in relation to task design.

## 36. Ecological validity is not guaranteed by realistic stimuli

Videos, films, conversations, and virtual interactions can improve realism.

But realism alone does not establish construct validity.

A 2024 systematic review of dynamic/multimodal social-cognition tools found 65 tools across 149 works and highlighted limited standardization/validation despite ecological ambitions.

Naturalistic tasks require psychometric validation too.

## 37. Dynamic mentalizing "ground truth" is difficult

A 2024 naturalistic ToM study used continuous judgments during a mockumentary and compared individuals to a baseline sample's consensus ratings.

This is an innovative convergence method.

However:
**group-consensus judgment ≠ direct access to the character's actual mental state**.

The criterion is an operational reference, not literal mind reading.

## 38. Interpersonal accuracy requires a criterion

To claim accuracy, specify the target criterion:
- target's self-report;
- target's later report;
- behavioral outcome;
- known experimental ground truth;
- consensual judgment;
- observer agreement.

Each criterion has limitations.

No social judgment can be called "accurate" without a defined criterion.

## 39. Thought–feeling accuracy in live interaction

A 2025 Annual Review defines thought–feeling accuracy as accuracy about the content of another person's thoughts/feelings during live interaction.

Accuracy can depend on:
- target expressivity;
- cue availability;
- relationship knowledge;
- perceiver attention;
- motivational goals;
- stereotypic assumptions.

This is different from static-face emotion recognition.

## 40. Meta-accuracy

Meta-accuracy asks:
**How accurately does a person know how others perceive them?**

A 2026 systematic review identified 93 studies and found substantial diversity in:
- dyadic versus generalized meta-accuracy;
- relationship context;
- trait domain;
- measurement.

Most studies were cross-sectional and student-based.

Do not treat "knowing how others see you" as one unitary ability.

## 41. Metaperception bias can shape interaction

A person can respond not to what another actually thinks, but to what they believe the other thinks.

Thus social interaction may involve:
- first-order inference: "what do they think/feel?";
- meta-perception: "what do they think about me?";
- higher-order inference: "what do they think I think?"

Errors at these levels can become behaviorally consequential even if the original inference was false.

## 42. Observer agreement is not criterion validity

If many observers rate a target as dominant, that shows consensus.

Accuracy requires an independent criterion.

Shared stereotypes or visual conventions can create high consensus with low truth validity.

Always separate:
- consensus;
- reliability;
- accuracy.

## 43. Self-report social cognition is not behavioral performance

Self-reported:
- empathy;
- perspective taking;
- mentalizing confidence

can reflect:
- self-concept;
- social desirability;
- metacognitive belief.

Large-sample evidence has found weak relationships between self-reported empathy and behavioral social-cognitive tasks.

Thus:
**"I am good at reading people" ≠ demonstrated interpersonal accuracy.**

## 44. Confidence is not accuracy

Observers can be:
- accurate and confident;
- accurate and uncertain;
- inaccurate and confident;
- inaccurate and uncertain.

Social confidence may reflect:
- experience;
- personality;
- social status;
- feedback history.

Calibration must be measured explicitly.

## 45. Cultural context changes cue meaning

Social cues can vary across cultures in:
- eye contact norms;
- emotional display;
- politeness;
- directness;
- role expectations;
- gesture;
- social hierarchy.

A task created in one culture may change meaning after translation even if words are linguistically accurate.

## 46. Cross-cultural assessment remains limited

A 2025 systematic review of social-cognition tests in non-Western/minoritized groups identified:
- 85 eligible articles;
- 23 newly developed tests;
- 51 standardized/validated tests.

Psychometric quality varied, and the authors called for stronger consensus frameworks and culturally appropriate validation.

**Western norm ≠ universal norm.**

## 47. Translation is not full cultural validation

Translation/back-translation addresses language equivalence.

It does not automatically establish:
- construct equivalence;
- cue familiarity;
- scalar invariance;
- criterion validity;
- normative comparability.

Cultural adaptation needs empirical validation.

## 48. Social categories and stereotypes

Perceivers use social-category information to form expectations.

This can increase predictive efficiency when category information genuinely carries base-rate information, but can also:
- overgeneralize;
- ignore individual evidence;
- reproduce social bias;
- contaminate "accuracy" criteria.

Stereotype and prejudice mechanisms require a dedicated later SOC block.

For SOC-01, the rule is:
**category prior ≠ individual fact**.

## 49. Base rates and Bayesian social inference

A useful formal framing:

\`P(mental state/trait | cues, context) ∝ P(cues | state/trait, context) × prior\`

This expresses that inference depends on:
- cue diagnosticity;
- context;
- prior expectations.

It is a computational analogy, not proof that the brain explicitly performs exact Bayes calculations.

A strong prior can dominate weak evidence; an invalid prior can systematically bias judgment.

## 50. Cue validity and cue utilization

Accuracy requires both:
1. **valid cue availability** — the cue genuinely covaries with the target property;
2. **cue utilization** — perceiver appropriately uses it.

Failure can occur because:
- target does not express the state;
- perceiver misses the cue;
- cue is ambiguous;
- perceiver applies wrong prior.

Do not attribute all inaccuracy to perceiver incompetence.

## 51. Social inference can be strategically distorted

People may intentionally:
- conceal feelings;
- exaggerate;
- lie;
- conform;
- perform roles;
- manage impressions.

Observed behavior can then be evidence of strategic communication rather than direct internal state.

Inference models must allow deception/impression management without assuming it by default.

## 52. Interaction changes the target

Unlike static object perception, social inference often occurs in reciprocal interaction.

The observer's:
- gaze;
- questions;
- status;
- warmth;
- suspicion

can change the target's behavior.

Thus person perception can be dynamically self-influencing.

Observer and target are not always independent.

## 53. Self-fulfilling processes

An initial expectation can alter:
- observer behavior;
- target response;
- later evidence.

This can make a false initial belief appear confirmed.

Longitudinal/interactive studies are needed to distinguish:
- accurate prediction;
- behavioral elicitation;
- selective attention.

## 54. Thin slices

Brief observations can sometimes support above-chance social judgments when valid cues are available.

But accuracy varies by:
- trait;
- context;
- channel;
- target expressivity;
- criterion.

"Humans can infer some things from thin slices" does not justify unrestricted intuition or personality reading.

## 55. Static versus interactive social cognition

Static tasks:
- controlled;
- repeatable;
- easier to score.

Interactive tasks:
- reciprocal;
- context-rich;
- potentially more ecologically relevant.

But interaction adds:
- strategic behavior;
- feedback loops;
- uncontrolled variance.

No format is universally superior.

## 56. Neural boundary

Mentalizing tasks often recruit networks including medial prefrontal, temporoparietal, precuneus, and temporal regions.

But these regions also contribute to other cognitive operations.

Activation does not uniquely identify:
- specific belief;
- intention;
- empathy;
- deception.

Detailed neural interpretation belongs to NEU.

## 57. Machine inference boundary

Algorithms can infer social labels from:
- face;
- voice;
- language;
- interaction traces;
- digital behavior.

Performance may exploit:
- dataset artifacts;
- demographic correlations;
- context;
- label conventions.

High held-out accuracy does not prove:
- latent mental state decoded;
- causality;
- fairness;
- individual-level certainty.

External, cross-population, and construct validation are required.

## 58. Social Cognition Inference Gate — 60 questions

### A. Observation and target
1. What exactly was observed?
2. What is inferred: emotion, belief, desire, intention, goal, trait, or ability?
3. Is the inference about a momentary state or stable trait?
4. Is behavior voluntary, constrained, habitual, or role-governed?
5. Is there enough behavior across contexts for a trait claim?
6. Could skill/opportunity explain the behavior?

### B. Context and attribution
7. What situational constraints are present?
8. What social role is the person occupying?
9. Is the behavior incentivized or required?
10. Is a current goal a plausible explanation?
11. Is a trait explanation being favored without comparison?
12. Is correspondence bias being assumed rather than demonstrated?
13. Is actor–observer asymmetry being treated as universal?
14. Does event valence influence attribution?
15. Are reasons distinguished from dispositions/causes?
16. Could impression management alter the behavior?

### C. Mentalizing
17. What mental-state type is inferred?
18. Is ToM distinguished from empathy?
19. Is cognitive mentalizing separated from affect sharing?
20. Is perspective taking being equated with accuracy?
21. Does the task require language/vocabulary?
22. Does the task require executive/memory processing?
23. Could ceiling effects obscure individual differences?
24. Is mentalizing about self, known other, or hypothetical other?
25. Is task construct validity established?
26. Is performance being generalized to everyday interaction?

### D. Accuracy criterion
27. What is the independent criterion for accuracy?
28. Is target self-report treated as imperfect but relevant criterion?
29. Is observer consensus being mistaken for truth?
30. Is group consensus being used as operational "ground truth"?
31. Could criterion itself be biased/noisy?
32. Are confidence and accuracy separated?
33. Is cue validity independently established?
34. Does the target actually express diagnostic cues?

### E. Person perception
35. Are face, voice, body, language, and context integrated?
36. Is static appearance being used for moral/personality inference?
37. Is modest group-level accuracy being converted into individual certainty?
38. Could background context shift the impression?
39. Are first-impression dimensions being treated as actual traits?
40. Is a category/stereotype prior being substituted for individual evidence?

### F. Culture and measurement
41. Was the measure validated in the relevant language/culture?
42. Is translation being confused with measurement invariance?
43. Are cultural display/interaction norms relevant?
44. Is the task reliable in the target population?
45. Is test–retest evidence available?
46. Does the measure have convergent/discriminant validity?
47. Is ecological validity empirically tested?
48. Is self-report being substituted for behavioral ability?
49. Are static and dynamic tasks measuring the same construct?
50. Is clinical known-group validity being generalized to healthy individual differences?

### G. Interaction and causality
51. Could the observer change the target's behavior?
52. Could expectancy create a self-fulfilling pattern?
53. Is temporal order known?
54. Is a social association being interpreted causally?
55. Are reciprocal effects possible?
56. Could group membership/context account for the inference?
57. Is machine/classifier output being treated as mind reading?
58. Is neural activity being used as unique mental-state evidence?
59. Which viable competing explanation remains?
60. What is the narrowest inference justified by cues, context, criterion, and measurement?

## 59. Failure modes

A. Behavior -> trait: **invalid without cross-context evidence**.

B. Behavior -> intention: **invalid without alternative goal/constraint analysis**.

C. Face -> trustworthiness/morality: **weak/nonunique individual inference**.

D. Observer agreement -> truth: **consensus/accuracy conflation**.

E. Spontaneous trait inference -> trait causality: **invalid**.

F. Fundamental attribution error -> universal law: **overgeneralized**.

G. Actor–observer bias -> pervasive: **contradicted by meta-analytic average**.

H. ToM -> one unitary ability: **unsupported measurement simplification**.

I. RMET -> global mentalizing: **invalid task-generalization**.

J. Perspective taking -> accuracy: **invalid**.

K. Mentalizing -> empathy: **construct conflation**.

L. Self-reported empathy -> social-cognitive performance: **poor proxy**.

M. Static test -> everyday social competence: **ecological transport assumption**.

N. Translation -> cultural validity: **invalid**.

O. Stereotype/category -> individual fact: **invalid**.

P. Consensus "ground truth" -> direct mind access: **invalid criterion reification**.

Q. Algorithm accuracy -> mental state decoded: **construct leap**.

R. Neural activation -> specific thought/intention: **reverse inference**.

## 60. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- social cognition is inference under uncertainty;
- behavior is multiply determined;
- trait and mental-state inference require context;
- observer consensus is not accuracy;
- actor–observer asymmetry is not a robust universal effect;
- ToM/mentalizing measurement is heterogeneous;
- many adult ToM tasks have ceiling/reliability/validity limitations;
- social-cognition measures require population/cultural validation;
- self-report social confidence/empathy is not behavioral accuracy.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- spontaneous trait inferences occur reliably on average;
- some first impressions contain modest predictive information;
- thought–feeling accuracy can exceed chance in live interaction;
- dynamic/multimodal tasks may improve ecological relevance;
- social roles and situations alter attribution;
- multimodal cues create structured first impressions.

### Not established as universal
- one social-cognition ability;
- one ToM test as gold standard;
- facial appearance as trustworthy individual trait detector;
- a universal fundamental attribution error;
- one observer-independent ground truth for mental states;
- a culture-free norm for social inference;
- algorithmic or neural mind reading.

## Canonical operating rule

**Observe behavior/cues -> identify situation, role, incentives, and interaction context -> specify whether inference concerns state, intention, goal, or trait -> generate competing person/situation explanations -> for mentalizing, define the exact mental-state target -> require a valid accuracy criterion -> check task reliability, cognitive demands, culture, and ecological transport -> preserve uncertainty -> state only the narrowest supported social inference.**

Never:

## Scope and next dependency

SOC-01 establishes the inference and measurement foundations needed before deeper social-affective constructs.

**Next block: SOC-02 — Empathy and interpersonal accuracy: cognitive/affective empathy, emotional contagion, compassion/empathic concern, perspective taking, self–other distinction, empathic accuracy, context, and measurement validity.**

SOC-02 should prevent collapsing mentalizing, empathy, sympathy/compassion, contagion, and prosocial motivation into one "empathy" score. Stereotyping, social categorization, norms, group processes, and social influence should remain separate later SOC dependencies.
