# BIO-03 — Glycolysis

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-19**  
Область: 05 — Bioenergetics / Exercise Metabolism

## 0. Core decision

Glycolysis is a cytosolic ATP-producing pathway that:

- converts glucose/G6P to pyruvate;
- produces ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation;
- reduces NAD+ to NADH;
- does **not** directly consume molecular O2.

Project rule:

**glycolysis can be highly active in oxygenated muscle; lactate formation does not prove anoxia.**

---

## 1. Glucose and glycogen are not identical entry substrates

Both routes converge at glucose-6-phosphate.

### Free glucose
Requires hexokinase:

`glucose + ATP → G6P + ADP`.

### Muscle glycogen
Most α-1,4-linked glucosyl residues are released as G1P and converted to G6P without paying the hexokinase ATP cost.

Project rule:

**substrate identity changes glycolytic entry cost.**

---

## 2. Glycolytic ATP yield

For one free glucose molecule:

- 2 ATP invested;
- 4 ATP produced;
- net = **2 ATP**.

For most α-1,4 glycogen-derived glucosyl units entering as G1P→G6P:

- hexokinase ATP cost is bypassed;
- net = **3 ATP**.

Branch-point α-1,6 residues can be released as free glucose.

Therefore:

**3 ATP is the dominant-path yield for most glycogen-derived units, not an exact whole-polymer average for every residue.**

---

## 3. ATP yield is not ATP-production rate

### ATP yield
Stoichiometric ATP per substrate unit.

### ATP-production rate
ATP generated per unit time.

### Total carbohydrate ATP yield
Includes downstream mitochondrial oxidation and is not the same as glycolytic ATP yield.

Project rule:

**yield ≠ rate ≠ total oxidative ATP yield.**

---

## 4. Core glycolysis pathway

Core glycolysis is:

`glucose/G6P → pyruvate`

with:

- ATP investment;
- substrate-level ATP formation;
- NAD+→NADH.

LDH-mediated lactate formation is tightly coupled to glycolytic redox balance but is conceptually downstream of the canonical core glycolysis steps.

---

## 5. “Anaerobic glycolysis” terminology

“**Anaerobic glycolysis**” remains common shorthand.

It should not be interpreted literally as:

> glycolysis occurring only when oxygen is absent.

Preferred project wording:

- glycolytic ATP production;
- rapid glycolytic flux;
- lactate-forming glycolytic metabolism when relevant.

Project rule:

**anaerobic glycolysis ≠ evidence of anoxia.**

---

## 6. Pyruvate is a branch-point metabolite

Relevant pyruvate fates include:

- LDH-mediated conversion to lactate;
- mitochondrial import and PDH-mediated acetyl-CoA formation;
- alanine formation;
- other context-dependent pathways.

Project rule:

**pyruvate is not uniquely “aerobic,” and lactate is not uniquely “anaerobic.”**

---

## 7. Lactate formation and NAD+ regeneration

LDH reaction:

`pyruvate + NADH + H+ ↔ lactate + NAD+`.

This can regenerate cytosolic NAD+ and support continued glycolytic flux.

Project rule:

**lactate formation is a redox-linked pyruvate fate, not proof of oxygen deficiency.**

Detailed lactate transport/oxidation/shuttling remains BIO-04.

---

## 8. Other cytosolic redox routes

Cytosolic reducing equivalents can also be handled through mitochondrial redox shuttles, including:

- malate-aspartate shuttle;
- glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle.

Project rule:

**LDH is an important NAD+-regeneration route, not the only one.**

---

## 9. High glycolytic ATP-production rate

During high-intensity exercise, glycolytic ATP flux can rise rapidly and can exceed maximal oxidative ATP-synthesis rate in specific protocols.

Exact flux values are task/method dependent.

Project rule:

**glycolysis is a high-rate ATP source, but no universal resistance-exercise rate is retained.**

---

## 10. Glycolytic capacity is finite

Glycolytic ATP supply is not unlimited.

Relevant constraints include:

- substrate availability;
- pathway regulation;
- NAD+ regeneration/redox state;
- pH/metabolite state;
- changing ATP demand;
- oxidative interaction.

Project rule:

**high glycolytic rate ≠ unlimited glycolytic capacity.**

---

## 11. Energy systems operate concurrently

Reject the literal sequence:

`phosphagen → glycolytic → aerobic`.

During intense exercise:

- PCr contribution changes rapidly;
- glycolytic ATP production can be large;
- oxidative ATP synthesis is already active.

Project rule:

**relative contributions change continuously; systems do not wait for one another to switch on.**

---

## 12. Maximal effort does not mean constant glycolytic flux

Direct human sprint/high-intensity studies show glycolytic ATP-production rate can decline during ongoing maximal work.

Project rule:

**maximal external effort ≠ constant maximal internal glycolytic flux.**

---

## 13. Muscle glycogen depletion

High-intensity and resistance exercise can substantially reduce muscle glycogen.

Magnitude depends on:

- set/bout number;
- duration;
- intensity;
- recruitment;
- starting glycogen;
- training status;
- protocol.

Project rule:

**there is no universal glycogen-depletion percentage per set or session.**

---

## 14. Glycogen depletion is not total exhaustion

Muscle glycogen is heterogeneous across:

- fibers;
- recruited regions;
- subcellular pools.

A reduced biopsy average does not mean all glycogen stores are empty.

Project rule:

**glycogen depletion ≠ complete glycogen exhaustion.**

---

## 15. Low glycogen and performance

Low glycogen can impair some high-intensity exercise tasks.

But there is no single universal glycogen concentration that defines fatigue for all tasks.

Project rule:

**low glycogen is a task-dependent performance constraint, not a universal fatigue switch.**

---

## 16. Glycogen repletion

Glycogen can be resynthesized after exercise.

Repletion depends on:

- time;
- substrate availability;
- insulin/contraction-related glucose handling;
- prior depletion;
- nutrition context.

BIO-03 retains the mechanism only.

Exact carbohydrate dose/timing belongs BIO-10/NUT.

---

## 17. Glycolytic enzyme adaptations

Training can modify glycolytic enzyme content/activity.

But responses differ by:

- enzyme;
- protocol;
- population;
- duration.

Examples include changes in:

- glycogen phosphorylase;
- hexokinase;
- PFK;
- LDH.

Project rule:

**glycolytic enzymes are trainable, but no universal all-enzyme increase is assumed.**

---

## 18. Enzyme activity is not pathway flux or performance

Measured enzyme content/activity is a biochemical capacity marker.

It is not identical to:

- in-vivo glycolytic ATP flux;
- ATP turnover;
- glycogen depletion;
- lactate appearance;
- performance.

Project rule:

**enzyme adaptation ≠ in-vivo flux ≠ guaranteed performance improvement.**

---

## 19. Canonical non-equivalences

1. Blood glucose ≠ muscle glycogen.
2. Free-glucose entry cost ≠ glycogen-derived G6P entry cost.
3. 3 ATP glycogen yield ≠ exact whole-polymer average for every residue.
4. ATP yield ≠ ATP-production rate.
5. Glycolytic ATP yield ≠ total carbohydrate oxidation ATP yield.
6. Glycolysis ≠ process requiring absence of O2.
7. “Anaerobic glycolysis” ≠ proof of anoxia.
8. Pyruvate ≠ uniquely aerobic product.
9. Lactate ≠ uniquely anaerobic product.
10. Lactate formation ≠ proof of hypoxia.
11. LDH reaction ≠ canonical core glycolysis step.
12. LDH route ≠ only NAD+-regeneration route.
13. High glycolytic rate ≠ unlimited capacity.
14. Major glycolytic contribution ≠ glycolysis-only exercise.
15. Maximal effort ≠ constant maximal glycolytic flux.
16. Glycogen depletion ≠ glycogen exhaustion.
17. Low glycogen ≠ universal sole fatigue cause.
18. Glycogen repletion mechanism ≠ nutrition prescription.
19. Enzyme activity/content ≠ in-vivo flux.
20. Enzyme adaptation ≠ guaranteed performance gain.
21. Blood lactate ≠ direct quantitative measure of muscle glycolytic ATP production.
