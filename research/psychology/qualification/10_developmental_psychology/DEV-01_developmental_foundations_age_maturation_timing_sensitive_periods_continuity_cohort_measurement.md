# DEV-01 — Developmental foundations: age, maturation, developmental timing, sensitive periods, continuity/change, plasticity, cohort effects, and measurement

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public developmental-science evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

PER established that personality stability and change must be distinguished and that age-related population trends cannot be assigned directly to an individual.

DEV begins the broader developmental problem:

**What does it mean to say that a psychological process "develops with age," how should chronological age be separated from maturation and historical cohort, when is a developmental window legitimately called sensitive or critical, how can continuity coexist with changing behavioral forms, and what measurement/design assumptions are required for valid developmental inference?**

High-risk errors to prevent:
- "older age caused the change";
- "age = maturation";
- "puberty = chronological age";
- "pubertal status = pubertal timing";
- "pubertal timing = pubertal tempo";
- "adolescence is one biologically fixed stage";
- "age difference = developmental change";
- "cross-sectional age difference = within-person development";
- "longitudinal change = pure age effect";
- "cohort effect = age effect";
- "period effect = cohort effect";
- "age, period, and cohort can be estimated freely from the same data";
- "early exposure is always more important";
- "critical period = sensitive period";
- "sensitive period = fixed age window";
- "higher plasticity = better outcome";
- "plasticity = unlimited reversibility";
- "same behavior across ages = same process";
- "different behavior across ages = different process";
- "same scale score means the same construct at every age";
- "measurement invariance proves construct identity";
- "repeated testing is neutral";
- "attrition is random unless proven otherwise";
- "developmental cascade = causal chain";
- "population trajectory = individual developmental law".

## Core rule

**Development is change in psychological or behavioral organization over time, not chronological age itself. Age is an index that can correlate with multiple developmental processes, including biological maturation, learning, role transitions, accumulated experience, historical period, and cohort. Valid developmental inference requires defining the process, timescale, developmental clock, measurement equivalence, and competing age/cohort/context explanations.**

Canonical reasoning:

\`define developmental construct -> choose developmental clock/timescale -> distinguish age from maturation/exposure -> establish repeated measurement and construct comparability -> model within-person change separately from between-person age differences -> test timing/period hypotheses with appropriate exposure resolution -> separate age, cohort, and period assumptions -> inspect continuity/discontinuity of behavior, function, and mechanism -> infer only the narrowest developmental claim\`.

## 1. Chronological age is an index, not a mechanism

Chronological age can covary with:
- neural maturation;
- endocrine maturation;
- schooling;
- accumulated practice;
- family roles;
- peer ecology;
- legal/institutional transitions;
- historical exposure.

Therefore:

**age predicts X**
does not mean
**age causes X**.

A developmental explanation needs a candidate process.

## 2. Age can still be useful

Chronological age is:
- objective;
- easy to measure;
- often strongly associated with developmental change;
- useful for prediction and description.

It becomes problematic when treated as:
- mechanism;
- biological maturity;
- universal stage membership.

Use age as a time coordinate unless stronger developmental meaning is established.

## 3. Developmental clocks

Different processes can be indexed by different clocks:

- chronological age;
- time since birth;
- gestational age;
- pubertal stage/status;
- pubertal timing;
- pubertal tempo;
- time since event;
- time in role/schooling;
- biological aging indices;
- accumulated exposure.

The best clock depends on the process.

## 4. Timescale is theoretical

The 2026 Annual Review on developmental timescales emphasizes that development can unfold across:
- seconds;
- days;
- months;
- years;
- decades.

Sampling density and observation duration determine what change can be detected.

A process operating over days can disappear in annual measurements.
A decade-scale trajectory cannot be inferred from a short intensive burst.

## 5. Clock time versus process time

Two people of the same chronological age can differ in:
- pubertal maturation;
- schooling exposure;
- duration since adversity;
- experience with a skill.

For some developmental questions, process time is more informative than age.

Age adjustment is not a substitute for measuring the process.

## 6. Development is not always monotonic

Developmental trajectories can be:
- increasing;
- decreasing;
- plateauing;
- U-shaped;
- inverted-U;
- sigmoid;
- discontinuous/step-like;
- multi-phase.

A linear age term should not be the default developmental theory.

## 7. 2025 meta-meta-analysis warns against assumed age growth

A 2025 meta-meta-analysis examined age-related trajectories across 25 early linguistic and cognitive phenomena.

Many phenomena did not show the simple age-related increases that are often assumed.

This demonstrates:

**including age linearly in a regression is not evidence that development is linear.**

## 8. Maturation

Maturation refers to developmental changes associated with biological growth and organization.

Potential processes include:
- endocrine changes;
- neural development;
- physical growth;
- reproductive maturation.

Maturation can correlate strongly with age but is not reducible to age.

## 9. Puberty is a multi-process developmental system

Current expert consensus distinguishes overlapping biological processes including:
- adrenarche;
- gonadarche.

Puberty should not be represented as one binary event.

Relevant dimensions include:
- status/stage;
- timing;
- tempo;
- hormones.

## 10. Pubertal status

Pubertal status describes:
**how far a person has progressed through puberty at a given assessment**.

Examples include:
- Tanner-type stage measures;
- physical-development scales;
- hormonal indicators.

Status is not timing.

## 11. Pubertal timing

Pubertal timing asks:
**whether an individual reaches a pubertal stage earlier or later relative to a reference group or developmental trajectory**.

Timing is inherently comparative.

It depends on:
- population;
- sex-related developmental pattern;
- reference model;
- age distribution.

## 12. Pubertal tempo

Pubertal tempo describes:
**the rate of progression through puberty**.

A person can:
- start relatively early but progress slowly;
- start later but progress quickly.

Timing and tempo are distinct.

## 13. Pubertal status ≠ timing ≠ tempo

A 2026 editorial explicitly warns that puberty research increasingly blurs these constructs.

Canonical rule:

**state of maturation, relative timing, and rate of maturation must be modeled separately.**

## 14. 2026 longitudinal puberty consensus

Current expert consensus recommends:
- repeated longitudinal measurement;
- attention to timing and tempo;
- multimethod measurement;
- appropriate longitudinal modeling;
- explicit handling of sex/gender and contextual factors.

Single-wave puberty assessment is weak for tempo inference.

## 15. Pubertal measurement is method-dependent

Methods include:
- self-report physical development;
- clinician/observer staging;
- salivary hormones;
- anthropometry/growth;
- event markers.

Each captures partly different aspects.

No one measure is a universal puberty gold standard for all developmental questions.

## 16. 2025 multimodal puberty study

The Human Connectome Project in Development study:
- N=1,304;
- ages 5–21;
- self-report and hormone measures;
- cross-sectional and longitudinal data.

It showed:
- sigmoid pubertal trajectories;
- sex-related timing/tempo differences;
- hormone-age associations;
- individual variability.

This supports multimodal modeling rather than age-only inference.

## 17. Pubertal timing/tempo can explain variance beyond age

A 2026 ABCD longitudinal study found pubertal timing and tempo related differently to cortical and subcortical developmental trajectories after accounting for chronological age.

This is direct evidence that:
**age and maturation are not interchangeable predictors**.

It does not prove a simple puberty→psychological-outcome causal pathway.

## 18. Retrospective puberty measurement has limits

A 2025 study comparing contemporaneous and adult retrospective pubertal timing found moderate-to-high convergence overall.

But:
- early maturers were sometimes recalled as on-time;
- some associations were attenuated.

Retrospective timing can be informative but should not be treated as exact developmental chronology.

## 19. Developmental stage labels are summaries

Labels such as:
- infancy;
- childhood;
- adolescence;
- emerging adulthood;
- midlife;
- late adulthood

are useful descriptive partitions.

They do not imply:
- homogeneous biology;
- one universal transition age;
- identical mechanisms within every person.

Use precise age/process measures when mechanisms matter.

## 20. Sensitive period

A **sensitive period** is a developmental interval during which experience has a relatively stronger or more enduring effect on a specific system than the same experience would at other times.

It is:
- process-specific;
- exposure-specific;
- outcome-specific.

Sensitive does not mean deterministic.

## 21. Critical period

A **critical period** is a stronger claim:
a restricted developmental interval in which particular input is necessary or has uniquely powerful effects, with markedly reduced possibility outside that period.

Human psychological research should use "critical period" cautiously.

Evidence is strongest in some sensory systems.

## 22. Sensitive ≠ critical

Critical-period language often implies:
- narrower window;
- stronger necessity constraints;
- larger loss of plasticity afterward.

Sensitive-period language allows:
- graded susceptibility;
- continued learning outside the period.

Do not use the terms interchangeably.

## 23. Period of heightened sensitivity ≠ demonstrated sensitive period

The 2024 adolescence review emphasizes the distinction between:
- a developmental phase with heightened sensitivity/plasticity indicators;
- evidence that a particular exposure has larger causal effects during that phase.

The first does not establish the second.

## 24. Sensitive period must specify the system

A person can simultaneously be in different developmental states for:
- vision;
- language;
- reward learning;
- social cognition;
- emotion regulation.

There is no one global "brain sensitive period."

## 25. Sensitive period must specify the exposure

A developmental window may be sensitive to:
- language input;
- stress;
- social evaluation;
- sensory deprivation;
- training.

Sensitivity to one input does not imply sensitivity to all experience.

## 26. Sensitive period must specify the outcome

The same exposure timing can affect:
- one brain system;
- not another;
- one behavior;
- not another.

Therefore:
**earlier/later is not intrinsically better or worse**.

## 27. 2025/2026 hierarchical critical-period review

The current human-neurodevelopment review proposes that periods of heightened plasticity may unfold hierarchically across:
- sensorimotor;
- association cortical systems.

However, it emphasizes that precise human timing and consequences remain under active investigation.

Use as a hypothesis framework, not a universal calendar.

## 28. 2026 adversity timing systematic review

A systematic review of 43 neuroimaging studies found:
- earlier onset/longer adversity often related to limbic/paralimbic and global-brain measures;
- evidence for discrete sensitive periods was mixed;
- patterns varied by system, adversity, and method;
- retrospective exposure and cross-sectional designs were common limitations.

Canonical rule:

**do not infer a discrete sensitive window from heterogeneous timing associations alone.**

## 29. Sensitive periods are probabilistic

The 2026 review recommends conceptualizing sensitive periods as:
- probabilistic;
- system-specific;
- dependent on exposure timing/chronicity;
- linked to underlying plasticity rather than chronological age alone.

This is the project default.

## 30. Plasticity

Developmental plasticity is the capacity for experience-dependent change in:
- behavior;
- cognition;
- neural systems;
- physiological regulation.

Plasticity does not imply:
- direction of change;
- benefit;
- reversibility;
- unlimited magnitude.

## 31. Higher plasticity can increase opportunity and vulnerability

A highly plastic system can be more affected by:
- enrichment;
- adversity.

Therefore:

**plasticity ≠ resilience**
and
**plasticity ≠ positive development**.

Direction depends on experience and system.

## 32. Plasticity is not unlimited

Development is constrained by:
- prior organization;
- biology;
- accumulated learning;
- environment;
- resources;
- developmental stage.

Later change can remain possible even if plasticity declines.

Reduced plasticity ≠ zero plasticity.

## 33. Adversity can interact with plasticity

The 2026 review on adversity and neuroplasticity proposes that exposure may:
- interact with current plasticity;
- accelerate/delay timing;
- amplify/dampen plasticity magnitude.

These are candidate developmental mechanisms.

They require direct longitudinal evidence for specific systems.

## 34. Early exposure is not universally stronger

"Earlier is worse" is too broad.

Effect can depend on:
- system under development;
- exposure type;
- duration;
- chronicity;
- later environment.

A developmental timing hypothesis must state why that age is expected to matter.

## 35. Duration and timing are confounded easily

Earlier-onset exposures often have:
- longer duration;
- more recurrence;
- more cumulative dose.

An apparent "early sensitive period" may partly reflect greater cumulative exposure.

Timing, duration, and dose should be modeled separately.

## 36. Retrospective timing is vulnerable to reconstruction

Retrospective reports of:
- adversity age;
- symptom onset;
- developmental milestones

can be affected by recall.

Prospective timing data are preferable for strong sensitive-period claims.

Retrospective evidence remains useful with appropriate uncertainty.

## 37. Continuity

Developmental continuity concerns persistence or predictability across time.

Continuity can refer to:
- behavior form;
- function;
- construct;
- rank order;
- mechanism.

These are not equivalent.

## 38. Homotypic continuity

Homotypic continuity means roughly:
**the same or highly similar form of behavior/construct persists across development**.

Example structure:
same type of symptom/behavior predicts itself later.

This is not necessarily the same mechanism.

## 39. Heterotypic continuity

Heterotypic continuity means:
**an underlying function/process may persist while its observed behavioral manifestation changes with development**.

But a predictive relation between two different behaviors does not by itself prove a shared underlying process.

Mechanism must be tested.

## 40. 2024 continuity review

The contemporary review emphasizes:
- same behavior can arise for different reasons at different ages;
- different behaviors can serve similar functions;
- using the same measure over time can incorrectly assume homotypic continuity;
- different measures can be necessary if manifestation changes.

Canonical rule:

**behavioral form, function, and mechanism must be evaluated separately across age.**

## 41. Same behavior ≠ same function

Example structure:
avoidance at age 5 and age 15 may look similar but arise from different:
- cognitive capacities;
- social expectations;
- motivations.

Same observable form does not guarantee mechanistic continuity.

## 42. Different behavior ≠ different function

Development can change how a process is expressed.

The same regulatory or motivational function may be expressed through:
- different language;
- different social behavior;
- different strategies.

Heterotypic continuity is a hypothesis about underlying function, not surface similarity.

## 43. Changing measures can be scientifically necessary

A measure valid for:
- preschoolers

may be developmentally inappropriate for:
- adolescents.

Using age-appropriate measures can improve construct validity.

But changing measures creates a linking problem.

## 44. 2025/2026 heterotypic-measurement work

Recent developmental work explicitly models changing measures to account for heterotypic continuity.

This highlights a core tension:

- same items across age can preserve metric comparability but lose developmental validity;
- age-specific items can preserve developmental relevance but complicate longitudinal linking.

Design must solve both.

## 45. Measurement invariance

Longitudinal developmental comparisons often require that a measure functions sufficiently comparably across:
- ages;
- waves;
- groups.

Configural/metric/scalar concepts remain relevant.

But invariance is a statistical property of the measurement model, not proof of construct identity.

## 46. Measurement invariance is necessary but not sufficient

A 2025 methodological demonstration showed that even a nonsense scale can satisfy strong invariance constraints.

Therefore:

**measurement invariance does not prove that the same meaningful construct is being measured.**

It is one piece of validity evidence.

## 47. Age invariance can be partial

A 2026 representative German SDQ study across ages:
- 3–5;
- 6–10;
- 11–13;
- 14–17

supported:
- full metric invariance;
- residual invariance;
- only partial scalar invariance.

Some item intercepts differed by age.

This demonstrates why raw developmental mean comparisons need item-level checks.

## 48. Early-childhood invariance can also vary by group

A 2025 early-childhood SDQ study tested invariance across:
- age;
- race;
- sex;
- socioeconomic status.

Developmental comparisons can be confounded by demographic measurement differences as well as age.

## 49. Invariance thresholds are methodological choices

A 2026 methods paper argues that fixed cutoffs such as:
- ΔCFI = -.01

can be poor universal criteria for longitudinal/dyadic data.

Measurement-invariance evidence should be evaluated with:
- design;
- sample;
- model;
- degree/location of noninvariance.

Do not mechanize cutoff rules.

## 50. Measurement change can itself be developmental

Item interpretation may legitimately change as:
- language develops;
- self-concept changes;
- social roles change;
- cognitive capacity changes.

Noninvariance can reveal developmental transformation, not only bad measurement.

## 51. Cross-sectional design

Cross-sectional studies compare different-age people at one time.

Strengths:
- efficient;
- broad age range;
- no retest effect.

Limitations:
- cohort confounding;
- no direct within-person change;
- selection differences.

Cross-sectional age slope ≠ longitudinal developmental trajectory.

## 52. Longitudinal design

Longitudinal studies repeatedly measure the same people.

Strengths:
- within-person change;
- temporal ordering;
- individual trajectories.

Limitations:
- attrition;
- practice/retest;
- historical period;
- selective survival;
- measurement drift.

Longitudinal ≠ pure causal development.

## 53. Cross-sectional and longitudinal estimates can differ

Differences can arise from:
- cohort effects;
- practice effects;
- selective attrition;
- historical change;
- sampling.

One design should not automatically be treated as the truth.

Triangulation is useful.

## 54. Accelerated/cohort-sequential longitudinal designs

Accelerated designs recruit multiple age cohorts and follow them for overlapping intervals.

Advantages:
- cover wider age range faster;
- estimate within-person change.

Core assumption:
overlapping cohorts can be linked onto a common developmental trajectory after accounting for cohort differences.

That assumption must be tested.

## 55. Cohort effects threaten accelerated designs

If cohorts differ because they grew up under different:
- education systems;
- technologies;
- economic conditions;
- social norms;

their overlapping age data may not form one common trajectory.

Include cohort terms/interactions where possible.

## 56. Age–period–cohort problem

Three temporal concepts:

- **age:** years since birth;
- **period:** historical time of measurement;
- **cohort:** birth period.

Identity:

\`age = period - cohort\`.

They are exactly collinear.

No statistical model can estimate all three freely without identifying assumptions.

## 57. 2025 age–period–cohort personality demonstration

A study using:
- four repeated cross-sectional datasets;
- total N > 2 million;
- 2003–2022

showed that conclusions about age, cohort, and period differences depend strongly on identification assumptions.

This is a general developmental warning:

**age/period/cohort decomposition is assumption-dependent, not mechanically identifiable.**

## 58. Cohort effect

A cohort effect means people born in different historical periods differ because of experiences tied to their generation/birth era.

Potential sources:
- education;
- nutrition;
- technology;
- social norms;
- economic conditions.

Cohort difference ≠ biological age effect.

## 59. Period effect

A period effect is an influence of a historical event/time that affects multiple ages simultaneously.

Examples:
- pandemic;
- war;
- recession;
- policy change;
- technological shift.

Period exposure can look like developmental change if measurement waves align with the event.

## 60. Age effect

An age effect refers to systematic differences associated with aging/developmental time.

But age itself remains an index.

A strong age effect still needs developmental interpretation.

## 61. Practice/retest effects

Repeated assessment can improve scores through:
- familiarity;
- strategy learning;
- reduced anxiety.

This is especially relevant for:
- cognitive tasks;
- neuropsychological tests.

Observed longitudinal improvement can therefore overestimate developmental growth or mask decline.

## 62. Practice effects can be large enough to distort age interpretation

A 2026 randomized follow-up design with N=14,802 found:
- extra prior testing produced about 0.13 SD higher global cognitive scores;
- testing frequency added further gains.

Those differences corresponded to several years of between-person age differences in that sample.

Canonical rule:

**retest experience is a developmental confound, not trivial noise.**

## 63. Practice effect can itself be informative

Practice-related gain may reflect:
- learning capacity;
- strategy use;
- resilience.

Do not automatically subtract all retest change as artifact.

Model:
- generic retest offset;
- individual practice response;
- developmental trajectory

separately where relevant.

## 64. Attrition

Longitudinal studies lose participants over time.

Attrition can be related to:
- health;
- socioeconomic position;
- mobility;
- burden;
- psychological traits;
- developmental transition.

If dropout is selective, observed trajectories can be biased.

## 65. Missing at random is an assumption

Modern missing-data methods can reduce bias under assumptions.

They cannot recover information if:
- missingness depends on unobserved outcomes in unmodeled ways.

Report:
- retention;
- predictors of dropout;
- sensitivity analyses where important.

## 66. Selective survival

In older-adult research, participants remaining alive/healthy enough to be assessed are selected.

This can make older groups appear:
- healthier;
- more stable;
- less variable

than the source population.

Survival is part of the sampling process.

## 67. Repeated-measure burden changes the sample

High-burden developmental studies may preferentially retain:
- more organized;
- healthier;
- more resourced families/participants.

Measurement design can create developmental selection.

## 68. Developmental cascades

A developmental cascade is a sequence where change in one domain may influence later change in another.

Examples can connect:
- motor;
- language;
- social;
- cognitive;
- family processes.

Cascade is a framework for temporal interdependence.

It is not causal proof.

## 69. 2025 cascade review

The review of developmental cascades emphasizes:
- cross-domain connections;
- multiple timescales;
- reciprocal effects;
- high causal-identification requirements.

A chain of longitudinal correlations is not enough to establish a causal cascade.

## 70. Transactional development

Transactional models propose reciprocal person↔environment influences over time.

Examples:
- child behavior changes caregiver response;
- caregiver response changes later child behavior.

This is more realistic than one-direction environment→child models.

But reciprocal paths require appropriately timed repeated data.

## 71. Developmental equifinality

**Equifinality:** different developmental pathways can lead to similar outcomes.

Therefore:
same outcome ≠ same developmental history/mechanism.

This is especially important in clinical inference.

## 72. Developmental multifinality

**Multifinality:** similar early exposure can lead to different later outcomes.

Therefore:
same early risk ≠ same future.

Moderators, later contexts, and chance/learning matter.

## 73. Age norms are distributions

Developmental milestones have:
- central tendencies;
- broad normal variation.

A person being earlier/later than average is not automatically:
- impaired;
- advanced;
- pathological.

Thresholds require criterion validity, not just percentile rarity.

## 74. Developmental delay versus difference

A lower score at one age can reflect:
- delayed trajectory;
- stable individual difference;
- measurement issue;
- different developmental pathway.

"Delay" implies a trajectory hypothesis:
eventual movement toward a normative sequence.

One cross-sectional low score cannot establish delay.

## 75. Stage theories require explicit tests

Stage theories predict:
- qualitative transitions;
- ordered structure;
- discontinuity.

A nonlinear age curve alone does not prove a stage.

Need evidence for:
- qualitative reorganization;
- reliable ordering;
- stage-specific structure.

## 76. Developmental discontinuity is not pathology

Rapid/qualitative change can occur during:
- puberty;
- school transition;
- role transitions;
- skill acquisition.

Discontinuity can be normative.

## 77. Stability is not developmental success

Stable:
- behavior;
- trait;
- symptom

can be adaptive or maladaptive depending on function/context.

Development should not be evaluated solely by stability.

## 78. Change is not developmental improvement

A shift can be:
- adaptive;
- maladaptive;
- neutral.

Direction requires an outcome criterion.

"Development" does not mean "progress" by definition.

## 79. Chronological age thresholds are institutional, not always biological

Legal/school thresholds can create:
- exposure changes;
- expectations;
- peer composition.

Observed discontinuities at age cutoffs may reflect institutions rather than maturation.

Natural-experiment designs can sometimes exploit these thresholds.

## 80. Culture shapes developmental pathways

Development occurs within:
- family systems;
- schools;
- communities;
- economic structures;
- cultural norms.

The same chronological age can carry different:
- roles;
- autonomy;
- responsibilities;
- social expectations.

Western age-stage models should not be assumed universal.

## 81. 2025 ecocultural adolescent framework

The current Annual Review emphasizes adolescence as a neurobiological and sociocultural transition.

It treats development as interaction between:
- changing neural systems;
- cultural/social environment.

This supports:
**context is constitutive of developmental processes, not a nuisance covariate**.

## 82. Developmental timing can be culturally structured

School entry, work, partnership, autonomy, and family responsibilities occur at different ages across societies.

A "developmental milestone" can therefore mix:
- biology;
- institution;
- culture.

Specify which.

## 83. Age moderation does not automatically prove development

If an X→Y association is stronger at age 15 than 25:
possible explanations include:
- maturation;
- cohort;
- context;
- range restriction;
- measurement differences.

Age interaction is a clue, not a mechanism.

## 84. Developmental mediation needs temporal resolution

A mediator should:
- change after exposure;
- precede outcome change;
- be measured on appropriate timescale.

Annual measures cannot identify a mediator operating within days without strong assumptions.

## 85. Dense data do not solve developmental inference automatically

Intensive longitudinal data can measure:
- moments;
- days;
- weeks.

They still require:
- psychometric reliability;
- measurement invariance;
- missingness handling;
- time-scale alignment.

The 2026 psychometrics primer notes many intensive studies still omit these checks.

## 86. Within-person ≠ developmental causality

A within-person association:
**when X is higher than usual, Y later changes**
reduces some between-person confounding.

It does not eliminate:
- time-varying confounding;
- reverse causality;
- measurement coupling.

Within-person is not synonymous with causal.

## 87. Developmental prediction versus developmental mechanism

Early variable X can predict later Y because of:
- direct effect;
- shared causes;
- stable trait;
- ongoing exposure;
- mediation;
- selection.

Longitudinal prediction alone does not identify mechanism.

## 88. Individual trajectory uncertainty

Estimating one person's developmental slope can be noisy when:
- few waves;
- unreliable measure;
- short timespan.

Random-effects estimates are shrunk toward population mean.

Avoid overinterpreting individual slopes from sparse data.

## 91. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- chronological age is not a developmental mechanism;
- maturation and chronological age are noninterchangeable;
- pubertal status, timing, and tempo are distinct;
- sensitive and critical periods are distinct claims;
- sensitive-period effects are process/exposure/outcome specific;
- plasticity is neither universally beneficial nor unlimited;
- continuity can concern different behavioral forms/functions/mechanisms;
- cross-sectional and longitudinal age effects answer different questions;
- age/period/cohort separation requires identifying assumptions;
- longitudinal measurement invariance is important but insufficient for construct validity;
- practice effects and attrition can materially bias developmental trajectories.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- adolescence may involve heightened sensitivity for specific sociocultural processes;
- puberty can explain developmental variance beyond chronological age;
- sensitive periods may be probabilistic and linked to system-specific plasticity;
- accelerated longitudinal designs can efficiently estimate broad age trajectories;
- changing measures can better represent heterotypic developmental continuity;
- developmental cascades and reciprocal transactions are useful frameworks;
- cultural/institutional context can shift developmental timing and expression.

### Not established as universal
- one developmental clock;
- one universal adolescent sensitive period;
- one fixed pubertal age range for psychological mechanisms;
- one critical period calendar across cortex/behavior;
- one age trajectory across cohorts/cultures;
- one invariant measure from childhood through adulthood;
- one causal cascade inferred from observational longitudinal data.

## Canonical operating rule

**Define the developmental construct and timescale -> choose the relevant developmental clock rather than assuming chronological age -> separate maturation, exposure timing, duration, and context -> distinguish sensitive from critical-period claims -> establish whether continuity concerns behavior, function, construct, or mechanism -> choose a design that separates within-person change from age/cohort/period differences -> verify developmental measurement validity and invariance -> model practice, attrition, and changing expression -> state only the narrowest supported developmental inference.**

Never:
**age = cause; age = maturity; puberty = age; sensitive = critical; plasticity = benefit; cross-sectional difference = development; longitudinal change = pure age effect; cohort = age; same behavior = same mechanism; invariance = construct truth; cascade = causation.**
