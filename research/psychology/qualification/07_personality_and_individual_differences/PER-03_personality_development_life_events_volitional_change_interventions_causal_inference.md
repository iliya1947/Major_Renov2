# PER-03 — Personality development and intentional change: life events, goals, states, interventions, causal inference, and measurement

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public personality-development evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

PER-01 established that traits are probabilistic, context-sensitive, and both stable and changeable.
PER-02 established that trait hierarchy, criterion validity, and measurement method determine what a personality score can support.

PER-03 addresses the developmental and causal question:

**When does observed personality change plausibly reflect enduring trait change, what roles do life events, selection, socialization, repeated states, goals, and interventions play, and what evidence is required before claiming that an experience or intervention caused personality change?**

High-risk errors to prevent:
- "life event happened, therefore it changed personality";
- "marriage/parenthood/work automatically makes people mature";
- "post-event change = socialization";
- "pre-event group difference = event effect";
- "selection and socialization are mutually exclusive";
- "goal to change = actual change";
- "wanting to change harder = changing more";
- "state change = trait change";
- "trait questionnaire change = enduring personality change";
- "pre–post change = intervention effect";
- "waitlist control eliminates all expectancy/demand effects";
- "self-report change = objective behavioral change";
- "informant agreement = mechanism";
- "intervention effect = change in one specific process";
- "change persists for 3 months = permanent";
- "one-year follow-up proves no attrition bias";
- "small average life-event effects mean nobody changes";
- "large individual differences in change prove idiosyncratic causal effects";
- "life-event association = causal socialization";
- "personality can change, therefore any desired change is feasible";
- "plasticity = unlimited malleability".

## Core rule

**Personality change is a longitudinal inference, not a difference between two observed scores. Enduring trait change should be separated from momentary state shifts, measurement error, regression to the mean, recontextualization, expectancy effects, selection into environments, and normative age-related change. Life events and interventions can contribute to change, but average effects are usually modest and causal interpretation depends on prospective timing, comparison conditions, repeated measurement, multi-method evidence, persistence, and alternative explanations.**

Canonical reasoning:

\`establish reliable baseline -> define trait/state target -> measure pre-event/pre-intervention trajectory -> separate selection from exposure -> model event/intervention timing and normative change -> track state/behavior/habit changes -> test persistence and multi-method convergence -> rule out regression/demand/attrition/confounding -> infer only the narrowest supported developmental or causal claim\`.

## 1. Personality is plastic but not unconstrained

Modern personality science supports both:
- substantial rank-order stability;
- meaningful mean-level and individual change.

Plasticity means:
**traits can change under some conditions**.

It does not mean:
- all traits are equally malleable;
- any person can reach any desired score;
- change is easy;
- change is permanent by default.

## 2. Trait change versus score change

Observed score change can arise from:
- enduring trait change;
- state fluctuation;
- measurement error;
- response-style change;
- changed reference frame;
- expectancy/demand;
- regression to the mean;
- instrument drift.

Therefore:
**T2 − T1 ≠ trait change by definition**.

## 3. Enduring trait change

A strong claim of trait change requires evidence that:
- change exceeds expected unreliability;
- it persists beyond the immediate trigger;
- it is not only a temporary state;
- measurement meaning remains comparable;
- relevant alternative causes are considered.

No single duration threshold defines "enduring" universally.

## 4. Level, rank, profile, and variability change remain distinct

PER-01's longitudinal distinctions remain mandatory.

A person can:
- change absolute level;
- retain rank relative to peers;
- change profile shape;
- change state variability.

"Personality changed" must specify which metric changed.

## 5. Natural personality development is multicausal

Possible contributors include:
- biological maturation;
- learning;
- role demands;
- repeated experiences;
- relationships;
- health;
- goals;
- institutions;
- intentional practice.

A life event is one possible trigger, not a complete mechanism.

## 6. 2024 mechanism framework

Jackson and Wright's 2024 review organizes personality change mechanisms around:
- **preconditions**;
- **triggers**;
- **reinforcers**;
- **integrators**.

The review emphasizes:
- both naturally occurring and intentional change;
- forces that stabilize traits;
- gradual change as common;
- abrupt change as possible;
- generally modest effect sizes;
- mixed and incomplete evidence on mechanisms.

This project uses the framework as a causal checklist, not as a proven universal sequence.

## 7. Preconditions

Change may depend on:
- opportunity;
- motivation;
- cognitive/behavioral capacity;
- environmental support;
- discrepancy awareness.

A trigger without supportive preconditions may produce only transient change.

## 8. Triggers

Triggers can include:
- life events;
- interventions;
- crises;
- role transitions;
- repeated feedback;
- deliberate goals.

A trigger starts a candidate change process.

It does not guarantee integration into a trait.

## 9. Reinforcers

New behavior or states may persist when reinforced by:
- reward;
- success;
- social feedback;
- habit;
- environmental fit;
- identity consistency.

Repeated reinforcement can stabilize changed state distributions.

## 10. Integrators

Integration refers to processes that make new patterns more self-maintaining, such as:
- self-concept updating;
- habitualization;
- environmental restructuring;
- role consistency.

These mechanisms are plausible but not equally established empirically.

## 11. TESSERA-style logic

One influential developmental logic is:

\`Triggering situation -> Expectancy -> State/State Expression -> Reaction -> Reinforcement -> repeated sequences -> trait change\`.

The useful principle is:
**trait change should emerge from repeated changes in momentary patterns, not from a label alone**.

Do not assume every state deviation accumulates into a trait.

## 12. Life events as candidate triggers

Commonly studied events include:
- graduation;
- employment;
- unemployment;
- marriage;
- childbirth;
- divorce;
- widowhood;
- retirement.

They can alter:
- roles;
- routines;
- incentives;
- social networks;
- stress;
- opportunities for trait expression.

## 13. Life event ≠ standardized treatment

Two people experiencing "the same" event can differ in:
- timing;
- voluntariness;
- meaning;
- intensity;
- duration;
- role investment;
- resources;
- background stressors.

Event labels hide exposure heterogeneity.

## 14. Selection effect

**Selection** means pre-existing personality predicts:
- entering;
- avoiding;
- timing;
- persistence

in an environment/event.

Example:
people who enter a role may already differ before the event.

Selection is not socialization.

## 15. Socialization effect

**Socialization** means exposure to an environment/event contributes to subsequent personality change.

To infer it, compare:
- pre-event trajectory;
- exposed versus credible comparison group;
- post-event changes;
- confounders.

A post-event change in exposed people alone is insufficient.

## 16. Selection and socialization can coexist

A person can:
1. select an environment partly because of personality;
2. then be changed by that environment.

Therefore:
**selection versus socialization**
is often a decomposition problem, not an either/or theory choice.

## 17. Anticipatory change

Personality can change before an event because people:
- prepare;
- anticipate;
- change roles gradually;
- experience precursor conditions.

A simple pre/post design can misattribute anticipatory change to the event itself.

Use multiple pre-event waves when possible.

## 18. Normative developmental change is a confound

If conscientiousness increases with age, people starting jobs during the same age period may also increase even without the job.

Event inference needs:
- comparison groups;
- modeled age/time trends;
- multiple waves.

## 19. Regression to the mean

Individuals/groups selected because of unusual baseline values tend to show less extreme later values even without causal influence.

This is especially dangerous when:
- change is predicted from baseline discrepancy;
- peer groups are compared by initial distance.

## 20. 2025 peer-group replication warning

A preregistered randomized peer-group study:
- N = 351;
- 92 study groups;
- first-semester students

initially showed patterns consistent with assimilation toward peer-group personality.

But effects were similar when:
- group assignments were randomly reshuffled after data collection;
- time order was reversed;
- alternative centering was used.

The authors concluded regression to the mean was a more plausible explanation than peer socialization.

Canonical lesson:

**apparently elegant change effects can be statistical artifacts even under partial randomization.**

## 21. 2024 life-event meta-analysis

The preregistered meta-analysis integrated:
- 44 studies;
- 89 samples;
- N = 121,187.

Findings:
- reliable but relatively small event-related personality changes;
- stronger/more consistent effects in work than love domains;
- graduation, first job, new relationship, marriage, and divorce showed some of the stronger patterns.

Effects were heterogeneous.

This supports:
**life events can relate to personality change**
but not
**life events reliably transform personality**.

## 22. Average event effects are small

The 2025 coordinated data analysis across seven large panel studies:
- N = 196,256

found several replicable Big Five changes around events.

Average standardized event-related personality change:
- approximately \`b_std = .06\`.

Changes were:
- similar in magnitude to self-esteem changes;
- smaller than life-satisfaction changes.

## 23. Specific examples from 2025 CDA

Across coordinated analyses:
- new employment predicted increases in conscientiousness;
- new employment predicted increases in emotional stability;
- marriage predicted a decrease in openness.

These are average associations under the coordinated models.

They are not deterministic individual trajectories.

## 24. Small average effect ≠ no meaningful individuals

An average of .06 can coexist with:
- larger change in some people;
- no change in others;
- opposite-direction change in others.

Population means do not reveal individual response distributions.

## 25. Individual differences in event response

A 2026 study:
- N = 623;
- up to three informants per target;
- three waves at 2-year intervals

found event-related personality effects depended on:
- age;
- gender;
- subjective event perception;
- type of personality change;
- timing;
- repeated events;
- assessment method.

This supports substantial heterogeneity.

It also warns against universal event→trait rules.

## 26. Heterogeneity does not prove person-specific causal effects

If effect sizes vary across people:
- true moderation may exist;
- measurement error may vary;
- event exposure may differ;
- models may be underpowered.

Individual heterogeneity requires its own reliable estimation.

## 27. Repeated minor experiences can matter

A frequently assessed 2023/2024 study:
- N = 4,904;
- 47,814 assessments;
- median retest ≈ 35 days;
- 25 major/minor events

found trajectory shifts after:
- some major events;
- some recurrent minor experiences.

This supports repeated-experience theories.

It does not make every repeated event causal.

## 28. Major event versus repeated microexperience

A major event may alter personality because it changes everyday experience repeatedly.

Thus:
**event label**
may be less mechanistically informative than:
**what repeated states, routines, rewards, and roles changed afterward**.

## 29. Event perception matters

Objective event occurrence and subjective event appraisal can differ.

Potential dimensions:
- positive/negative valence;
- controllability;
- predictability;
- impact;
- centrality.

Subjective perception may moderate effects.

But it can also be influenced by personality itself.

## 30. Event appraisal can be both mediator and confound

Personality may shape event appraisal.
Event appraisal may shape subsequent state patterns.

Therefore:
**event -> appraisal -> trait change**
and
**trait -> appraisal**
can both operate.

Cross-lagged association alone does not establish mediation.

## 31. 2026 military-training study: selection and change

A 2026 weighted control-group study followed:
- 1,191 soldiers;
- 441 civilians at baseline;
- 54 weeks;
- four assessments.

Before training, soldiers differed from civilians:
- higher conscientiousness;
- lower open-mindedness.

These are **selection differences**.

During 18-week training, soldiers showed larger mean-level increases in:
- extraversion;
- conscientiousness;
- emotional stability;
- self-esteem.

These are **group differences in change consistent with socialization-like exposure effects** under the design.

## 32. Post-training persistence in military study

After training:
- no further group differences in change were found.

This means the study supports short-term group-differential change during training.

It does not establish:
- unlimited permanence;
- identical individual response;
- one causal mechanism.

## 33. Social investment was not supported as moderator there

The military-training study found no evidence that:
- stronger social investment;
- explicit trait-change goals

moderated the observed changes.

Therefore role investment is theoretically plausible but not a universally demonstrated mechanism.

## 34. Life events can be selected by traits

Prior longitudinal research shows personality can predict event occurrence.

Examples can include:
- relationship transitions;
- employment transitions;
- volunteering;
- retirement timing.

Therefore any event→change model should test trait→event pathways when plausible.

## 35. Environmental fit versus change

An apparent adaptation can occur because people:
- change themselves;
- change environment;
- select better-fitting environment;
- reinterpret environment.

Not every improved person–environment fit is trait change.

## 36. Intentional personality change

Volitional personality change means:
**self-directed change toward a personally endorsed trait goal**.

Possible targets:
- more extraverted;
- more conscientious;
- more emotionally stable;
- less neurotic.

A desire is a goal, not an outcome.

## 37. Desire to change is common but weakly predictive alone

The 2024 systematic review identified:
- 30 longitudinal empirical studies;
- N = 7,719.

Across non-intervention studies:
**simply wanting to change was only weakly related to actual trait change**.

Therefore:
**goal intensity ≠ realized change**.

## 38. Structured interventions show preliminary efficacy

The same systematic review found intervention-induced desired trait change:
- 7 studies;
- 26 effect sizes;
- overall \`d = .22\`;
- 95% CI [.005, .433].

Follow-up:
- 4 studies;
- 17 effect sizes;
- \`d = .37\`;
- 95% CI [.140, .591].

Evidence is promising but still limited in study count.

## 39. Trait-specific intervention evidence is imprecise

The systematic review reported trait-specific estimates with few studies/effects.

Some estimates favored:
- conscientiousness;
- extraversion.

Confidence intervals were wide for several traits.

Do not rank trait "changeability" confidently from the current small evidence base.

## 40. Goal alone is insufficient

The review supports a broader principle:
lasting change likely needs:
- repeated behavior/state changes;
- feedback;
- habit formation;
- environmental support;
- self-regulation.

A stated goal without repeated implementation is weak evidence.

## 41. 2021 PEACH randomized intervention

A major digital RCT:
- N = 1,523;
- 3-month smartphone intervention.

Intervention participants changed more than waitlist controls in desired directions.

Self-report effects:
- increases: \`d=.52\`;
- decreases: \`d=-.58\`.

Observer-reported increases:
- \`d=.35\`.

Observer-reported decreases:
- not significant (\`d=-.22\`).

This is strong evidence that structured intervention can alter measured traits.

## 42. Multi-method convergence strengthens but does not close causality

Observer detection in PEACH reduces the explanation:
**self-report demand alone**.

But observer report can still reflect:
- communicated goals;
- changed reputation;
- shared expectations.

Multi-method change is stronger evidence, not direct mechanism proof.

## 43. Three-month maintenance is not permanence

PEACH changes persisted for:
- 3 months after intervention.

This supports medium-term maintenance.

It does not establish lifelong change.

## 44. One-year follow-up

A later 1-year follow-up found achieved changes:
- remained stable for some goals;
- continued in desired direction for some goals.

However:
- only n = 157 completed the 1-year follow-up from baseline N = 1,523;
- completers differed from non-completers on several personality traits.

Thus the long-term persistence signal is encouraging but vulnerable to attrition bias.

## 45. Attrition is a causal/validity threat

If participants who remain:
- are more conscientious;
- are more agreeable;
- differ in neuroticism/openness;

then maintenance estimates may not represent all original participants.

Long-term change claims need:
- attrition analysis;
- weighting/imputation when justified;
- sensitivity analysis.

## 46. State change as candidate pathway

A 2025 analysis of the PEACH intervention:
- N = 956;
- daily/weekly state measures;
- trait self- and observer-reports

found greater repeated state deviations in the intended direction were associated with greater trait change.

This supports:
**repeated state expression as a plausible proximal pathway**.

## 47. Engagement matters more than desire alone

In the same 2025 study, stronger change was associated with:
- commitment;
- completing implementation intentions;
- enjoyment/reinforcement.

In contrast:
- desire strength;
- expected attainability;
- belief that personality is changeable

showed no consistent effects in that sample.

## 48. Mediation-like association is not definitive mechanism

People who engage more may:
- differ in unmeasured motivation;
- respond better generally;
- report change more favorably.

Association between state change and trait change supports mechanism hypotheses but does not uniquely identify the pathway.

## 49. 2025 self-improvement/self-acceptance RCT raises demand concerns

Three studies:
- combined N = 2,094;
- 3-month interventions;
- 6-month follow-up;
- self-improvement and self-acceptance conditions.

Participants in intervention groups:
- reduced current–ideal discrepancy;
- improved well-being.

Critically:
- control participants changed similarly;
- no significant between-group difference in key change patterns.

The authors highlighted:
- recruitment framing;
- expectancy;
- demand;
- control-group specification

as possible explanations for apparent intervention change.

## 50. Pre–post intervention change is not treatment effect

The 2025 RCT provides a direct rule:

**within-group improvement ≠ intervention efficacy**.

Need:
- randomized comparison;
- active/waitlist control;
- credible counterfactual.

Even waitlist control may not fully remove expectancy if recruitment itself induces change.

## 51. Control condition matters

Different controls estimate different causal contrasts:

- no-treatment control;
- waitlist;
- attention control;
- active control;
- alternative intervention.

A waitlist answers:
**intervention package versus waiting**.

It may not isolate:
- expectancy;
- engagement;
- contact;
- self-monitoring.

## 52. Active controls remain a priority

The 2026 review of volitional personality development identifies priorities:
- active-control randomized trials;
- multi-method outcomes;
- long-term follow-up.

This reflects current uncertainty about:
- specific components;
- nonspecific effects;
- mechanisms.

## 53. Intervention component dismantling

To know what works, separate:
- goal setting;
- psychoeducation;
- implementation intentions;
- behavioral challenges;
- feedback;
- reinforcement;
- social support;
- self-monitoring.

Package-level efficacy does not identify active ingredient.

## 54. Microrandomization can improve mechanism inference

For mobile interventions, repeated randomization of:
- prompts;
- timing;
- strategy

can estimate proximal component effects.

This is stronger than correlating engagement with outcomes.

## 55. 2026 tailoring study

A 2026 intensive longitudinal study:
- N = 399;
- 39,878 observations;
- intervention targeting neuroticism

tested strategy, timing, and context for proximal outcomes.

After a published correction to data preparation, the corrected report indicates:
- state-level strategies produced more positive momentary-affect effects than habit-level strategies;
- adherence varied by context/timing, including greater adherence in quieter/home contexts.

This informs proximal intervention design.

It does **not** establish which component causes enduring trait change.

## 56. Corrections matter

The 2026 tailoring article had an erratum involving:
- mislabeled variables;
- inconsistent compliance preparation;
- duplicated rows;
- changed numerical results.

Canonical methodological lesson:

**use the corrected version when interpreting current evidence**.

Publication status/version is part of evidence quality.

## 57. 2025 older/younger adult intervention

A preregistered multi-method intervention:
- N = 165;
- age 19–78;
- 8-week socio-emotional training;
- 3- and 12-month follow-up

found changes in:
- state emotional stability;
- state extraversion;
- explicit trait self-concepts;
- implicit extraversion self-concept.

Effects were broadly similar across younger and older adults.

## 58. State-to-trait relations were selective there

In that study:
- state emotional-stability change predicted explicit trait-self-concept change;
- state extraversion change did not show comparable clear state→trait association;
- implicit measures did not move uniformly.

This is evidence against:
**one universal state→trait accumulation rule**.

## 59. Age does not eliminate plasticity

The 2025 study suggests older adults can show intervention-related changes.

But:
- N was modest;
- targets were emotional stability/extraversion;
- one intervention format was used.

Do not generalize to all traits or all ages.

DEV will treat age-specific development more deeply.

## 60. Intentional change versus self-acceptance

A personality goal can be addressed by:
- changing current trait expression;
- changing ideal standards;
- increasing acceptance;
- altering person–environment fit.

Reducing current–ideal discrepancy is not identical to trait change.

## 61. Personality goal can be maladaptive or externally pressured

Change goals may be:
- self-endorsed;
- socially imposed;
- based on unrealistic ideals;
- responses to stigma.

An intervention should not assume:
**more socially desirable trait = better for every person/context**.

## 62. Ethics of personality intervention

The 2026 Perspectives review identifies concerns including:
- potential harm;
- social pressure to conform;
- unequal access;
- societal implications.

Ethical personality intervention requires:
- autonomy;
- informed consent;
- self-endorsed goals;
- risk monitoring;
- no coercive normative target.

## 63. Trait desirability is context-dependent

Higher:
- extraversion;
- conscientiousness;
- agreeableness;
- emotional stability

can predict many desirable outcomes on average.

But every trait has:
- costs;
- nonlinearities;
- context dependence.

Personality intervention should not target arbitrary maximization.

## 64. Change goal ≠ clinical treatment target

Wanting lower neuroticism is not the same as treating:
- anxiety disorder;
- depression;
- trauma.

Clinical interventions may change traits as secondary outcomes.

Trait change does not replace diagnosis/treatment evaluation.

## 65. Clinical-state change can mimic trait change

In treatment contexts, reductions in:
- depression;
- anxiety;
- stress

can shift trait questionnaire responses.

Need to distinguish:
- symptom/state relief;
- enduring trait change.

Clinical research suggests they are related but not perfectly redundant.

## 66. Self-concept change can mimic behavioral trait change

A person can change:
- how they describe themselves;
- their standards;
- their interpretation of items

without equivalent change in daily behavior.

Therefore multi-method/state/behavioral assessment is valuable.

## 67. Behavior can change before self-concept

Repeated new behavior may initially feel atypical.

Over time:
- self-concept may update.

This is a plausible integration pathway.

Do not assume self-concept leads behavior in every case.

## 68. Self-concept can change before behavior

Psychoeducation or reframing can change:
- self-perception;
- labels;
- confidence

before stable behavioral changes occur.

Therefore temporal ordering must be measured.

## 69. Trait change can be environment-specific

A person may become more:
- assertive at work;
- organized in study;
without global cross-context change.

Contextualized trait change may precede or remain separate from global trait change.

## 70. Generalization is required for broad trait claims

If intervention changes behavior only in trained situations:
valid claim:
**context-specific skill/state change**.

A broad trait claim requires evidence of transfer across:
- settings;
- tasks;
- relationships;
- time.

## 71. Measurement invariance over time

Longitudinal mean change assumes the measure retains comparable meaning.

Test:
- longitudinal invariance;
- item functioning;
- response-shift effects.

If item interpretation changes, mean differences can be misleading.

## 72. Response shift

Intervention can change standards used for self-rating.

Example:
after learning what conscientious behavior entails, a person may rate themselves more critically despite behavioral improvement.

This can hide or invert real change.

Self-report change must be interpreted with measurement process.

## 73. Repeated testing effects

Frequent questionnaires can:
- increase self-monitoring;
- clarify goals;
- induce behavior change.

Thus the measurement schedule itself may function as an intervention.

Control groups should receive comparable assessment when appropriate.

## 74. Demand characteristics

Participants may infer:
- desired direction of change;
- experimenter's hypothesis.

They may report accordingly.

Demand risk is especially high when:
- change goal is explicit;
- outcome is self-report;
- participants know intervention purpose.

Observer/behavioral measures and active controls help.

## 75. Expectancy effects

Belief that intervention will work can change:
- motivation;
- self-report;
- behavior.

Expectancy is not "fake"—it can itself be causal.

But if the claim is about a specific intervention mechanism, expectancy must be separated.

## 76. Adherence is post-randomization

People who adhere more often differ from those who adhere less.

Therefore:
**adherence–outcome correlation ≠ causal adherence effect**.

Microrandomized or instrumental approaches can strengthen inference.

## 77. Informant change can be reputation change

Observers may update impressions because:
- behavior changed;
- target discussed change goals;
- observer expectations shifted.

Informant report is useful but not mechanism-free.

## 78. Behavioral verification

Stronger trait-change evidence can include:
- experience-sampled states;
- observed behavior;
- informant reports;
- objective records;
- repeated trait scales.

No one channel is mandatory for every study.

Triangulation should match the construct.

## 79. Long-term follow-up

Follow-up should ask:
- is change maintained?
- does it decay?
- does it grow?
- does behavior generalize?
- do observers detect it?
- does criterion functioning change?

"Maintenance" is a trajectory question, not one delayed time point.

## 80. Trait change can continue after intervention

Post-intervention growth may occur because:
- new habits self-reinforce;
- environments respond differently;
- identity updates;
- participant continues practice.

It can also reflect:
- selective attrition;
- ongoing external changes.

Need continued measurement.

## 81. Reversion does not prove intervention failure

Some targeted change may decay when:
- environmental supports disappear;
- old routines return.

This can indicate:
- context dependence;
- insufficient integration.

Trait malleability does not imply zero stabilizing forces.

## 82. Person–environment transactions can maintain change

Changed behavior can elicit:
- new social feedback;
- different opportunities;
- different expectations.

These environmental responses can reinforce the new pattern.

This creates feedback:
\`person change -> environment change -> further person change\`.

## 83. Selection can undermine naive event estimates

If people with certain traits are more likely to:
- marry;
- divorce;
- get promoted;
- volunteer;
- enter military roles

then pre-existing differences can produce apparent event effects.

Prospective baseline measurement is essential.

## 84. Causal identification hierarchy for natural events

Stronger designs can include:
1. multiple pre/post waves;
2. matched comparison groups;
3. fixed-effects models;
4. propensity weighting;
5. interrupted/time-varying models;
6. natural experiments;
7. randomized exposure where ethically possible.

No method fully solves all confounding.

## 85. Randomization is strongest for interventions, not all mechanisms

Randomizing an intervention can identify:
**effect of the intervention package**.

It does not automatically identify:
- which component worked;
- mediation pathway;
- why people differed.

Mechanism needs additional design.

## 86. Mediation requires temporal and causal assumptions

If:
- intervention changes states;
- state change predicts trait change,

that is consistent with mediation.

Stronger claim requires:
- temporal ordering;
- intervention→mediator effect;
- mediator→outcome identification;
- confounder control.

Post-treatment mediator–outcome association alone is insufficient.

## 87. Trait change and outcome change

If personality change correlates with:
- well-being change;
- health behavior change;
- relationship change,

possible models include:
- trait change causes outcome change;
- outcome change causes trait change;
- common process causes both.

Trait change is not automatically the mediator.

## 88. Intervention efficacy versus utility

An intervention can produce a statistically detectable trait change but still have uncertain practical utility if:
- effect is small;
- burden high;
- benefits unclear;
- harms possible.

Evaluate:
- desired outcome;
- cost;
- persistence;
- adverse effects.

## 89. Individual differences in intervention response

Average treatment effect can hide:
- strong responders;
- nonresponders;
- opposite-direction responders.

Reliable treatment-effect heterogeneity needs:
- sufficient sample;
- shrinkage;
- preregistered moderators;
- replication.

Avoid post hoc responder typologies.

## 90. Personalized intervention

2026 reviews propose tailoring:
- timing;
- strategy;
- context;
- trait target.

Personalization is promising.

It should be evaluated against:
- simpler fixed interventions;
- overfitting risk;
- fairness;
- privacy.

## 91. Change toward ideal versus change toward function

A person's ideal trait score may reflect:
- personal values;
- social desirability;
- unrealistic comparison.

Functional outcome may improve without moving toward ideal.

Intervention success should not be defined only by ideal-self discrepancy.

## 92. Desired direction may change

People can revise goals after:
- experience;
- feedback;
- changed environment.

Goal revision is not necessarily failure.

MOT principles of goal monitoring and disengagement apply.

4. Is variability change relevant?

6. Is longitudinal invariance established?

8. Is change persistent beyond immediate state effects?

## 94. Failure modes

A. Event occurred -> personality changed because of event: **invalid**.

B. Pre/post difference = socialization: **invalid**.

C. Baseline difference = event effect: **invalid**.

D. Selection or socialization as mutually exclusive: **false dichotomy**.

E. Marriage/parenthood/work = maturity: **unsupported universal rule**.

F. Small average event effect = nobody changes: **invalid**.

G. Heterogeneity = proven person-specific causal effect: **invalid**.

H. Goal to change = actual change: **invalid**.

I. Desire strength = change success: **unsupported**.

J. State change = enduring trait change: **invalid**.

K. Trait score change = true trait change by definition: **invalid**.

L. Within-group pre/post improvement = intervention effect: **invalid**.

M. Waitlist = control for all nonspecific effects: **invalid**.

N. Self-report change = objective behavioral change: **invalid**.

O. Informant change = mechanism proof: **invalid**.

P. Adherence correlation = causal treatment-dose effect: **invalid**.

Q. Three-month persistence = permanent: **invalid**.

R. One-year completer effect = unbiased long-term effect: **invalid without attrition controls**.

S. Intervention package effect = identified active ingredient: **invalid**.

T. Plasticity = unlimited malleability: **invalid**.

U. More socially desirable trait = always better: **invalid**.

V. Trait change = clinical recovery: **invalid construct substitution**.

## 95. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- personality traits are plastic but relatively stable;
- score change is not automatically trait change;
- life-event personality effects are generally small on average;
- selection and socialization can coexist;
- event studies require prospective timing and comparison conditions;
- volitional change goals alone weakly predict realized change;
- structured personality interventions can change measured traits on average;
- within-group pre/post change is not causal evidence;
- repeated state/behavior change is a plausible proximal pathway;
- intervention effects require persistence and multi-method validation;
- regression to the mean, demand, expectancy, attrition, and response shift are major threats.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- work/love life events can predict small Big Five changes;
- repeated minor experiences may contribute to trait trajectories;
- military training can produce group-differential short-term trait changes;
- PEACH-style interventions can produce desired self- and some observer-reported change;
- commitment, implementation, and reinforcement are associated with greater intervention gains;
- older adults can show intervention-related personality changes;
- context/timing can influence proximal intervention engagement.

### Not established as universal
- one life-event effect on personality;
- social investment as a universal mediator;
- one state-to-trait accumulation mechanism;
- one effective personality-change intervention;
- permanent change after brief follow-up;
- personality-change goals as sufficient causes;
- one optimal trait direction for all people;
- one active intervention component explaining package effects.

## Canonical operating rule

**Define whether change concerns state, behavior, self-concept, or trait -> establish reliable pre-change trajectory -> separate selection, anticipation, normative development, and exposure -> model event/intervention timing with a credible counterfactual -> measure repeated state/behavior implementation -> use multi-method outcomes and longitudinal invariance -> test persistence, generalization, attrition, demand, expectancy, regression-to-mean and response-shift alternatives -> separate intervention-package efficacy from active mechanism -> state only the narrowest supported developmental or causal inference.**

Never:
**event = personality cause; pre/post = intervention effect; goal = change; state = trait; waitlist = full placebo control; adherence = causal dose; observer agreement = mechanism; short follow-up = permanence; plasticity = unlimited malleability.**
