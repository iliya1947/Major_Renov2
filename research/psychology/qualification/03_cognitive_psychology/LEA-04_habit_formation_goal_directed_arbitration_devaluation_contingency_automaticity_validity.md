# LEA-04 — Habit formation and goal-directed/habit arbitration: devaluation, contingency degradation, automaticity, overtraining, and measurement validity

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**

Current evidence review: **2026-11-19**.

## Purpose and dependency

LEA-03 established that model-free/model-based computational indices cannot be equated automatically with psychological habit/goal-directed control. This block addresses the next inferential failure: calling repetition, speed, automaticity, a devaluation-test error, or fitted model-free control a demonstrated stimulus–response habit.

The target is **instrumental action control**, not everyday use of the word "habit" in all its meanings. Naturalistic routines and questionnaires are covered as related but noninterchangeable constructs.

## Core rule

**An observed failure to adapt action after an outcome or contingency change is not uniquely diagnostic of stronger stimulus–response habit.** Before attributing it to habit, establish that the person learned the action–outcome relation, noticed or encoded the change, experienced an effective value/contingency manipulation, could respond and inhibit appropriately, and was tested under conditions that distinguish early automatic response preparation from later goal-directed correction. Test reliability, construct validity, and generalization separately.

Conversely, successful adjustment does not prove that no learned stimulus–response tendency exists; goal-directed control may override that tendency.

## 1. Define goal-directed instrumental control operationally

An action is goal-directed in the associative action-control framework when its selection is sensitive to both:
- represented causal action–outcome relation (R–O); and
- the current value of the expected outcome.

A response that reduces appropriately after independent outcome devaluation or contingency degradation supports goal-directed involvement **in that task and test condition**.

This is not equivalent to conscious deliberation on every trial or full model-based planning in a complex RL model.

## 2. Define the classical instrumental habit

Classical S–R habit: antecedent stimulus/context directly evokes a practiced response with relatively weak dependence on the current value or causal efficacy of its consequence at response selection.

The usual *negative* operational marker is reduced sensitivity to outcome devaluation and/or action–outcome contingency degradation after training.

**Insensitivity is evidence requiring exclusion of confounds, not a direct recording of an S–R synapse.**

## 3. Learning versus expression

Different questions:
- Acquisition: Did repeated S–R–O experience strengthen an automatic response tendency?
- Expression: Did it govern the observed choice under present time pressure, attention, goals, and available response preparation?
- Arbitration/competition: Which controller won on this trial?
- Measurement: Does the task actually isolate those constructs?

An acquired tendency may remain latent when goal-directed control suppresses or redirects the response. An observed error may arise without any increase in habitual learning if goal-directed control failed.

## 4. Outcome devaluation logic

Train action R to produce outcome O. Separately lower the current value of O (e.g. selective satiety, aversive revaluation, or explicit reward-value change). Test responding, often without immediate reinforcement to avoid fresh feedback learning.

A goal-directed agent with intact R–O knowledge and effective revaluation should reduce responding for devalued O while retaining valued-outcome responding.

A persistently high R rate is **compatible with** habit, but cannot establish it unless O was actually devalued for that agent and the test was understood.

## 5. Outcome-devaluation implementation confounds

Check:
1. Did the manipulation *actually* change the subjective or task-effective value of O?
2. Was devaluation selective relative to a valued control?
3. Was outcome identity and the R–O mapping encoded and retained?
4. Did participants understand whether learned reward value should be updated at test?
5. Could satiety, response fatigue, instruction confusion, reward collection motives, or generalized extinction alter responding?
6. Were reward availability, task framing, and response inhibition comparable?
7. Was a no-feedback/extinction probe used appropriately to assess learned control before corrective feedback?
8. Is the scoring sensitive to baseline performance and response opportunities?

**2026 decisive warning:** a preregistered conceptual replication with strengthened devaluation found that much apparently habit-like responding tracked *ineffective devaluation*, not amount of prior training; reanalysis supported this association across earlier datasets (Martínez-López et al., 2026, Behavior Research Methods). Therefore verify devaluation effectiveness before assigning habit status.

## 6. Contingency degradation logic

The relevant instrumental contingency is the relative causal advantage of responding:

`ΔP = P(O | R) − P(O | no R)`.

A contingency can be degraded by raising background/noncontingent outcome delivery, reducing response-contingent outcomes, or reversing the efficacy of responding. These are *not identical manipulations*.

Goal-directed action should adapt when the causal benefit of R decreases; habit-like responding may persist. Interpretation requires checking whether the learner perceived the contingency and whether the environment supplied enough evidence to update.

Do **not** conflate learned R–O contingency degradation with instruction-based response remapping or reversal: these can involve different task representations.

## 7. Human contingency-degradation results constrain the method

In three preregistered experiments, a 2025 study contrasted more versus less trained stimulus–response–outcome mappings over three days and tested degraded contingency. The overtrained condition did **not** show the predicted increase in habitual responding.

This is evidence that a particular human degradation protocol failed to detect a training-induced habit shift; it is **not** evidence that humans cannot form habits.

Conclusion: do not use human contingency-degradation failure as a one-step trait or clinical marker.

## 8. Overtraining is a manipulation, not a diagnosis

Classic animal work sometimes shows a transition toward devaluation-insensitive responding after extended instrumental practice, but the result depends on schedule, context, response–outcome structure, and test.

In humans:
- Tricomi et al. (2009) provided an influential initial demonstration;
- de Wit et al. (2018) reported five failed attempts to show overtraining-induced inflexibility in variants of established tasks;
- later studies vary by task and probe, with some evidence under response-time constraints.

Thus **more repetitions -> demonstrably stronger human habit** is not a universal empirical rule. Repetition is a *candidate cause* and experimental manipulation whose effect must be established.

## 9. Time pressure reveals an expression window

Hardwick et al. (2019) showed rapidly prepared, practiced responses can compete with later goal-directed responses. If given more preparation time, goal-directed correction can replace early habitual preparation.

Therefore:
- fast early wrong action can reveal automatic response bias;
- slower correct action can reveal goal-directed override;
- single unstratified response accuracy can obscure both.

Do not equate short reaction time alone with habit; include congruent/incongruent controls, speed–accuracy analyses, response preparation limits, and training manipulation.

## 10. Early response preparation differs from response initiation

Recent evidence distinguishes habit-like biases in **response preparation** and **response initiation**; the two may not be captured by the same test (2025 Nature Human Behaviour).

The timing of an automatic tendency, a commission error, and the final executed action are different dependent variables. A task showing a fast tendency can still show successful final control.

## 11. 2026 evidence for context-dependent early action slips

A 2026 multiexperiment study ("Naturally disengaging control to reveal habits") used hierarchical trials, self-correction opportunity, and switches between extensively and minimally practiced behaviors. Overtraining shifted early slips toward the overpracticed context while later responses were more goal-directed.

Its claimed mechanism is that task design can reveal competing automatic response tendencies without simply forcing an earlier deadline. This is promising **task- and timing-specific evidence**, not a universal quick laboratory test or stable individual trait estimate.

## 12. Time-pressure paradigms require convergent validation

Martínez-López et al. (2025/2026 publication record, PMID 41286251) compared a forced-response paradigm and an outcome-devaluation/"aliens" response-time switch-cost paradigm after multiday training. Both showed training-related patterns and acceptable reliability within their respective measures, yet **the two measures did not correlate**.

Therefore:
- reliable within-paradigm response scores ≠ convergent validity;
- the measures may index different components of action control;
- no single metric should be marketed as the general "habit strength" parameter.

## 13. Devaluation and response remapping are not interchangeable

A 2026 matched time-pressure comparison reported divergent findings from outcome devaluation and response remapping after six days of training. Each method loads distinct representational and response-control demands.

A mapping conflict demonstrates persistence/competition of practiced responses; it does not automatically show insensitivity to outcome value. Conversely, devaluation sensitivity measures value-guided adjustment under its manipulation.

Preserve this difference in outcome claims.

## 14. Habituality, automaticity, frequency, and skill

Distinguish:
- **Frequency:** how often behavior occurs.
- **Repetition:** practice exposure.
- **Automaticity:** low apparent effort, fast cue-linked initiation, diminished deliberative involvement.
- **Skill:** efficient and accurate performance after practice.
- **Routine:** regular contextual sequence.
- **S–R habit:** learned cue–response tendency relatively insensitive to current outcome value/contingency.

These properties can overlap but do not entail one another. Highly practiced goal-directed skill can be automatic; repeated action can remain outcome-sensitive.

## 15. Naturalistic self-report measures

Self-Report Habit Index (SRHI) and Self-Report Behavioral Automaticity Index (SRBAI) target perceived automaticity/habit-related features in stable contexts. Frequency and context stability can help predict routine behavior.

But retrospective reports:
- require introspective access to automatic processes;
- can mix past frequency with current intention and opportunity;
- cannot directly establish devaluation-insensitive S–R control.

Health-behavior habit-formation meta-analyses based on these questionnaires answer a **different question** from experimental instrumental S–R habit induction. Never pool the two constructs uncritically.

## 16. Habit does not mean persistence under every challenge

Bouton's *Habit and persistence* review examines whether devaluation-defined habits are necessarily resistant to extinction, contingency change, context shift, relapse, or permanent change.

One operational insensitivity does not establish permanent, context-independent, or unstoppable behavior.

Do not assume:
- habit = irreversibility;
- habit = compulsion;
- habit = global loss of agency;
- habit = resistance to all intervention;
- extinction persistence = a valid generic habit measure.

## 17. Context and arbitration

Stimulus/context cues can activate practiced responses; current goals, inferred task state, memory, and response-control resources can modify their expression.

Competition is not necessarily an exclusive switch between two anatomically isolated systems. Multiple control processes may run in parallel and at different latencies.

Observed performance must separate:
- automatic activation/preparation;
- valuation/contingency representation;
- executive response correction;
- environmental opportunity and task demands.

A "strong habit" estimate extracted from one context is not a domain-general property without external validation.

## 18. Distinguish habit from related clinical or moral labels

Habitual ≠ impulsive ≠ compulsive ≠ addictive ≠ morally bad ≠ unconscious. Compulsion and clinical symptoms require independent constructs and assessment.

The 2024 *Bad habits–good goals?* meta-analysis discusses translation difficulties and weak separation between some behavioral measures and psychopathology, underscoring the need to avoid clinical overinterpretation.

**No individualized diagnosis or psychiatric mechanism follows from a laboratory habit score.**

## 19. Neural evidence boundary

Corticostriatal circuits are associated with habit/goal-directed control, with compelling causal animal experiments and correlational human work. But neural activation/connectivity does not uniquely identify a controller governing a given choice.

Especially after LEA-03: dopamine/RL fit/neural MF signals ≠ behavioral proof of S–R habit.

Claim neural mechanism only at the level supported by causal manipulations, temporal resolution, and competing accounts.

## 20. Human measurement validity hierarchy

A plausible instrument should establish **separately**:
1. construct/operational definition and target controller;
2. manipulation check (devaluation or contingency detection);
3. positive control for R–O learning and valued responding;
4. negative/control conditions for fatigue, motor bias, confusion, and simple perseveration;
5. training-dose manipulation with appropriate comparison;
6. speed/preparation-time tests when automatic capture may be masked;
7. task/model discrimination against explicit knowledge, WM, proactive control, and rule memorization;
8. repeatability and parameter/score recovery where applicable;
9. convergent and discriminant validity across different paradigms;
10. ecologically meaningful prediction if claiming real-world habit.

Good internal consistency or test–retest correlation alone does not substitute for the other criteria.

## 21. What supports and what does not support an inference

| Observation | Warranted interpretation | Not warranted alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Response falls after valid selective devaluation | Goal-directed value sensitivity under test | No underlying habitual tendency exists |
| Response persists after devaluation | Possible value-insensitive response | S–R mechanism established without manipulation check |
| Response persists after contingency degradation | Possible reduced causal sensitivity | Habit if contingency awareness/unlearning not checked |
| Extended training | More exposure/practice | Automatic transition to habit |
| Quick practiced response | Efficient/rapid response | Habit as defined by outcome insensitivity |
| Early slip, later correction | Competing time-dependent response tendencies | Single stable habit/goal-directed trait |
| High questionnaire automaticity | Subjective cue-linked automaticity | Laboratory S–R/devaluation-resistant habit |
| High model-free fit | Computational task-specific signal | Clinically meaningful habit strength |
| Brain activation | Neural correlate of measured processes | Exclusive causal control of behavior |

## 22. Habit Inference Gate — 48 questions

### A. Target and terminology
1. What exact behavior and cue/context are studied?
2. Does "habit" mean S–R outcome-insensitivity, perceived automaticity, a routine, or a learned skill?
3. Is the outcome value defined and measurable?
4. Is action–outcome causal knowledge assessable?

### B. Training and acquisition
5. Was response–outcome learning demonstrated?
6. Was practice duration/trial count characterized?
7. Was the training schedule and reward probability controlled?
8. Was there a short-training comparison?
9. Does overtraining actually change the targeted measure?
10. Could increased practice improve goal knowledge as well?

### C. Outcome devaluation
11. What outcome was revalued and how?
12. Did the manipulation change experienced/subjective reward value?
13. Was value change selective rather than generalized?
14. Does the participant know which response obtains which outcome?
15. Were instructions understood?
16. Was a valued-outcome control retained?
17. Could devaluation failure produce a false habit classification?
18. Could extinction, fatigue, or response-inhibition demands confound the test?

### D. Contingency degradation
19. Which quantity changes: P(O|R), P(O|no R), or both?
20. Is the contingency causal rather than merely correlated?
21. Was the change noticed/learnable?
22. Was a contingency-awareness control administered?
23. Did enough postchange experience occur for adaptive revision?
24. Is response-remapping being mistaken for causal degradation?

### E. Timing and controllers
25. Was response preparation time manipulated?
26. Were early action slips distinguished from final choices?
27. Could goal-directed control override a prepared response?
28. Were response preparation and initiation separated?
29. Were congruent/incongruent trials compared?
30. Was speed–accuracy tradeoff modeled?
31. Did task instructions themselves increase goal-directed scrutiny?
32. Could the same data arise from motor bias/perseveration?

### F. Measurement
33. Is the test reliable under repetition?
34. Is the score valid for the intended construct?
35. Are manipulation-check failures excluded or modeled?
36. Does the measure respond to a graded practice manipulation?
37. Is convergent validity established across distinct habit tests?
38. Is discriminant validity established against general executive ability?
39. Does it predict behavior outside the specific task?
40. Are individual differences stable and interpretable?

### G. Computation and biology
41. Is model-free computation being conflated with habit?
42. Does neural signal encoding demonstrate behavioral control?
43. Are R–O, S–R, WM, state inference, and perseveration alternative models considered?
44. Is the claim about mechanism stronger than the available causal evidence?

### H. Transfer and individual inference
45. Is the paradigm animal, laboratory human, or naturalistic self-report?
46. Is the studied task context comparable to the target setting?
47. Are "habit", "impulsive", "compulsive", and "addictive" kept distinct?
48. What limited, falsifiable conclusion survives the alternatives?

## 23. Failure modes

A. Repetition -> habit: **invalid without demonstrated control transition**.

B. Fast -> habit: **invalid without evidence of outcome-insensitive action selection or independently validated automatic response capture**.

C. Automaticity -> S–R habit: **construct substitution**.

D. Failed devaluation test -> habit: **invalid when devaluation did not actually work**.

E. No response to degraded contingency -> habit: **invalid without contingency awareness and adequate learning opportunity**.

F. Successful goal-directed choice -> habit absent: **invalid; masking/override remains possible**.

G. Overtraining null result -> humans lack habits: **invalid; task sensitivity/expression conditions remain**.

H. Under-time-pressure error -> permanent habit: **invalid; expression window is context-dependent**.

I. Two reliable measures -> same latent construct: **invalid absent convergence**.

J. MF computation -> habit: **invalid categorical equivalence**.

K. Habit -> compulsion/addiction/clinical symptom: **invalid diagnostic leap**.

L. Habit -> unchangeable/sticky under all contexts: **invalid overgeneralization**.

## Canonical working rule

**Training dose -> identify learned R–O knowledge -> verify value/contingency manipulation -> observe early and late responding -> test confounds and alternative controllers -> establish measurement reliability AND validity -> infer only task-conditional habit expression.**

Never: **repetition/speed/automaticity/MF score/devaluation error -> universal habit trait**.
