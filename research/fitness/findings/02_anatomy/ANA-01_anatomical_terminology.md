## 1. Международный стандарт

FIPAT/IFAA maintains the international standard anatomical nomenclature.

Current official framework:
**Terminologia Anatomica, second edition (TA2)**:
- published online in 2019;
- approved/adopted by IFAA General Assembly in 2020;
- maintained as the international terminology for adult human anatomy.

FIPAT provides:
- official Latin term;
- UK/US English equivalents;
- accepted synonyms;
- related/non-preferred terms.

## 2. Anatomical position

The reference position makes directional terms invariant.

Standard concept:
1. body upright;
2. head/eyes facing forward;
3. upper limbs by sides;
4. palms facing anteriorly;
5. lower limbs/feet oriented forward in the standard stance.

Exact illustrations/textbook stance widths vary slightly; the critical reference is orientation, especially palms anteriorly.

Why this matters:
in anatomical position:
- radius is lateral to ulna;
- thumb is lateral;
- palm is anterior;
- forearm is supinated.

Movement is described relative to this reference even if actual exercise begins elsewhere.

## 3. Median plane vs sagittal planes

### Median (midsagittal) plane
Single vertical plane through body midline dividing it into right and left halves.

### Sagittal plane
Any vertical plane parallel to median plane, dividing into right/left portions.

Terminology caution:
“midsagittal” = median;
“parasagittal” = parallel but off-midline.

## 4. Frontal / coronal plane

Vertical plane perpendicular to sagittal plane.

Divides body into:
- anterior;
- posterior portions.

Synonyms:
- frontal plane;
- coronal plane.

For project, use **frontal (coronal)** at first mention.

## 5. Transverse / horizontal / axial plane

Plane perpendicular to longitudinal body axis.

Divides into:
- superior;
- inferior portions.

Common equivalents in imaging:
- transverse;
- axial;
- cross-sectional.

## 6. Oblique plane

Any plane not aligned exactly with standard sagittal/frontal/transverse planes.

Important practical point:
real multi-joint movement often occurs in oblique/multiplanar paths.

Planes are coordinate/reference tools, not a claim that human movements are confined to geometric sheets.

## 7. Axes

A movement in a plane is conventionally described as rotation around an axis perpendicular to that plane.

### Sagittal-plane motion
around mediolateral (frontal/transverse) axis.

Typical:
- flexion;
- extension.

### Frontal-plane motion
around anteroposterior axis.

Typical:
- abduction;
- adduction;
- lateral flexion.

### Transverse-plane motion
around longitudinal/superior-inferior axis.

Typical:
- medial/lateral rotation;
- trunk/head rotation.

Project warning:
the **joint’s actual instantaneous axis** can move and need not equal a simple textbook global axis. Detailed joint mechanics belongs to MEC.

## 8. Directional terms

### Superior / inferior
- superior = toward head;
- inferior = toward feet.

### Anterior / posterior
- anterior = toward front;
- posterior = toward back.

### Medial / lateral
- medial = toward median plane;
- lateral = away from median plane.

### Proximal / distal
Primarily limbs/tubular structures:
- proximal = nearer origin/attachment/trunk;
- distal = farther.

### Superficial / deep
Relative to body surface.

### Intermediate
Between medial/lateral or other described structures where relevant.

### Ipsilateral / contralateral
- ipsilateral = same side;
- contralateral = opposite side.

### Bilateral / unilateral
- bilateral = both sides;
- unilateral = one side.

## 9. Limb-specific directional terms

Upper limb:
- radial = toward radius/thumb side;
- ulnar = toward ulna/little-finger side.

Lower limb:
- tibial = toward tibia/medial side;
- fibular = toward fibula/lateral side.

Hand:
- palmar/volar side;
- dorsal side.

Foot:
- plantar;
- dorsal.

These can be clearer than medial/lateral when limb orientation changes.

## 10. Flexion and extension

General convention:
- flexion decreases angle between relevant segments / bends;
- extension increases angle / straightens.

Typical sagittal-plane pair.

Important exceptions/nuances:
1. shoulder/hip flexion/extension defined relative to anatomical position rather than merely “angle decreases”;
2. trunk flexion/extension follows directional bending;
3. knee flexion moves lower leg posteriorly despite generic “forward bend” intuitions.

Project rule:
use joint-specific anatomical definition, not simplistic angle heuristic when ambiguous.

## 11. Hyperextension

Extension beyond anatomical/neutral reference range.

Term describes motion/range, not pathology by itself.

Do not label all hyperextension dangerous.

Safety depends on:
- joint;
- load;
- capacity;
- context.

## 12. Abduction and adduction

General:
- abduction = away from median plane;
- adduction = toward median plane.

Typical frontal-plane movements.

### Hand/fingers exception
Reference axis is usually middle digit.

### Foot/toes
Reference axes are local to foot/toes.

Thus “away from body midline” is not universally sufficient for digits.

## 13. Medial/internal and lateral/external rotation

Rotation around longitudinal axis of limb.

- medial/internal rotation: anterior surface turns toward median direction;
- lateral/external rotation: away.

At shoulder/hip:
widely used.

Project terminology:
prefer **medial/lateral rotation**, with internal/external as common equivalents.

## 14. Forearm pronation and supination

These are specific radioulnar movements.

### Supination
radius and ulna are relatively parallel; in anatomical position palm faces anteriorly.

### Pronation
radius crosses over ulna; palm turns posteriorly in anatomical position.

Important:
this is **not** simply “rotate wrist”.

Primary motion occurs at proximal/distal radioulnar joints.

For elbow flexed ~90°:
- supination commonly described palm up;
- pronation palm down.

This context difference explains why “palm up/down” is convenient but not the formal universal definition.

## 15. Wrist movements

1. flexion;
2. extension;
3. radial deviation;
4. ulnar deviation.

Radial/ulnar deviation correspond to movement toward respective forearm bone side.

“Wrist abduction/adduction” may be used synonymously in some sources, but radial/ulnar deviation is clearer in training context.

## 16. Ankle/foot terms

### Dorsiflexion
dorsum of foot moves toward anterior leg / ankle angle closes anteriorly.

### Plantarflexion
foot moves toward plantar direction, as in pressing a pedal/standing on toes.

### Inversion
sole turns medially.

### Eversion
sole turns laterally.

Actual foot mechanics can involve multiple joints/planes; detailed subtalar mechanics belongs to MEC.

## 17. Scapular movements

For training, standard vocabulary:

1. elevation;
2. depression;
3. protraction;
4. retraction;
5. upward rotation;
6. downward rotation;
7. anterior/posterior tilting;
8. internal/external rotation of scapula in 3D descriptions.

Important:
“protract shoulders” and “move humerus forward” are different.

Scapula and humerus must be described separately.

## 18. Elevation and depression

- elevation = move superiorly;
- depression = move inferiorly.

Common:
- scapula;
- mandible (with context).

## 19. Protraction and retraction

Commonly used for scapula/mandible.

Scapular:
- protraction = scapula moves around thorax anterolaterally;
- retraction = posteromedially toward spine.

Not identical to shoulder joint horizontal movement.

## 20. Circumduction

Composite sequential motion combining:
- flexion;
- abduction;
- extension;
- adduction
(or analogous sequence).

Distal segment traces cone-like path.

Not an independent single-axis rotation.

## 21. Opposition and reposition

Thumb opposition:
complex movement bringing thumb pad toward finger pads, involving multiple planes/joints.

Reposition:
return.

Relevant if grip/hand function later researched.

## 22. Lateral flexion

Side-bending of:
- neck;
- trunk.

Primarily frontal-plane description.

Do not call it “abduction of spine” in ordinary training terminology.

## 23. Rotation of trunk/head

Left/right rotation around longitudinal axis.

Need specify segment:
- cervical;
- thoracic;
- whole trunk.

“Twist” is colloquial; project uses rotation.

## 24. Horizontal abduction/adduction

At shoulder when humerus is elevated roughly to transverse level:
- horizontal adduction = move across anteriorly;
- horizontal abduction = move posteriorly away.

These are transverse-plane descriptions.

Useful for:
- fly;
- reverse fly;
- horizontal press/pull analysis.

## 25. Open issue: scapular-plane movement

Shoulder elevation often occurs in scapular plane rather than strict frontal plane.

This is an oblique plane related to scapular orientation.

Project implication:
textbook plane labels are reference categories; real exercise trajectories may be deliberately oblique.

Detailed scapular plane and shoulder kinematics → ANA-04/MEC.

## 26. Position vs movement

Do not confuse:
- joint position;
- direction of movement.

Example:
forearm can be **pronated** (position) while elbow is **flexing** (movement).

Similarly:
shoulder can be externally rotated while abducting.

Project descriptions should separate:
1. starting position;
2. movement;
3. final position.

## 27. Global vs local coordinate system

Global:
body reference planes.

Local:
segment/joint coordinate axes.

In biomechanics, joint rotations may be defined in local coordinate systems and 3D sequences.

Anatomy file uses simplified canonical language; MEC will handle coordinate-system complexity.

## 28. Training-description template

For any exercise movement:

1. body segment/joint;
2. starting position;
3. plane (approximate if useful);
4. axis (if needed);
5. primary movement term;
6. forearm/grip orientation;
7. scapular movement separately;
8. ROM endpoints.

Example:
“elbow flexion with forearm supinated” is much more precise than “curl hand upward”.

## 29. Common terminology traps

1. “Wrist rotation” when actual motion is forearm pronation/supination.
2. “Shoulder retraction” when meaning scapular retraction.
3. “Internal/external rotation” without naming joint.
4. “Abduction” without local reference for fingers/toes.
5. “Sagittal movement” as if joint cannot move obliquely.
6. “Anatomical plane” confused with actual joint surface plane.
7. “Hyperextension” automatically labeled pathological.
8. “Neutral grip” confused with anatomical-position supination.

## 30. Grip terminology for later training

Not formal anatomy category, but project operational definitions:

### Supinated grip
palms generally face anterior/up depending limb position.

### Pronated grip
palms generally face posterior/down.

### Neutral grip
palms face each other; forearm approximately midway between full pronation/supination.

Important:
exact forearm rotation angle varies with exercise/individual.

Thus “neutral = exactly 90°” is unnecessary precision.

## 31. Project standard

Use:
- anatomical position as reference;
- medial/lateral rather than vague “inside/outside”;
- proximal/distal for limbs;
- flexion/extension;
- abduction/adduction;
- medial/lateral rotation;
- pronation/supination specifically for forearm;
- radial/ulnar deviation for wrist;
- dorsiflexion/plantarflexion;
- inversion/eversion;
- explicit scapular movement.

## 32. Confidence

### HIGH
- TA2/FIPAT is current IFAA international anatomical terminology framework;
- anatomical position/planes/directional terms are standardized foundational conventions;
- pronation/supination are forearm/radioulnar movements;
- planes/axes are reference conventions and real movement can be multiplanar.

- exact wording in English/Russian varies between teaching traditions; project uses TA2-compatible concepts and clear operational terms.
