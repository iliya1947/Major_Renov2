## 1. Question and baseline

How can an analyst separate appraisal, experienced emotion, expression, action tendency, regulation attempt and subsequent behavior, including co-occurring opposing emotions? How can emotional changes be distinguished from changed expression, repeated exposure and regulation-related feedback?
Competing accounts: changed appraisal or goal relevance; mixed emotional experience; alternating states; display norms or deliberate modulation; spontaneous change; new events; observer interpretation; measurement or timing artifacts. Multiple accounts can coexist.
Reviewed AFF-01–04 and STR-03 queue summaries, plus selected passages AFF-01 §§12–16, 21; AFF-02 §§14–16, 19, 21; AFF-03 §§11–14, 30. Not a full rereading of canonical files. IND-02.1 handoff and existing construct distinctions retained.

Access: indexed publisher abstract, selected methods and discussion excerpts; direct publisher/APS opening failed. Many single-item retrospective measures within prompts; pooled population and partially pooled individual estimates do not uniquely identify one person's policy. Hossain, Kyron, Demarree & Naragon-Gainey (2025), Emotion Regulation in Daily Life: Testing Bidirectional Temporal Associations With Positive and Negative Affect. https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0001496 ; https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/emotion-regulation-in-daily-life-testing-bidirectional-temporal-a/

Do not interpret concurrent negative emotion during regulation as evidence that regulation caused it.

## 3. Integrated model: emotion as a temporal process

Distinguish event/contact, appraisal (meaning relative to goals, certainty, control and norms), subjective experience, physiology, action tendency, intended action, expressive behavior, regulation goal, strategy use, outcome and observer interpretation. These are related but not interchangeable. Emotion and appraisal can affect goal valuation and action; progress and consequences can change subsequent appraisal and emotion (IND-02.1). A conceptual feedback map is not an identified causal model.

Mixed affect: opposite-valence components in a specified time window; distinguish simultaneous experience, rapid alternation, evaluations of an object (attitudinal ambivalence), conflict between goals and felt emotional conflict. The latter constructs may coincide but none proves another. An aggregate average near neutral can hide strong opposite feelings or alternating states. Identify target/object, time resolution and measurement; neither a contradiction between actions nor a low positive-negative correlation uniquely establishes mixed affect.

Expression: recorded outward signal; low expression can be consistent with low experience, display constraints, regulation, another communication channel or limited observation. Suppression is a specific attempt to inhibit expression, not every instance of low expressivity. Suppression and expression can coexist or vary by emotion. Do not assume hidden emotion simply because expression is limited; do not equate visible intensity with experienced intensity or sincerity. Self-report is useful evidence with its own limits, not an infallible ground truth. Emotional experience is neither a commitment nor consent.

Regulation: identification of a regulation goal, strategy selection, implementation and monitoring. Separate strategy use from success, and immediate affect from later outcomes and relational consequences. Strategy choice can reflect initial distress; concurrent associations are vulnerable to selection/confounding. Goal and context matter, but no universally deterministic context-to-strategy mapping. Spontaneous change is not necessarily regulation.

Dynamics: baseline, event exposure, reactivity, recovery, recurrence and sampling interval are separate. Persistent negative affect may reflect repeated events, stronger responses or slower recovery. Observed time series and lag associations do not prove the causal direction; synchrony is not sufficient evidence of interpersonal co-regulation. Avoid treating a short-term state improvement as durable motive change or relationship outcome.

## 4. Practical inference sequence

1. Define the emotional proposition, its target, time interval and intended use; distinguish feelings from behavior, goals and decisions.
2. Separate directly observed action or expression, self-report, contextual facts and inferred internal states. Check opportunity and missingness.
3. Compare appraisal hypotheses and plausible alternative explanations rather than applying a fixed emotion dictionary. Preserve contradictory observations and independent evidence.
4. Assess co-occurrence versus alternation with appropriate timing; do not infer a latent mixed state from inconsistent behavior alone.
5. If regulation is proposed, specify its target, purpose and evidence of attempted use; check initial affect, selection, strategy alternatives, immediate and later outcomes. Keep emotional state and expression changes separate.
6. Trace events and feedback across time, including changing goals and constraints. Do not infer causal influence from temporal order, or extrapolate group estimates to individual certainty.
7. Report the narrowest supported proposition, what would distinguish alternatives, limitations and revision triggers. Use ordinary consensual observations/clarification; no covert emotional tests, inducement of distress or manipulation.

## 5. Practice and external evidence challenge

Generic invented examples unrelated to private material. Rubric: construct distinction, plausible competing explanation, evidence limit and discriminating observation. No independent examiner or blinded task.

A. A ceramic artist reports enjoyment of an exhibition and sadness about the closure of the venue during the same moment. Co-occurring opposite affect is supported by the report; alternative time-window ambiguity if retrospective. Clarify timing/targets and do not infer a decision or stable character.
B. A lecturer's voice remains steady while reporting fear before a presentation. Separate experience and vocal expression; practiced delivery, situational norms and deliberate suppression are alternatives. Report the contrast without asserting that suppression caused it.
C. A runner reports using distraction during the most frustrating sessions; those sessions end with more frustration. Strategy selection by initial distress is a competing explanation. Compare starting levels, timing, relevant contexts and subsequent affect; no conclusion that distraction worsens emotion from this association.
D. Synthetic time-window test: sequence 1 has positive and negative ratings (4,0) then (0,4); sequence 2 has (2,2) twice. Both have mean (2,2), only sequence 2 records co-occurrence at each assessment. This arithmetic illustration is not a validated measurement threshold and cannot resolve processes within each sampling interval.

External empirical challenge: E4's limited support for predicted strategy selection prevents using intensity/control/social setting as a deterministic rule. E5 supports checking feedback and initial affect before inferring a strategy effect. E2's method-dependent estimates prevent treating one mixed-emotion index as a universal ground truth. These are retrospective comparisons with already-inspected findings, not independent or prospective tests.
