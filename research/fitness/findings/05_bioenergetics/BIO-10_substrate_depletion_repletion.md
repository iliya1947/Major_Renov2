# BIO-10 — Substrate depletion and repletion

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-19**  
Область: 05 — Bioenergetics / Exercise Metabolism

## 0. Core decision

Exercise does not deplete one shared “fuel tank”.

The relevant substrate pools differ in:

- anatomical compartment;
- physiological role;
- rate and pattern of use;
- repletion pathway;
- nutritional dependence;
- measurement method;
- recovery kinetics.

Project rule:

**substrate depletion/repletion is pool specific; no single substrate marker or recovery duration defines total energetic recovery.**

---

## 1. PCr depletion and recovery

PCr can fall rapidly during high-intensity work and supports rapid ATP buffering.

Its postexercise resynthesis is primarily supported by oxidative ATP production.

Recovery kinetics depend on:

- degree of depletion;
- end-exercise pH/metabolic state;
- muscle and task;
- oxygen delivery/oxidative capacity;
- age/health;
- acquisition and modeling method.

Project rule:

**PCr usually recovers rapidly on a minutes scale, but there is no universal “fully restored after X minutes” rule.**

PCr depletion is not equivalent to ATP depletion or a complete fatigue diagnosis.

---

## 2. PCr and nutrition

Acute carbohydrate feeding is not a direct PCr-replacement process in the way carbohydrate availability supports glycogen restoration.

Project rule:

**PCr repletion ≠ carbohydrate refilling.**

Creatine supplementation and chronic total-creatine-store manipulation belong NUT.

---

## 3. Muscle glycogen depletion

Muscle glycogen is a local carbohydrate store whose use depends on:

- intensity;
- duration;
- total work/set structure;
- recruited muscle/fibres;
- starting glycogen;
- training status;
- carbohydrate availability;
- environmental context.

Project rule:

**muscle glycogen depletion is task-, recruitment- and muscle-specific rather than one fixed percentage per workout.**

---

## 4. Resistance exercise and glycogen

Resistance exercise can materially reduce local muscle glycogen.

Current pooled evidence supports an average decline but with:

- substantial protocol heterogeneity;
- wide prediction intervals;
- concentration of evidence in vastus lateralis;
- predominantly male study samples.

Project rule:

**resistance exercise can deplete muscle glycogen, but the magnitude cannot be assigned from “resistance training” as a category alone.**

---

## 5. Glycogen heterogeneity

Whole-muscle glycogen concentration is an average.

Different:

- muscles;
- fibres;
- recruited motor units;
- subcellular glycogen pools

can show different depletion.

Project rule:

**one whole-muscle glycogen value ≠ uniform glycogen status of every fibre or subcellular pool.**

Low glycogen does not imply zero glycogen.

Detailed glycogen-fatigue causality belongs BIO-11.

---

## 6. Carbohydrate during exercise

Exogenous carbohydrate can reduce net muscle-glycogen utilization in some endurance/high-intensity contexts.

The pooled average effect is small and protocol-specific effects can be much larger.

Project rule:

**carbohydrate ingestion can spare muscle glycogen, but no universal glycogen-sparing percentage is retained.**

Exact fueling strategy belongs NUT.

---

## 7. Muscle glycogen repletion

Rapid muscle-glycogen restoration depends strongly on carbohydrate availability after depletion.

However recovery depends on:

- initial depletion;
- carbohydrate/energy availability;
- exercise mode;
- muscle damage;
- training state;
- recovery duration.

Project rule:

**adequate carbohydrate supports glycogen restoration, but no fixed intake guarantees complete muscle-glycogen recovery by a universal hour.**

---

## 8. Carbohydrate timing

Early carbohydrate availability can increase short-window glycogen-storage rate.

When total 24-h carbohydrate is matched, delayed intake can sometimes produce similar 24-h muscle glycogen despite different next-day performance.

Project rule:

**carbohydrate timing can matter when the recovery window is short, but timing is not the sole determinant of 24-h glycogen status or next-session performance.**

Exact timing prescription belongs NUT.

---

## 9. Repletion vs supercompensation

Repletion means restoration toward the prior state.

Supercompensation means storage above the prior/baseline state.

Project rule:

**glycogen repletion ≠ glycogen supercompensation.**

Carbohydrate-loading prescription belongs NUT.

---

## 10. Liver glycogen

Liver glycogen is distinct from muscle glycogen.

Its exercise role includes supporting hepatic glucose output and systemic blood-glucose homeostasis.

Project rule:

**muscle glycogen and liver glycogen are separate stores with different roles and potentially different depletion/repletion kinetics.**

---

## 11. Liver vs muscle glycogen recovery

Under some carbohydrate-fed recovery conditions, liver glycogen can recover faster than muscle glycogen.

That is not a universal ordering law.

Project rule:

**liver glycogen can recover faster than muscle glycogen in some protocols; it is not assumed always to do so.**

---

## 12. Liver glycogen concentration vs content

Liver volume can change concurrently with measured glycogen concentration.

Therefore concentration and total organ glycogen content can move differently.

Project rule:

**liver glycogen concentration ≠ total liver glycogen content when liver volume changes.**

---

## 13. Intramyocellular lipid terminology

Intramyocellular lipid storage is a real exercise substrate pool, but terminology and measurement matter.

Operationally distinguish:

- IMTG/IMTAG — intramyocellular triacylglycerol storage;
- IMCL — commonly an MRS/imaging-oriented intramyocellular-lipid construct;
- extramyocellular/intermuscular adipose tissue — separate from intramyocellular stores.

Project rule:

**IMTG and IMCL are related constructs, not perfectly interchangeable measurement labels.**

---

## 14. Intramyocellular lipid depletion

Prolonged endurance exercise can substantially reduce intramyocellular lipid, particularly in type-I fibres.

But severe intermittent high-intensity exercise does not necessarily cause net depletion.

Measurement technique can materially alter the apparent effect size.

Project rule:

**intramyocellular-lipid use is fibre-, mode- and method-dependent.**

---

## 15. Intramyocellular lipid repletion

Recovery can differ markedly by task:

- within hours in some resistance-exercise settings;
- around 24 h in some prolonged-endurance settings;
- up to ~48 h in other endurance/diet protocols.

Dietary fat availability can influence restoration after prolonged endurance exercise.

Project rule:

**there is no universal IMTG/IMCL recovery clock.**

Exact dietary-fat prescription belongs NUT.

---

## 16. Whole-body fat oxidation

Elevated whole-body fat oxidation does not identify which lipid depot supplied the oxidized fat.

Project rule:

**whole-body fat oxidation ≠ net IMTG utilization.**

---

## 17. Blood glucose

Blood glucose is not a large storage depot analogous to glycogen.

It is a regulated circulating concentration produced by the balance among:

- hepatic glucose output;
- exogenous glucose appearance;
- tissue uptake/disposal;
- hormonal control.

Project rule:

**blood glucose concentration ≠ glucose store.**

---

## 18. Blood glucose concentration vs turnover

Blood glucose can remain relatively stable while glucose appearance and disposal are both high.

Project rule:

**stable blood glucose concentration ≠ low glucose turnover.**

Tracer kinetics are required to separate appearance and disposal flux.

---

## 19. Hypoglycemia boundary

Exercise-induced hypoglycemia can occur and can matter for endurance performance.

But maintained blood glucose does not guarantee unlimited performance, and BIO-10 does not assign one universal fatigue mechanism.

Project rule:

**hypoglycemia is not canonized here as the universal sole cause of endurance fatigue.**

Detailed causal fatigue architecture belongs BIO-11.

---

## 20. Recovery-time architecture

The project does not retain one universal recovery clock.

### PCr
Often minutes-scale; exact kinetics are state/muscle/method dependent.

### Blood glucose
A dynamically regulated concentration/flux, not a depot with one repletion curve.

### Liver glycogen
Can change substantially over hours and can recover rapidly under some carbohydrate-fed conditions.

### Muscle glycogen
Can require many hours and may remain below baseline at 12 h or longer after large depletion despite high carbohydrate availability.

### Intramyocellular lipid
Can recover within hours in some tasks or take ~24–48 h in prolonged-endurance contexts depending on fibre, diet and protocol.

Project rule:

**PCr is generally fast, but liver glycogen, muscle glycogen and intramyocellular lipid cannot be arranged into one universal recovery hierarchy.**

---

## 21. Nutritional dependence

Different pools respond differently to nutrition.

- PCr: primarily oxidative resynthesis; not direct carbohydrate replacement.
- Muscle glycogen: strongly dependent on carbohydrate availability when rapid restoration is needed.
- Liver glycogen: strongly responsive to carbohydrate availability.
- Intramyocellular lipid: restoration can depend on dietary fat in some endurance contexts.
- Blood glucose: depends on hepatic and exogenous appearance versus tissue disposal.

Project rule:

**no single nutrient “refills all energy systems.”**

Exact dose/timing/form belongs NUT.

---

## 22. Repeated-session implications

A later session can begin with incomplete restoration of one or more substrate pools.

Which state matters depends on the next task.

Project rule:

**repeated-session substrate state is task specific; one recovered substrate cannot certify global readiness.**

Specific session scheduling belongs PRO/REC.

---

## 23. Substrate restoration vs performance recovery

Faster glycogen resynthesis does not necessarily accelerate force recovery.

Conversely, next-day performance can differ even when measured 24-h glycogen is similar.

Project rule:

**substrate restoration and functional/performance recovery are related but non-equivalent.**

---

## 24. Training-mode profiles

### Endurance
Can materially use muscle glycogen, liver glycogen and intramyocellular lipid over prolonged exercise.

### Sprint / HIIT
Can produce rapid PCr depletion and substantial local muscle-glycogen use without reproducing prolonged-endurance lipid depletion.

### Resistance
Can produce local PCr and muscle-glycogen depletion; lipid use is protocol/fibre dependent.

Project rule:

**training mode changes the likely depletion profile, but no mode has one fixed substrate signature.**

---

## 25. External work

External mechanical work alone does not determine substrate depletion.

Different intensity structures can produce different glycogen use despite very different total mechanical work.

Project rule:

**same or greater mechanical work does not imply the same or greater substrate depletion.**

---

## 26. Measurement boundaries

### PCr
Biopsy / 31P-MRS; recovery estimates depend on acquisition/modeling.

### Muscle glycogen
Biopsy biochemistry, histochemistry/immunofluorescence and 13C-MRS; whole-muscle averages can hide local heterogeneity.

### Liver glycogen
13C-MRS; concentration must be distinguished from total content if organ volume changes.

### Intramyocellular lipid
Biochemistry, microscopy and 1H-MRS operationalize somewhat different constructs and can yield different apparent changes.

### Blood glucose
Concentration measurement does not identify glucose appearance/disposal; tracer methods are needed for flux separation.

Project rule:

**measurement method is part of substrate-state interpretation.**

Detailed measurement protocol belongs TST.

---

## 27. Canonical non-equivalences

1. PCr depletion ≠ ATP depletion.
2. PCr depletion ≠ complete fatigue diagnosis.
3. PCr recovery ≠ fixed universal time.
4. PCr repletion ≠ direct carbohydrate refilling.
5. muscle glycogen ≠ liver glycogen.
6. whole-muscle glycogen ≠ every fibre/subcellular pool.
7. low glycogen ≠ zero glycogen.
8. glycogen depletion ≠ universal sole fatigue cause.
9. glycogen repletion ≠ glycogen supercompensation.
10. glycogen repletion ≠ functional recovery.
11. liver glycogen concentration ≠ total liver glycogen content when liver volume changes.
12. liver glycogen recovery ≠ universally faster than muscle glycogen recovery.
13. IMTG ≠ perfectly interchangeable with every IMCL construct.
14. intramyocellular lipid ≠ intermuscular/extramyocellular fat.
15. whole-body fat oxidation ≠ net IMTG use.
16. endurance IMTG use ≠ universal HIIT/resistance IMTG use.
17. blood glucose concentration ≠ storage depot.
18. stable blood glucose ≠ low glucose turnover.
19. hypoglycemia ≠ universal sole fatigue mechanism.
20. substrate repletion ≠ full readiness.
21. one substrate recovery time ≠ all-substrate recovery time.
22. liver glycogen / muscle glycogen / IMTG ≠ one fixed recovery hierarchy.
23. high carbohydrate availability ≠ guaranteed complete muscle glycogen by a fixed hour.
24. restored liver glycogen ≠ restored muscle glycogen.
25. same session duration ≠ same depletion profile.
26. same mechanical work ≠ same substrate depletion.
27. one biopsy/MRS value ≠ whole-body substrate state.

---

## 28. Confidence

### HIGH

- substrate depletion/repletion is pool specific;
- PCr recovery is rapid and oxidative but not governed by one universal full-repletion time;
- muscle glycogen use is task/recruitment dependent;
- resistance exercise can materially reduce local muscle glycogen;
- muscle and liver glycogen are distinct stores;
- liver glycogen concentration can differ from total content when volume changes;
- intramyocellular lipid is an exercise substrate;
- IMTG/IMCL estimates depend on fibre/mode/method;
- blood glucose is a regulated concentration/flux, not a depot;
- stable glucose concentration can coexist with high glucose turnover;
- substrate restoration and functional recovery are non-equivalent;
- no single recovery clock applies to all pools.

### MODERATE-HIGH / CONTEXT DEPENDENT

- exact resistance-exercise glycogen depletion magnitude;
- exact glycogen-sparing effect of carbohydrate during exercise;
- exact liver-vs-muscle recovery rate in a given protocol;
- exact intramyocellular-lipid depletion/repletion magnitude;
- dietary-fat effect size on IMTG restoration;
- exact substrate level required for a later task.

### NOT RETAINED AS UNIVERSAL

- PCr fully restored at exactly 3 min;
- muscle glycogen fully restored at exactly 24 h;
- liver glycogen always restores faster than muscle glycogen;
- IMTG always needs 24–48 h to recover;
- every exhaustive task ends at zero glycogen;
- blood glucose is a storage tank;
- hypoglycemia is the sole endurance-fatigue mechanism;
- high carbohydrate refills all energy systems;
- one recovered substrate proves full readiness.
