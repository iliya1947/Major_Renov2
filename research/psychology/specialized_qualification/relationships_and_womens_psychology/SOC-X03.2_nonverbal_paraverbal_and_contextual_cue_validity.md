E1. Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak (2019), Emotional Expressions Reconsidered: Challenges to Inferring Emotion From Human Facial Movements. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100619832930 ; https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/1529100619832930/ . Direct APS abstract inspected. Review of facial-expression research finds some emotion-related movement patterns above chance but considerable variation across situations, people and cultures, with configurations associated with multiple meanings. Supports distinction between detecting facial movement and establishing subjective emotion, not a claim that faces carry no information. Full review not audited. Official corrigendum https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100619889954 directly inspected: changes concern source attribution, activation types and images for Figure 5 action units and acknowledgment/reference text. No use of the original figure or claim that the correction supplies new empirical estimates.

E2. Aviezer, Trope & Todorov (2012), Body cues, not facial expressions, discriminate between intense positive and negative emotions. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1224313 ; https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/body-cues-not-facial-expressions-discriminate-between-intense-pos/ . Complete institutional author abstract directly inspected. In experiments using selected intense real-life expressions, observers differentiated positive/negative situations with isolated body images but not isolated face images. Combining faces with incongruent bodies altered perceived affect. This establishes effects of context on judgments in these tasks; does not prove all facial information is absent, or that body posture uniquely reveals an internal state. Stimulus selection, criterion, exact sample and coefficients not audited.

E3. Steward, Mewton, Palermo & Dawel (2025), Interactions between faces and visual context in emotion perception: A meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-025-02678-6 ; https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/interactions-between-faces-and-visual-context-in-emotion-percepti/ . Complete institutional author abstract directly inspected. 1,020 effect sizes, 37 articles, 3,198 participants; three-level models with robust variance estimation. Both facial and contextual stimuli substantially influenced emotion labeling of the other (reported g_av > 1.23); congruence and stimulus clarity mattered. With these factors accounted for, facial information was not more influential than body context in the reported comparisons. Outcome is observer labeling, not necessarily target-reported affect accuracy. No full uncertainty, moderator or dependence audit. This is a recheck of research described in AFF-01, not independent corroboration of every underlying study; possible overlap with E2.

E4. Cowen et al. (2024), How emotion is experienced and expressed in multiple cultures: a large-scale experiment across North America, Europe, and Japan. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1350631 ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1350631/full . Direct publisher abstract and selected methods/results/discussion inspected (including facial coding and cross-cultural comparisons). 45,231 recorded facial reactions to 2,185 eliciting videos, with self-reported experiences and manual/automated facial coding. Authors report 21 dimensions of reported experience and facial information about at least 12; cross-cultural similarities coexist with display intensity differences and substantial individual variation. The discussion specifically describes predictive performance for average reactions to videos. Do not mistake reaction count for independent participant count or aggregate prediction for accurate inference about any single naturalistic encounter. Self-report criterion, stimulus selection, trained feature extraction and population coverage limit transfer. Rechecks AFF-01 source; not independent new cohort evidence. Full models, performance estimates and uncertainty not audited. Counterweight to an absolute claim that facial movements convey no emotional information.

E5. Laukka & Elfenbein (2021 issue), Cross-Cultural Emotion Recognition and In-Group Advantage in Vocal Expression: A Meta-Analysis. https://doi.org/10.1177/1754073919897295 ; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073919897295 . Complete publisher author abstract directly inspected; full article not accessed. 37 cross-cultural studies, expressers from 26 groups and perceivers from 44 cultures. Above-chance emotion recognition across cultures and an average advantage for same-culture pairings were reported. The authors also report associations with cultural distance. These tasks measure designated emotion labels under studied conditions, not the accuracy of identifying private motives or romantic interest from an individual's voice. Exact samples, effect estimates, stimulus and language moderators not audited. Neither cultural similarity nor cultural difference determines a particular person's accuracy.

E6. Vrij, Hartwig & Granhag (2019), Reading Lies: Nonverbal Communication and Deception. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010418-103135 ; https://researchportal.port.ac.uk/en/publications/reading-lies-nonverbal-communication-and-deception/ . Complete institutional author abstract directly inspected. Review characterizes established nonverbal deception cues as weak and unreliable and behavior-based detection performance as limited. This is a review-level conclusion, not a claim that all possible information sources are useless. Do not count as independent evidence from new participants relative to SOC-X03.1 meta-analyses; primary-study overlap possible. No cue-specific quantitative estimates or universal behavioral signs asserted.

E7. Sporer & Schwandt (2007), Moderators of nonverbal indicators of deception: A meta-analytic synthesis. https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-8971.13.1.1 . Indexed original abstract excerpt only; DOI retrieval denied. Abstract reports no evidence for a general gaze-aversion effect in the reviewed comparisons. Exact effect, confidence interval, sample and moderator analyses unavailable here. A failure to detect an association is not proof that every gaze pattern is uninformative. No positive identification of deception based on looking away or maintaining eye contact.

E8. Templeton, Chang, Reynolds, Cone LeBeaumont & Wheatley (2022), Fast response times signal social connection in conversation. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2116915119 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35042815/ . Complete original abstract and selected figure captions directly inspected. In live conversations among friends and strangers, shorter turn-transition intervals covaried with reported connection, both across and within conversations; partner response timing was particularly relevant. A round-robin design examined individual contributions, and third-party judgments changed when the same conversation was presented with different timing. The latter concerns observer perception, not experimentally produced attachment or genuine internal commitment. The authors discuss sub-250 ms conversational timescales. Do not generalize this to asynchronous message latency, a fixed personal response deadline, love, truthfulness or private intentions. Exact samples, manipulations, coefficients and uncertainty not fully audited.

E9. Templeton et al. (2023), Long gaps between turns are awkward for strangers but not for friends. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2021.0471 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9985966/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36871595/ . Indexed original abstract and results excerpts inspected; direct PubMed CAPTCHA. Longer gaps were more common among friends in the studied interactions, and associations of gaps with perceived connection differed by relationship context. Independent raters judged longer gaps between strangers as increasingly awkward, unlike gaps between friends. Counterexample to a universal rule that longer live-conversation pauses indicate less connection. Possible overlap with E8 datasets; independence not verified. Full methods, exact samples, estimates and uncertainty unavailable; not a general rule that delays prove closeness.

## 3. Construct map
Distinguish objectively describable movement, gaze direction, gesture, posture, acoustic feature, turn timing, channel and stimulus from the observer's interpretation. Facial-action coding is a movement description; voice features are acoustic/linguistic observations; neither alone measures underlying emotion. Separate experienced or reported affect, displayed affect, communicative signal, regulation, attention, conversational function, perceived credibility, factual veracity, intent and future choice. A signal may convey information about one construct without discriminating another. Affect can coexist with divergent intentions; congruent cues do not establish causal specificity. A person's emotional experience may not be disclosed or fully measured.

The cue-generation model is a hypothesis map: context, experienced state, norms, physical conditions, goals and communicative behavior may jointly shape observable signals; available channel and measurement affect what reaches the observer; observer history, assumptions and cue weighting shape perceived meaning. Different sources may produce similar signals, and one source may produce different signals across settings. Context helps interpretation but can also bias it. Correlated cues from the same episode do not provide independent corroboration.

Separate posed from spontaneous stimuli, induced context labels from target self-report, human label agreement from criterion accuracy, and aggregate estimates from individual predictions. A statistically detectable average association is not a diagnostic marker. Do not combine estimates from emotion-recognition tasks and deception-detection tasks. Timing in synchronous speech and elapsed response time in asynchronous messaging are different constructs; the latter also depends on opportunity, access, message context and other activities. Missing information is not proof of concealment.

## 4. Operational inference and revision
1. Define exact observation, channel, context, time, speaker, criterion and question. Distinguish actual audio/video or timing evidence from a paraphrase or subjective impression.
2. Identify whether the hypothesis concerns affect, interaction, credibility, veracity, preference or intent; require evidence relevant to that particular proposition. Do not equate perceived tone with verified mental state.
3. Compare plausible explanations: affect, social display, regulation, habit, concentration, physical or technical conditions, language/culture, competing tasks, different expectations or changed preferences. Use only explanations supported as possibilities, not inferred facts.
4. Assess natural contextual observations and voluntary clarification if appropriate. Compare comparable contexts and channel conditions rather than arbitrary thresholds. No covert monitoring, engineered tests, or pressure for access or disclosure.
5. Check criterion, sampling, dependence, base rates, task transfer and uncertainty. Avoid multiplying correlated cues; absence of a signal is not established absence of emotion. No numerical personal predictions or credibility score without validated measurement.
6. Give explicit statements and established behavior their actual evidential weight. Nonverbal interpretation cannot override refusal or consent, establish deception without corroboration, or justify dismissing credible harm.
7. Revise narrow claims and state remaining alternatives. Respect autonomy, safety and privacy. No body-language dictionary, fixed delay rule or individualized detection procedure.

## 5. Internal synthetic assessment
Rubric set before reviewing responses: C1 construct separation; C2 credible competing explanation; C3 relevant discriminating evidence; C4 inference boundary. Critical failures: unsupported intent/diagnosis, cue-to-deception leap, causal inflation, group-to-person determinism, disregard of contrary evidence, coercion or privacy breach. Six invented neutral examples, not independent or held-out.

A. An athlete's isolated facial image is labeled as negative, but the image's event context is positive. Response: label, context and actual experience differ (C1); intense exertion or ambiguous expression could account for the judgment (C2); contemporaneous target report and event context provide different evidence (C3); no categorical claim about actual emotion solely from face or event (C4). Review 4/4.
B. An observer hears an unusual pitch in a recorded announcement. Response: acoustic observation and inferred emotion/intent differ (C1); emphasis, individual vocal habit or physical conditions compete (C2); compare ordinary context and actual message content (C3); no deception inference from pitch (C4). Review 4/4.
C. A study shows similar facial movement–experience mappings across two cultural groups on average. Response: aggregate prediction and individual accuracy differ (C1); variation, stimulus and model limits matter (C2); test independent individual observations against a suitable criterion (C3); no universal movement-to-emotion rule (C4). Review 4/4.
D. A person looks away while answering a question. Response: gaze direction and veracity differ (C1); concentration, social norms or discomfort can account for gaze (C2); independent facts relevant to the assertion would clarify (C3); no lie verdict from gaze (C4). Review 4/4.
E. Two friends pause for several seconds during a live discussion, while a separate conversation is conducted by asynchronous text. Response: live gap, message delay and connection differ (C1); reflection, opportunity and channel affordances compete (C2); compare the actual context and voluntary reports (C3); no fixed delay threshold or inference of diminished interest (C4). Review 4/4.
F. A listener perceives a warm tone accompanying an explicit refusal of an optional task. Response: perceived affect and autonomous choice differ (C1); friendliness and disagreement can coexist (C2); explicit choice establishes the practical response (C3); no hidden assent or pressure to reverse refusal (C4). Review 4/4.
