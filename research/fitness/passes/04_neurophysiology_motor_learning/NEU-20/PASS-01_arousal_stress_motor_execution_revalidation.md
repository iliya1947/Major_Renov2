## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a defensible arousal/stress framework for motor execution without:

- treating arousal, stress, anxiety and pressure as synonyms;
- assuming one universal inverted-U curve;
- treating “attentional narrowing” as always harmful;
- assuming pressure necessarily impairs performance;
- assuming high arousal necessarily improves force/power;
- reducing choking to one mechanism;
- treating automaticity as immunity to pressure;
- assuming expert skill always survives pressure;
- treating acute pressure-related performance loss as loss of learning;
- importing sport-psychology treatment protocols into NEU-20;
- inferring causal mechanisms from HRV/EEG/fMRI markers alone.

### Primary downstream context

Healthy adult resistance trainees performing learned or partially learned motor tasks under:

- heavy load;
- evaluation;
- competition;
- time pressure;
- motivational incentives;
- self-imposed pressure.

### Critical outcomes

- acute task performance;
- motor accuracy;
- executional success;
- force/velocity when strength is the task;
- kinematic stability;
- attentional control;
- delayed retention after pressure removal.

## 2. Operational distinctions

### Arousal
Physiological/central activation state.

### Stress
Process/state in which demands are appraised relative to resources/coping.

### Anxiety
Affective/cognitive state involving apprehension/worry and somatic activation.

### Pressure
Situation in which performing well is perceived as especially important.

These constructs overlap but are not equivalent.

2026 ESSA position statement (PMID **42271117**) explicitly treats pressure as a distinct performance episode linked to:

- awareness of performance importance;
- motivation to perform well.

Project rule:

**name the construct being manipulated or measured; do not collapse arousal, anxiety, stress and pressure into one scalar.**

Confidence: **HIGH taxonomy**.

## 3. Physiological arousal

Physiological arousal can involve changes in:

- heart rate;
- autonomic balance/HRV;
- muscle activation/tension;
- pupil-linked arousal;
- catecholaminergic activation;
- cortical preparation.

But a physiological marker is not itself “performance readiness.”

The relationship between arousal and performance depends on:

- task;
- required force vs precision;
- expertise;
- interpretation/appraisal;
- attentional demand;
- current activation baseline.

The 2024 arousal-performance work (PMID **38277436**, commentary PMID **38570252**) provides converging evidence for an inverted-U relation in perceptual decision tasks using pupil-indexed spontaneous arousal.

However:

- this is not a universal motor-skill law;
- many sport-pressure studies use induced anxiety/pressure rather than direct arousal dose;
- strength tasks can benefit from deliberate activation.

Canonical rule:

**arousal-performance relation is task dependent; moderate-arousal inverted-U evidence cannot be converted into a universal prescription for motor execution.**

Confidence: **HIGH against universal curve / MODERATE for task-specific nonlinear effects**.

---

## 4. Direct strength-specific counterexample to “high arousal is bad”

2026 deadlift study (PMID **41628393**):

- 200 competitive strength athletes;
- free-choice psyching-up vs passive control;
- deadlift velocity:
  - psyching-up ≈ 0.39 m/s;
  - control ≈ 0.34 m/s;
- reported performance increase ≈ **18.58%**;
- estimated predicted 1RM increase ≈ **4.3%**;
- no significant performance difference among eight psyching-up strategy categories.

This shows:

**activation-oriented mental preparation can acutely improve a high-force task.**

Important limits:

- acute performance, not learning;
- self-selected/free-choice intervention;
- velocity/predicted 1RM rather than competition 1RM;
- psychological strategy category was not experimentally isolated.

Project rule:

**do not prescribe global arousal reduction when the task benefits from activation.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for acute strength performance**.

## 5. Pressure can improve, preserve or impair performance

The 2026 ESSA position statement (PMID **42271117**) explicitly distinguishes:

- **choking** — acute substantial performance decrement under pressure;
- **clutch performance** — successful/facilitative performance under pressure.

Older expert-golf evidence (PMID **21265862**) even found increased pressure associated with greater putting accuracy in that protocol despite higher anxiety/effort/HR.

Therefore:

`pressure`
≠
`performance impairment`.

Canonical rule:

**pressure is a context; the behavioral response can be facilitative, neutral or debilitative.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 6. Simple inverted-U model — useful heuristic, not universal law

A 2025 elite-shooting paper (PMID **41309875**) interpreted within-competition performance as an inverted-U relation across presumed stress/arousal stages.

But the inference is limited because:

- arousal/stress/anxiety were not independently manipulated as one causal variable;
- game stage can change fatigue, strategy, learning, pacing and stakes;
- several psychological variables were assumed to increase with stage.

Thus it cannot establish a universal motor-performance curve.

Project rule:

**an inverted-U can describe some task-specific data; it is not a general diagnostic model for an individual athlete.**

Confidence: **HIGH against universalization**.

## 7. Attentional narrowing

Pressure/anxiety can change:

- attentional scope;
- task-relevant vs irrelevant allocation;
- self-monitoring;
- visual search;
- distractibility.

But “attentional narrowing” is not inherently bad.

2025 multi-study running research (PMID **40566819**) showed deliberate attentional narrowing could increase effort and running pace.

Under pressure, the problem is more accurately:

**whether attention becomes mismatched to task information needs.**

Possible maladaptive states include:

- excessive self-monitoring;
- attention to threat/worry;
- reduced environmental sampling;
- fixation on one irrelevant feature.

Possible adaptive states include:

- task-focused selective attention;
- reduced irrelevant distraction;
- stable pre-performance fixation in closed skills.

Canonical rule:

**attentional narrowing is harmful only when the narrowed information set is insufficient or task irrelevant.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 8. Pressure-related attentional theories

The 2026 ESSA synthesis groups choking theories into two major attentional families:

### Self-focus / explicit monitoring / reinvestment
Pressure increases conscious monitoring/control of movement that is normally more automated.

### Distraction / attentional-control
Pressure shifts resources toward:

- worry;
- external distractors;
- threat signals;

reducing task-relevant control.

These are not mutually exclusive universal mechanisms.

2026 review of choking mechanisms likewise lists:

- overarousal;
- distraction;
- self-focused attention;
- predictive-coding modulation.

Canonical rule:

**choking is mechanistically heterogeneous; do not diagnose “reinvestment” or “distraction” from poor performance alone.**

Confidence: **HIGH against one-mechanism account**.

---

## 9. Attention can change without obvious performance loss

2023 interceptive-task study (PMID **37251048**):

- pressure/error feedback increased failure-probability/cost appraisals and anxiety;
- downstream performance/attention effects were not consistently observed.

2026 time-pressure studies (PMID **41556930**):

- urgency increased distress;
- distress impaired some efficiency measures;
- direct effects simultaneously improved some efficiency measures while decreasing accuracy;
- total effects differed by outcome.

These studies show:

**stress effects can dissociate across accuracy, efficiency, attention and subjective distress.**

Project rule:

**measure the relevant performance dimension; “more anxious” does not automatically mean “worse motor performance.”**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 10. Coordination under stress

Pressure can alter movement execution through changes in:

- muscular tension;
- movement variability;
- timing;
- kinematics;
- force control;
- gaze/visual sampling.

Classic pressure-putting work (PMID **20409012**) found pressure-associated changes in:

- anxiety/effort;
- heart rate/HRV;
- muscle activity;
- lateral clubhead acceleration;

alongside poorer putting performance.

2026 free-throw study (PMID **42151265**) found environmental pressure and sensory uncertainty increased anxiety/failure appraisals and were associated with performance and kinematic/neurophysiological changes.

2026 7T-fMRI reaching study (PMID **39547475**) found pressure-related choking associated with task-specific cerebellar/internal-model network effects.

These establish that pressure can reorganize motor execution.

They do not identify one universal coordination signature.

Canonical rule:

**stress-related coordination changes are task specific and can be compensatory, neutral or performance degrading.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 11. Excessive arousal / choking

Choking should not mean any bad attempt.

2026 Delphi expert-consensus work on choking definition identified a multi-element definition with 91% expert consensus and stressed distinguishing choking from:

- ordinary underperformance;
- yips;
- mental blocks;
- other performance failures.

The 2026 ESSA statement similarly defines choking as an acute substantial performance decrement under increased pressure/anxiety with reduced perceived control.

Project rule:

**do not label every pressure-associated miss as choking.**

Confidence: **HIGH construct boundary**.

---

## 12. Choking interventions

2026 meta-analysis (PMID **41951087**):

- 21 studies;
- interventions included:
  - quiet-eye training;
  - pre-performance routines;
  - mindfulness;
  - left-hand contraction;
  - self-talk;
  - rational-emotive approaches;
- pooled improvement:
  - motor accuracy g ≈ **0.660**;
  - executional success g ≈ **0.444**;
  - target deviation g ≈ **0.301**;
- effects were more consistent in closed-skill tasks;
- open-skill estimates were less robust after publication-bias adjustment.

Therefore:

**pressure resilience is trainable to some extent, but there is no universally superior intervention.**

Exact psychological intervention design belongs PSY/COA.

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 13. Pressure training

2024 anxiety-training study (PMID **38376935**):

- n = 84;
- 300 golf putts over 2 days;
- mild-pressure training vs no-pressure control;
- on day 3, anxiety-trained group maintained performance across low/mild/high-pressure tests;
- control group deteriorated under pressure;
- mental effort and movement reinvestment changed, but neither clearly mediated the performance effect.

This is strong evidence that:

**practice under manageable pressure can improve robustness under later pressure.**

But:

- one early-learning putting task;
- pressure dose was task specific;
- mechanism remained unresolved.

ESSA 2026 advises pressure training should be:

- gradual;
- purposeful;
- monitored;
- not overused;
- generally avoided while initially learning a new skill.

Project rule:

**pressure exposure can be trained, but should be introduced after a viable skill/strategy exists rather than used as indiscriminate stress inoculation.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 14. Familiarity and automaticity

Experience and proceduralization can change how pressure affects performance.

Classic automaticity evidence shows:

- novices often benefit from more explicit monitoring;
- experts may perform worse when attention is redirected toward well-proceduralized execution details (PMID **15260208**).

This supports the reinvestment idea in some tasks.

However:

- experts can still choke;
- automaticity does not guarantee pressure immunity;
- explicit/implicit learning history, pressure type and task demands matter;
- some high-pressure expert tasks improve under pressure.

Project rule:

**automaticity can reduce the need for conscious control, but it is not a protective shield against all pressure.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 15. Familiarity with pressure itself matters

Task familiarity and pressure familiarity are different.

Older expert anxiety-training work (PMID **19123115**) and the larger 2024 replication in early learning (PMID **38376935**) both support:

**exposure to the relevant pressure state can improve later performance under similar pressure.**

Therefore:

`expert at task`
≠
`adapted to pressure context`.

Project rule:

**pressure robustness is partly context-specific and should be demonstrated under the target pressure conditions.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 16. Pressure and learning must remain separate

This is closure-critical.

A learner can:

1. retain the skill;
2. perform worse temporarily under pressure;
3. return to baseline when pressure is removed.

That pattern is:

**state-dependent performance degradation**, not evidence that learning was erased.

Likewise:

- performing well under pressure does not prove broader transfer;
- pressure-trained performance does not prove better low-pressure learning;
- acute psyching-up benefit does not prove improved skill retention.

Canonical rule:

**to claim loss of learning, demonstrate persistent deterioration outside the transient pressure state or impaired delayed retention/relearning—not merely a pressured performance decrement.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 17. Stress-state dependence vs durable learning

The 2024 anxiety-training study provides a useful structure:

- same learned putting task;
- performance tested under no, mild and high pressure;
- pressure history changed robustness.

This demonstrates the importance of testing:

`skill memory`
×
`current state/context`.

Project rule:

**retention and robustness-to-pressure are related but distinct properties of skill.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 18. Strength-task application boundary

Direct pressure/choking evidence in resistance exercise is sparse relative to:

- golf;
- shooting;
- basketball;
- darts;
- laboratory visuomotor tasks.

Strength-specific evidence supports psyching-up and activation for acute force/velocity.

It does not establish:

- optimal arousal level for squat/deadlift/bench;
- one pressure-training protocol;
- one anxiety threshold for technical degradation;
- whether competition pressure improves or worsens a specific lifter's technique.

Canonical rule:

**strength tasks may benefit from higher activation than precision tasks, but individual/task-specific testing is required.**

Confidence: **MODERATE**.

---

## 19. Practical synthesis for later resistance-training coaching

A defensible future heuristic:

1. distinguish task type:
   - maximal force/power;
   - precision/technical learning;
   - mixed;
2. distinguish arousal from anxiety/pressure;
3. establish low-pressure competent execution first for a new skill;
4. assess how performance changes under realistic pressure/load;
5. do not automatically lower arousal if activation improves force output;
6. do not automatically increase arousal if precision/coordination degrades;
7. simplify attention toward task-relevant information under pressure;
8. avoid adding excessive conscious movement control to an already automated skill unless a specific correction is needed;
9. introduce manageable pressure training progressively if pressure robustness is a real downstream requirement;
10. after a pressure-related performance drop, retest under baseline conditions before concluding the skill was lost.

Status:

**PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not a universal arousal prescription.

## 22. Measurement boundaries

### Physiological arousal
Measured activation state using specified physiological marker(s).

### Pressure
Increased importance/incentive to perform well.

### Anxiety
Subjective/cognitive/somatic response.

### Stress appraisal
Perceived demands relative to coping resources.

### Choking
Substantial acute performance decrement attributable to pressure context.

### Clutch/facilitative response
Successful goal-directed performance under heightened pressure.

### Acute performance robustness
Ability to preserve target performance during pressure.

### Skill retention
Delayed capability independent of transient pressure state.

### Pressure-specific transfer
Performance under a different but relevant pressure context.

## 23. Established content-level findings

### HIGH

1. Pressure can facilitate, preserve or impair performance.
2. Arousal, stress, anxiety and pressure are distinct constructs.
3. There is no universal optimal arousal level across motor tasks.
4. Acute pressure-related performance decrement is not equivalent to lost learning.
5. Choking is not any poor attempt.
6. Pressure effects are task/outcome dependent.
7. Psychological pressure can alter attention and motor execution.

### MODERATE-HIGH

8. Attention mismatch/self-focus/distraction can contribute to choking.
9. Pressure training can improve later pressure robustness in some tasks.
10. Automaticity/expertise changes attentional requirements.
11. Pressure interventions can reduce choking on average, especially in closed skills.
12. Deliberate psyching-up can acutely improve strength performance.

### MODERATE

13. Attentional narrowing can be either adaptive or maladaptive depending on task information needs.
14. Physiological arousal can show nonlinear relations with performance in some task classes.
15. Familiarity with pressure is partly trainable/context specific.

### LOW / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

16. Individual optimal arousal level for compound lifting.
17. Exact arousal threshold for technical degradation.
18. Exact pressure-training dose for resistance exercise.
19. Exact mechanism of choking in an individual.

## 25. Evidence-ledger candidates

1. Arousal, stress, anxiety and performance pressure are distinct constructs and should not be collapsed.
2. Pressure may facilitate, preserve or impair performance; choking is one possible response, not the default.
3. Inverted-U arousal-performance effects exist in some tasks but are not a universal motor-performance law.
4. Attentional narrowing is not inherently harmful; pressure failure is better framed as task-relevant attentional-control mismatch.
5. Choking mechanisms are heterogeneous: self-focus, distraction, overarousal and internal-model/predictive mechanisms all have supporting evidence.
6. Psychological pressure can alter motor kinematics/coordination, but no universal coordination signature exists.
7. Pressure-training/anxiety-training can improve later performance robustness in some tasks.
8. Automaticity can reduce explicit-control demands but does not make experts immune to pressure.
9. Strength-specific psyching-up can acutely improve deadlift velocity; this is performance evidence, not learning evidence.
10. Counter-choking interventions show moderate pooled benefits with stronger/more robust effects in closed than open skills.
11. Acute pressure-related performance loss does not establish loss of learning.
12. Sport-psychology intervention design is downstream Area 12 scope.
13. Direct compound-lift pressure/choking evidence remains sparse.
