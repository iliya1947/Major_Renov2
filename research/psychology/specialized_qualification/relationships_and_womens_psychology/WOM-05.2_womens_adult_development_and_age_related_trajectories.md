# WOM-05.2 — Women's adult development and age-related trajectories

Date: 2026-11-26. Status: bounded block COMPLETE_FOR_SPECIFIED_SCOPE (internal reasoning review only); WOM-05 remains IN_PROGRESS.

## 1. Question and dependency gate
How can age-associated differences in women's traits and life goals be distinguished from within-person development, historical/cohort differences, selection into roles, opportunity and measurement effects? The practical gap is treating chronological age or maternal status as a sufficient explanation of an adult woman's personality or motivation. Claim types: descriptive longitudinal association and methodological inference, not identified causal mechanisms. Scope: adult women, including women without children; mixed-sex evidence used only with explicit applicability limits. Reopened WOM-05.1 and SPQ-00, relevant excerpts of IND-01.2, WOM-01.2 and WOM-02.1. Retain IND-03 and INT-02 transfer rules. Closure: evidence for developmental continuity and variability, a counterexample to uniform age/role trajectories, actionable differential inference and synthetic checks; not an exhaustive review.

## 2. Evidence and provenance
Targeted public searches 2026-11-26: adult personality development longitudinal age sex differences; personality stability/change longitudinal meta-analysis; birth cohort personality trajectories; gender/parental status life goals; longitudinal well-being women without children and mothers. Select longitudinal primary studies, coordinated analysis and meta-analysis; seek null and alternative patterns. No systematic search or raw-data reanalysis. Full articles were not read end-to-end. Indexed author abstracts and publisher sections are distinguished below.

E1. Bleidorn et al. (2022), Personality stability and change: A meta-analysis of longitudinal studies. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000365 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35834197/
Indexed author abstract and publisher extract inspected; direct opens blocked/cache miss. New longitudinal evidence includes 189 study contributions (N=178,503) for rank-order stability and 276 (N=242,542) for mean-level change. Rank stability increases mainly earlier in life and levels off in young adulthood. This does not imply no later mean-level or individual change. Reused IND-01.2 evidence, not an independent replication. Western overrepresentation; sex-specific precision and moderator tables not independently verified here.

E2. Graham et al. (2020), Trajectories of Big Five Personality Traits: A Coordinated Analysis of 16 Longitudinal Samples. https://doi.org/10.1002/per.2259 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7869960/
Publisher abstract directly inspected; indexed author full-text result extracts inspected, direct PMC open blocked. Identical multilevel growth models across 16 samples, over 60,000 participants, mainly midlife/older adulthood; self-reported traits. Linear summaries showed declines in conscientiousness, extraversion and openness; nonlinear models suggested rising late-life neuroticism. Heterogeneous estimates and mixed age/sex moderators. Indexed results for sex differences in change: openness B=0.00, 95% CI [-0.02,0.02]; conscientiousness B=-0.02, CI [-0.04,0.01]; agreeableness B=0.00, CI [-0.02,0.02]. Do not interpret rounded estimates as exact zero or equivalence. A coauthor's explanatory page reports somewhat steeper neuroticism declines among women; sex coding, units and full moderator tables not verified here. The samples' age composition and measurement differences limit direct comparison with E1; not a contradiction of early-adult increases. No universal female trajectory.

E3. Brandt, Drewelies, Willis, Schaie, Ram, Gerstorf & Wagner (2022), Acting Like a Baby Boomer? Birth-Cohort Differences in Adults' Personality Trajectories During the Last Half a Century. https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976211037971
Complete publisher abstract and open-practice statement directly inspected, indexed results extracts available. Seattle Longitudinal Study N=4,732, 53% female, mean age 52.93 (SD 16.69), birth years 1883–1976, observation span 50 years (not 50 years per participant). At the common reference age of 56, later-born cohorts had lower agreeableness and neuroticism, higher extraversion and openness. Differences in within-person slopes by birth cohort were uncommon, reported for agreeableness only, with stronger increases in later cohorts. Distinguish cohort differences in levels from slopes. Indexed results give approximately 0–15% explained variance across levels and rates, but specific models/precision not independently verified. Observational growth models, not preregistered; no unique causal decomposition of age, period and cohort or proof of social-investment mechanisms. Seattle cohort may overlap with other coordinated samples; do not assume E2 and E3 independent.

E4. Buchinger, Richter & Heckhausen (2022 issue; online 2021), The Development of Life Goals Across the Adult Life Span. https://doi.org/10.1093/geronb/gbab154
Publisher abstract, selected results and limitations directly inspected. German SOEP N=52,052, ages 18–84, 24-year observation period; cohort-sequential multigroup latent growth models of nine single-item importance ratings. Relationship/marriage goal trajectories differed by gender, with higher female ratings earlier and higher male ratings late in adulthood. The importance of parenthood decreased to about age 60 among women without children, whereas other parental-status/gender groups showed increasing trajectories; all groups showed increases after age 70 in the fitted results. Future parents differed from participants remaining without children even at age 18 for some goals: happy relationship/marriage d=0.264, social/political engagement d=0.220 in opposite direction (mixed-sex comparisons, not female-specific causal effects). Prior differences undermine a simple post-parenthood causation interpretation. Cohort analyses show the common-trajectory assumption is imperfect. Importance ratings do not measure effort, current partner preference, consent or behavior. Single items, historical German setting and cohort-sequential assumptions limit inference. Reuse WOM-02.1 evidence, not a new independent sample.

E5. Buchinger, Wahring, Ram, Hoppmann, Heckhausen & Gerstorf (2024), Kids or No Kids? Life Goals in One's 20s Predict Midlife Trajectories of Well-Being. https://doi.org/10.1037/pag0000862 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39666489/
Indexed primary author abstract and institution abstract inspected; direct publisher access denied, institution open failed. Preregistered propensity-score matched SOEP sample N=562, average 24.9 waves, ages 18–30 through age 40 for women/50 for men. Eight well-being facets. Few significant mean midlife trajectory differences between parents and adults without children; early parenthood-goal priority was associated with lower later mental health and cognitive/affective well-being among those without children. Some gender moderation for loneliness. This does not establish equivalence, a causal benefit/harm of parenthood, or that any woman without children regrets her status. Matching observed variables cannot eliminate unmeasured selection; exact effects, overlap, attrition and subgroup precision not independently verified. E4 and E5 use SOEP, so treat as potentially dependent evidence.

E6. Is Being Childless Detrimental to a Woman's Health and Well-Being Across Her Life Course? (2015), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25669856/
Indexed author abstract only. Australian HILDA, 10 waves, 52,381 observations (not distinct persons), longitudinal mixed models, SF-36. Associations between motherhood status and well-being varied by age and relationship status; no single direction throughout life. Contrast with E5 is not a direct contradiction because outcomes, ages, matching, contexts and estimands differ. Exact estimates and measures beyond abstract not verified. Supporting evidence only.

Reused female-specific evidence from WOM-01.2: four-wave historical Radcliffe alumnae study (N=166, ages 43–72) showing distinct trajectories of generativity, stagnation, integrity and despair; not a universal developmental stage model. Prior source access and invariance restrictions retain their original status. No new claim of direct inspection of that publication in this block.

## 3. Synthesis and discrimination
Age = elapsed time, not a mechanism. Calendar period = birth year + age; unrestricted linear age, period and cohort effects cannot all be uniquely identified from observations without constraints or additional assumptions. A longitudinal design helps distinguish within-person change from cross-sectional differences but also moves people through historical time. Compare same-age cohorts, same-cohort trajectories, role transitions and contemporaneous controls; state identifying assumptions and sensitivity. Separate mean level, rank stability, within-person variability, heterogeneity in slopes and facet-specific changes. A stable rank can coexist with changing mean. A null moderator test does not demonstrate identical distributions.

Competing accounts: durable trait/goal change; stable values with different opportunities; role selection and anticipatory change; repeated context-specific states; historical conditions and measurement/reference shifts; combinations. Chronological age, subjective age, time in role, health and resources are different variables. Parenthood is one possible pathway, not a necessary developmental milestone. Separate parenthood-goal importance, fulfillment, role satisfaction, actual choices and constraints. Do not infer an individual's current motivation from average age patterns.

Operational rules:
1. Define target construct, comparable measurement, time interval and contexts. Do not confuse trait, goal importance, enacted effort and outcome.
2. Recover actual prior observations and self-reports. Compare within-person changes across feasible contexts, not imagined age-based baselines.
3. Evaluate selection and anticipation using pretransition measurements and comparable nontransition trajectories where available. Do not infer mediation or causation from timing alone.
4. Preserve period/cohort alternatives, measurement invariance, attrition and subgroup uncertainty. Do not force a universal maturity scale or infer biological causation from age.
5. Update only the supported proposition. Stable reported valuation with renewed participation after opportunity restoration supports a constraint account but is not unique proof; sustained changed valuation across comparable feasible contexts supports the goal-change account. Missing comparators leave competing models open.
6. Respect voluntary self-report and explicit choices; no diagnosis, pressure or age-based prescriptions. Reproductive physiology and clinical questions have separate scope.

## 4. Internal transfer checks
Scoring rubric established for this internal exercise: one criterion each for construct separation, competing explanation, distinguishing evidence and inference boundary. Critical failure: age or sex determinism, unsupported diagnosis/causation, ignoring disconfirmation, coercion or private-data leakage. Examples generated solely for this generic block; self-authored, not held-out or independently assessed.

A. Two cross-sectional groups of adult women score differently on an openness scale. Response: difference is between people, not measured individual change. Alternatives include cohort, measurement and selection; compare repeated comparable scales and overlapping cohorts. No causal age attribution. Four criteria met.
B. A woman stops attending a local astronomy club after a shift-work change, reports the same interest, then resumes when her schedule clears. Response: distinguish opportunity from goal valuation and trait. Restored behavior favors a constraint explanation, but evolving interests or other simultaneous changes remain possible. Compare voluntary reports and similar opportunities. Four criteria met.
C. A longitudinal cohort average increases while individual rank order is unchanged; a second cohort at the same age has a different mean. Response: mean change and rank stability coexist; cohort difference may reflect historical exposures or selection. Neither establishes each individual's slope or an identified age mechanism. Repeated comparable measurements and explicit period assumptions are necessary. Four criteria met.
D. Matched parent and nonparent groups show nonsignificant average differences in midlife well-being; some participants have different prior goals. Response: null does not prove equivalence; matching does not remove unobserved selection. Examine goal fulfillment, specific well-being facets, precision and heterogeneous trajectories. No assertion of regret or causal benefit. Four criteria met.

Internal rubric: 16/16 criteria on these authored cases; no critical error identified within this limited check. This is not independent competence validation, calibrated forecast accuracy, clinical credential or model-weight change.
