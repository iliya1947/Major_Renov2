## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a sufficiently accurate reflex-regulation model for later force, balance, fatigue, SSC/plyometric, technique and motor-learning work without:

- treating the stretch reflex as one fixed all-or-none response;
- treating the entire stretch reflex as purely monosynaptic;
- treating the H-reflex as identical to the mechanically evoked stretch reflex;
- reducing reciprocal inhibition to a fixed agonist-on / antagonist-off switch;
- reducing Golgi-tendon-organ / Ib circuitry to a high-force “autogenic shutdown” safety mechanism;
- assuming presynaptic modulation always means less afferent transmission;
- treating rapid corrective responses as purely spinal;
- attributing the stretch-shortening-cycle effect primarily or universally to reflexes;
- treating one ground-contact-time threshold as a biologically privileged SSC boundary;
- inferring training benefit from increased/decreased reflex amplitude without task context.

### Question type

Spinal and supraspinal reflex regulation during human movement.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 8 exact Section 4 items must be covered at content level; short-latency spinal and long-latency supraspinal responses must be separated; H-reflex and mechanical stretch reflex must be distinguished; reciprocal/Ib circuits must remain task dependent; presynaptic modulation must incorporate modern PAD evidence; SSC neural contribution must be integrated with preactivation, tendon/mechanical and history-dependent muscle effects rather than treated as a single reflex phenomenon.

## 3. Stretch reflex

A rapid muscle stretch activates muscle-spindle afferents.

The shortest pathway is:

`muscle stretch → spindle Ia afferent → homonymous/synergist alpha motor neuron → muscle response`.

But the observed stretch response is not one unitary pathway.

Depending on muscle/task/latency it can include:

- short-latency spinal response;
- reciprocal/interneuronal influences;
- longer-latency supraspinal pathways;
- ongoing descending command;
- background activation and mechanical state.

Current human work shows stretch-response magnitude is strongly task dependent.

The 2026 temporal-analysis study found:
- early short-latency response was influenced largely by load/perturbation;
- late long-latency response showed stronger target/task modulation.

Project rule:

**stretch reflex = time-structured sensorimotor response, not one fixed reflex gain.**

## 4. Monosynaptic reflex arc

The classic monosynaptic component refers specifically to:

`Ia spindle afferent → alpha motor neuron`

with one central synapse.

This is the shortest spinal stretch-reflex pathway.

However the full mechanically evoked response also depends on:

- spindle transduction;
- afferent conduction;
- motoneuron state;
- muscle activation;
- interneuronal circuits;
- tendon/muscle mechanics;
- later supraspinal feedback.

### H-reflex boundary

The H-reflex is produced by electrical stimulation of peripheral nerve afferents, bypassing the spindle’s mechanical transduction.

It is often used as an experimental probe of Ia-to-motoneuron transmission.

Modern reviews emphasize that H-reflex amplitude is influenced by:

- Ia afferent recruitment;
- motoneuron excitability;
- presynaptic modulation;
- homosynaptic/post-activation depression;
- interneuronal pathways;
- methodological parameters.

Project rule:

**H-reflex is a probe of spinal sensorimotor transmission, not the same event as a natural mechanically evoked stretch reflex.**

---

## 5. Reciprocal inhibition

Classic reciprocal inhibition:

`agonist Ia afferent`
`→ Ia inhibitory interneuron`
`→ reduced antagonist motoneuron output`.

This mechanism helps coordinate antagonist activity.

But reciprocal inhibition is not fixed.

Human data show it changes with:

- standing vs walking;
- locomotor phase;
- descending corticospinal input;
- task demands;
- background activation.

It can also coexist with co-contraction when joint stability/task goals require simultaneous agonist-antagonist activation.

Project rule:

**reciprocal inhibition is task-regulated antagonist coordination, not a universal “agonist contracts → antagonist switches off” rule.**

---

## 6. Autogenic inhibition / Ib force feedback

The traditional fitness explanation:

`high tendon tension → GTO → same muscle inhibited to prevent injury`

is not supported as a complete physiological model.

GTO/Ib afferents provide force-related feedback into spinal interneuronal networks.

Their effects can be:

- inhibitory;
- facilitatory;
- redistributed across agonists/synergists/antagonists;
- state/task dependent.

Human locomotion evidence shows Ib facilitation can occur during walking rather than simple autogenic inhibition.

The historical review found no convincing evidence that strength training increases force by “turning down GTO inhibition.”

Modern force-feedback models instead emphasize distributed regulation of limb mechanics.

Project rule:

**Ib/GTO circuitry is distributed force feedback, not a high-force emergency shut-off switch.**

No claim is made that “overcoming GTO inhibition” is a mechanism to train for greater strength.

## 7. Presynaptic modulation

Classical model:

GABAergic axo-axonic mechanisms can reduce effective Ia transmission to motoneurons before the motoneuron synapse.

This has traditionally been termed **presynaptic inhibition**.

Human H-reflex methods have been used to infer this process indirectly.

But there are two important boundaries.

### Measurement boundary

Changes in H-reflex amplitude cannot by themselves identify presynaptic inhibition because the H-reflex also depends on:
- motoneuron state;
- reciprocal/Ib circuits;
- post-activation depression;
- stimulus/recruitment conditions.

### Modern PAD boundary

Recent human evidence (PMID 36916205), informed by newer animal physiology, supports that GABAergic primary-afferent depolarization can in some contexts facilitate Ia spike propagation by reducing branch-point failure rather than simply suppressing transmitter release.

Therefore:

`PAD / GABAaxo action`
does not always equal
`less sensory transmission`.

Project rule:

**presynaptic control gates afferent transmission in a task-dependent way; “presynaptic inhibition” should not be interpreted as a single universal brake.**

## 8. Reflex contribution to rapid movement

Reflex contribution depends on latency.

### Short-latency response

The earliest stretch response is dominated by fast spinal circuitry and current motoneuron/background state.

It can contribute to rapid resistance to unexpected perturbation.

### Long-latency response

Long-latency stretch responses recruit supraspinal pathways and are strongly task dependent.

Human 2026 evidence supports:
- cortical involvement;
- brainstem/reticulospinal involvement;
- modulation by instructions such as resist vs yield.

The 2026 J Physiol study (PMID 43032948) found loud acoustic stimulation selectively enhanced long-latency but not short-latency stretch responses, consistent with online reticulospinal contribution.

Project rule:

**rapid corrective movement is not purely spinal; short- and long-latency feedback use different levels of the motor system.**

Feedforward preactivation and reflex feedback operate together.

## 9. Stretch-shortening cycle — neurophysiological component

SSC:

`active lengthening → transition → shortening`.

Examples include:
- running;
- hopping;
- jumping;
- many plyometric actions.

Neural contributions can include:

- preactivation before ground contact;
- short-latency stretch-related feedback;
- longer-latency task-dependent feedback;
- modulation of motoneuron/spinal excitability.

But the SSC effect cannot be reduced to reflexes.

Current evidence supports contributions from:

- tendon/series-elastic energy storage and return;
- muscle–tendon interaction/decoupling;
- preactivation;
- reflex feedback;
- history-dependent muscle properties including residual force enhancement;
- task-specific timing and mechanics.

The 2026 ESSR review specifically argues that contractile/history-dependent mechanisms can contribute substantially to enhanced force/work in SSC actions.

Project rule:

**SSC performance is an integrated neural + muscle–tendon + history-dependent phenomenon, not a “stretch reflex gives extra power” mechanism.**

### Fast/slow SSC boundary

The common `~250 ms ground-contact` fast-vs-slow classification is retained only as an operational heuristic.

The 2026 review (PMID 42909940) found no evidence for a biological discontinuity at exactly 250 ms.

Project rule:

**250 ms is not a physiologically privileged reflex threshold.**

## 10. Why simplified fitness reflex explanations fail

### Simplification 1
“Muscle spindle senses stretch and automatically contracts the muscle.”

Problem:
- response depends on background drive, task, presynaptic modulation, reciprocal circuits and latency.

### Simplification 2
“GTO protects the muscle by shutting it off at high tension.”

Problem:
- Ib effects can inhibit or facilitate depending on task/circuit state;
- no general training mechanism of “removing GTO inhibition” is established.

### Simplification 3
“H-reflex measures CNS excitability.”

Problem:
- H-reflex is a specific electrically evoked spinal probe with multiple determinants.

### Simplification 4
“Reciprocal inhibition turns antagonists off.”

Problem:
- inhibition is graded/task dependent and can coexist with functional co-contraction.

### Simplification 5
“Stretch reflex is why plyometrics are powerful.”

Problem:
- SSC enhancement is multi-mechanistic: preactivation, elastic behavior, muscle–tendon interaction, reflexes and history-dependent muscle properties all contribute.

### Simplification 6
“Bigger reflex = better athlete / stronger nervous system.”

Problem:
- reflex gain can be increased or decreased adaptively depending on task;
- more reflex excitability is not universally beneficial.

Project rule:

**reflex regulation is adaptive gain control, not a set of fixed protective switches.**

## 11. H-reflex / tendon-reflex interpretation

H-reflex and tendon/mechanical stretch reflex overlap in Ia-motoneuron circuitry but are not interchangeable.

The 2025 soleus stretching study showed:
- H-reflex amplitude depressed during stretching and recovered between stretches;
- tendon-reflex depression persisted longer.

This dissociation demonstrates that:
- electrically evoked Ia transmission;
- muscle-spindle/mechanical-reflex behavior

can change differently.

Project rule:

**one reflex measure cannot stand in for all spinal/reflex mechanisms.**

Detailed measurement validity belongs TST.

## 13. Established project-level model

### Reflex response

`sensory perturbation`
`→ afferent input`
`→ spinal + supraspinal processing`
`→ task-dependent motor response`.

### Gain control

Response magnitude depends on:

- background activation;
- descending command;
- reciprocal/interneuronal circuits;
- presynaptic afferent modulation;
- motoneuron excitability;
- movement phase;
- mechanical context.

### Rapid movement

`preactivation + short-latency feedback + long-latency feedback`
interact with
`muscle–tendon mechanics`.

### SSC

`preactivation + reflex feedback + tendon/elastic behavior + muscle history-dependent properties`
`→ SSC performance`.

No single mechanism owns the effect.

## 14. Training-relevant implications

1. **Do not interpret a stretch reflex as one fixed monosynaptic response.**
2. **Do not equate H-reflex with natural stretch reflex.**
3. **Do not teach reciprocal inhibition as a simple antagonist-off switch.**

6. **Do not assume PAD always reduces afferent transmission.**
7. **Separate short-latency spinal from long-latency supraspinal corrective responses.**
8. **Treat reflex gain as task-specific regulation rather than universally good/bad excitability.**
9. **Do not attribute SSC/plyometric performance to stretch reflex alone.**
