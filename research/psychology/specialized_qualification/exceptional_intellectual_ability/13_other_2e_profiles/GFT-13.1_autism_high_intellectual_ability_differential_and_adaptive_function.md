E13.1a — diagnostic boundaries. CDC May 8 2025, https://www.cdc.gov/autism/hcp/diagnosis/ (criteria and tools directly read; pediatric-facing DSM-5 summary, not independent verification of entire current DSM-5-TR). Autism assessment concerns persistent differences across social communication/interaction and restricted/repetitive behavior, developmental history, clinically significant functional impact and alternatives. DSM-5 describes three social criteria and at least two of four restricted/repetitive categories, including sensory differences as one possible category; learned strategies or changing demands can alter visibility. NICE CG142 https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg142/chapter/recommendations indexed guidance (publisher 403); 2021 NCBI edition https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK554918/ overview directly opened, recommendation search failed. Indexed recommendation sections specify developmental history, comprehensive specialist assessment, differential/coexisting conditions. Full current guideline amendments and ICD-11 exact wording not independently reverified; inherited NDEV guidance applies, not a clinical credential. No diagnosis from one feature, IQ, score profile, or successful/unsuccessful social interaction.

E13.1b — direct gifted youth comparison. Doobay et al. (2014), https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-014-2082-1, publisher abstract directly checked; methods and Tables 1–3 inspected via article-text mirror https://www.academia.edu/29521345/ (not publisher full text). N=40 ASD and 41 no psychiatric diagnosis, ages 5–17, clinic-referred. Inclusion was verbal or nonverbal index >=130 within CI, NOT FSIQ>=130. DSM-IV-TR clinical evaluation included ADOS/ADI-R for ASD. FSIQ means 124.90 vs 127.88 (p=.168, no equivalence); PSI 96.43 vs 110.41 (p=.011 in reported post hoc test); VABS-II composite means 83.56 (n36) vs 108.56 (n25). Socialization means 76.54 vs 105.00, with larger group difference in that domain. Gender imbalance (85% vs 51% male), clinic ascertainment, historical criteria, parent ratings, incomplete non-ASD adaptive responses and differing assessment procedures limit transport; results not diagnostic sensitivities. FSIQ maximum 151 in ASD, but no adult extreme-tail subgroup. This comparison directly supports coexistence of strong measured ability with lower adaptive performance in this youth sample. It does not establish a causal effect of giftedness, universal discrepancy, or adult phenotype. Comparison of the two groups cannot test an IQ×ASD interaction without additional ability strata.

E13.1c — direct IQ-stratified youth evidence. Adaptive Behavior Profiles of Intellectually Gifted Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (2021), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34110306/; https://doi.org/10.1097/DBP.0000000000000907, indexed abstract; DOI open cache miss. Simons Simplex Collection, n51 ASD FSIQ>=130, matched ASD groups IQ85–115 and <70 on age, maternal education, sex. In high-IQ group age correlated negatively with age-standardized socialization (r=-.51) and daily living scores (r=-.48); adaptive communication negatively correlated with age across groups. Cross-sectional associations are not within-person decline, lower standardized scores can coexist with raw skill gains, and no non-ASD gifted comparator. Sample selection and exact adjusted estimates not rechecked. High ability does not guarantee normative adaptive skills. No inference of universal deterioration.

E13.1d — adults and mixed ages. Kraper et al. (2017), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28710532/, indexed abstract, direct challenge. N52 autistic adults without intellectual disability; adaptive scores lag IQ, socialization relatively weak, psychiatric comorbidity correlated with gap. No high-IQ-only or matched gifted comparison verified. Tillmann et al. (2019), https://doi.org/10.1002/aur.2081, indexed abstract and results extracts, publisher403/PMC challenge. N417 autistic children, adolescents and adults, VABS-II; distinguish absolute adaptive scores (lower IQ associated with lower functioning) from relative discrepancy (among those with gap >=1 SD, higher IQ associated with larger gap). The abstract compresses the direction of IQ associations; indexed detailed results clarify contrast. Cross-sectional baseline from longitudinal project; mathematical coupling of IQ with IQ-minus-VABS, selection on difference score, age, support and measurement constrain interpretation. No evidence that greater intelligence worsens adaptive skills. Psychiatric symptoms not uniquely associated in reported multivariable models, unlike simple associations in Kraper; different design/adjustment means not direct contradiction. Magiati et al. (2014), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24424351/, indexed review abstract: 25 longitudinal studies with childhood and adult assessment; heterogeneous trajectories, adaptive functioning improved in most studies, childhood IQ and language often predicted later outcome. Not proof of causal protection; distinguish raw/standardized measures, absolute vs relative outcomes, and cohort differences. Do not combine estimates as independent without cohort audit.

## Analytical transfer rules
1. Represent intellectual strengths, profile scores, developmental neurobehavioral patterns, contextual demands, observed functional outcomes, subjective effort and support needs separately. High ability and substantial support needs can coexist; neither is inferred from the other. Specify score type (FSIQ, index, achievement, estimate, self-report), test edition, confidence interval, ceiling, local norms. Discrepancy is not sufficient evidence of pathology, measurement invalidity, or masking. 
2. Distinguish adaptive capacity, realized performance and opportunity. An ordinary task result may reflect effective support; independent functioning can be hard despite success on structured tests. Ask about actual concrete limitations and resources, not assumed unrealized potential. Do not withdraw supports to test a mechanism. Gap = IQ - adaptive score has mathematical coupling; higher IQ can produce greater gap with the same or better adaptive score. Do not interpret age-standardized decline as absolute skill loss.
3. Competing explanations: task/environment fit, language conventions, sensory conditions, anxiety, ADHD, learning difficulty, sleep or health, preferences, social-communication differences and restricted/repetitive developmental pattern; several can coexist. A narrow interest, literal interpretation, intellectual mismatch, desire for solitude or deliberate scripting is neither necessary nor sufficient on its own. Context-dependent success does not exclude a developmental condition, and difficulty is not sufficient to establish one. Evaluate both core domains, time course, functional impact and disconfirming contexts with clinical specialist when warranted.
4. For hypothesis testing use ordinary voluntary observations across contexts, specify what evidence would revise interpretation: if only mismatched technical vocabulary drives communication difficulty, shared vocabulary should improve mutual comprehension; persistent unrelated developmental/functional patterns would weaken mismatch-only account. If fatigue or noisy environment explains a particular performance problem, comparable conditions should track changes; improvement under accommodation is evidence of local fit not etiological proof. Clinical differential remains professional assessment, not an inference from tests or fictional vignettes.
5. Transfer hierarchy: direct measured adult gifted comparison > adult non-ID or general clinical data as indirect for giftedness > pediatric high ability evidence for developmental possibility only. Seek four-group design for IQ×condition interaction; no exact IQ150–160+ claim based on mean/maximum/case/GAI/achievement. No universal prevalence or phenotype. Preserve strengths and needs without interpreting them as opposite ends of one axis.
