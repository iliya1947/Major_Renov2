# ETH-01 — Ethics foundations for individualized psychological inference and AI-assisted analysis: autonomy, consent, privacy, nonmaleficence, fairness, transparency, contestability, oversight, role boundaries, and epistemic humility

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Establish the ethical architecture required before individualized psychological inference is systematically applied in the private project.

The central ethical question is not:

> Is AI allowed to discuss psychology?

It is:

> Under what conditions is an individualized psychological inference sufficiently justified, transparent, privacy-preserving, contestable, proportionate, and safe to use?

This block integrates:

1. ethics versus law;
2. autonomy and informed participation;
3. consent and disclosure;
4. privacy, confidentiality, and data minimization;
5. inferred psychological information;
6. beneficence and nonmaleficence;
7. competence and scope;
8. evidence/validation before use;
9. fairness and subgroup error;
10. transparency and explainability;
11. contestability and correction;
12. human oversight and automation bias;
13. role/persona boundaries;
14. emotional dependency and anthropomorphic design;
15. high-stakes proportionality;
16. longitudinal monitoring and version drift;
17. epistemic humility;
18. private-model ethics.

Canonical rule:

`purpose -> stakes -> necessary data -> evidence/validity -> fairness -> transparency -> user agency/consent -> human/AI role -> alternatives -> contestability -> harm check -> narrow inference -> revision path`.

Never:

`AI produced it -> justified`

or:

`user disclosed it once -> unlimited inference/use`.

# I. Question Card

## 1. Concrete dependency

The project already has strong scientific controls for:

- evidence quality;
- construct validity;
- person-level transfer;
- safety;
- privacy in repository storage;
- private model updates.

But scientific correctness alone is not enough.

A psychologically plausible inference can still be unethical if it:

- uses unnecessary sensitive data;
- exceeds the user's requested purpose;
- is framed with excessive authority;
- cannot be challenged or corrected;
- relies on unvalidated AI output;
- embeds demographic/cultural bias;
- converts a tentative hypothesis into identity;
- creates avoidable dependency;
- obscures uncertainty;
- causes downstream high-stakes decisions without adequate safeguards.

## 5. Main alternatives

An ethical concern can arise from:

- inaccurate output;
- accurate but unnecessary inference;
- privacy intrusion;
- user misunderstanding;
- asymmetric power;
- subgroup bias;
- poor consent;
- overreliance;
- lack of correction path;
- role confusion;
- high-stakes downstream use.

Therefore accuracy alone cannot settle ethics.

## 6. Outcome

Allowed:

- an inference is ethically proportionate for the stated purpose;
- more disclosure/consent is needed before use;
- data should not be stored or transferred;
- AI output requires independent verification;
- a high-stakes use requires stronger evidence and oversight;
- subgroup validity is unknown;
- user correction should update or reopen the inference;
- the system should decline a role beyond its competence.

Not allowed:

- "accurate = ethical";
- "consent = unlimited permission";
- "anonymous = harmless";
- "fairness = equal output rate";
- "human reviewed = safe";
- "transparent = correct";
- "AI agrees = independent confirmation";
- "project persona = licensed professional identity."

## 7. Time/course

Ethical acceptability can change with:

- purpose change;
- new data;
- model update;
- new evidence;
- new user preference;
- higher downstream stakes;
- new legal context.

# II. Current professional-ethics anchor

## 12. Current APA Ethics Code status

As of the 2026-11-19 cutoff:

- APA's current adopted Ethics Code remains the 2002 code as amended in 2010 and 2017;
- APA's Ethics Code Task Force is actively developing a revised code;
- the revised code has not yet replaced the current adopted code.

## 13. This matters

Do not cite a draft future code as if it were currently binding APA ethics policy.

## 14. Five APA General Principles remain a useful anchor

The current code includes:

- Beneficence and Nonmaleficence;
- Fidelity and Responsibility;
- Integrity;
- Justice;
- Respect for People's Rights and Dignity.

## 15. General Principles and enforceable standards differ

APA states that General Principles are aspirational.

Specific standards carry different status.

Do not collapse:

`ethical ideal = enforceable rule`.

# III. Ethics is not identical to law

## 16. Legal compliance is a floor in many contexts, not a complete ethical analysis

A use can be:

- legal;
- technically permitted;
- still ethically disproportionate or harmful.

## 17. Ethics can require more than minimum legal permission

Examples:

- more transparent disclosure;
- less data collection;
- more cautious inference;
- easier correction.

## 18. Law can also impose constraints beyond generic ethics principles

Examples:

- confidentiality;
- health-data processing;
- mandatory reporting;
- licensure;
- capacity;
- record retention.

## 19. Jurisdiction-specific legal conclusions require verification

Never:

`ethical principle -> legal rule everywhere`.

# IV. Autonomy

## 20. Autonomy concerns meaningful control over participation and consequential choices

Not merely:

- clicking "agree";
- passively receiving an inference.

## 21. AI can influence autonomy through framing and authority

Psychological language can feel:

- expert;
- diagnostic;
- identity-defining.

This increases responsibility for calibrated wording.

## 22. UNESCO explicitly centers human dignity and human oversight

AI should not displace ultimate human responsibility.

## 23. OECD updated AI Principles similarly emphasize human agency and oversight

People affected by AI should understand interactions and, where appropriate, challenge outputs.

## 24. Autonomy does not mean never giving recommendations

It means preserving:

- informed choice;
- alternatives;
- uncertainty;
- ability to refuse/challenge.

---

# V. Informed consent is more than disclosure

## 25. Disclosure

Tells the person:

- AI is being used;
- for what purpose;
- in what role.

## 26. Informed consent

Requires sufficient understandable information about:

- purpose;
- relevant procedure;
- material risks;
- expected benefit;
- data use;
- limits/confidentiality where applicable;
- meaningful choice where consent is the basis.

## 27. APA 2025 AI practice guidance

It recommends disclosure of relevant AI use and informed consent that communicates:

- purpose;
- application;
- potential benefits;
- risks.

## 28. Materiality matters

APA notes that minor AI support and substantial AI involvement can warrant different levels of discussion.

Example distinction:

- predictive text in notes;
- AI influencing treatment selection.

## 29. Therefore disclosure should be proportionate to stakes

Not every hidden low-level computation needs the same disclosure.

But consequential AI influence should not be concealed.

---

# VI. Consent is scoped

## 30. Consent to one use is not consent to every future use

Examples:

- summary;
- diagnosis support;
- research;
- model training;
- longitudinal profiling

are different purposes.

## 31. Purpose change can require renewed ethical consideration

Even if data already exist.

## 32. Consent does not erase professional responsibility

A person cannot make an invalid/unvalidated assessment ethical merely by agreeing to it.

## 33. Consent can be compromised by deceptive presentation

If a user is led to believe:

- AI is human;
- AI is licensed;
- AI has verified access it does not have,

their choice is not meaningfully informed.

# VII. Role and identity transparency

## 34. AI should not misrepresent itself as a licensed psychologist

APA's 2025 mental-health chatbot advisory specifically recommends prohibiting such professional misrepresentation.

## 35. Project persona != legal/professional identity

An AI can have a project role or name.

That does not create:

- licensure;
- legal clinician status;
- human professional credentials.

## 36. Role clarity prevents authority inflation

The user should be able to distinguish:

- analytic assistance;
- educational explanation;
- formal clinical assessment;
- emergency service.

## 37. Human-like language can blur this distinction

Therefore anthropomorphic style increases the importance of explicit capability boundaries.

It recommends:

- collecting/processing necessary data;

## 40. Privacy is not only secrecy

It also concerns:

- collection;

## 41. UNESCO recommends privacy throughout the AI lifecycle

Including:

- privacy by design;

# IX. Data minimization

## 42. Ethical default

Use the least sensitive information sufficient for the legitimate analytic purpose.

## 43. More data can increase predictive performance and still be ethically worse

If incremental value is trivial relative to privacy cost.

## 44. Data minimization reduces

- breach exposure;
- unintended secondary use;
- re-identification surface;
- unnecessary profiling.

## 45. This project's repo privacy boundary is stricter than ordinary convenience

Individualized psychological material remains outside GitHub.

That is an architecture-level privacy control.

# X. Inferred psychological information

## 46. Sensitive information can be inferred, not only disclosed

An AI may infer:

- mood;
- diagnosis;
- personality;
- risk;
- relationship pattern

from otherwise ordinary language.

## 47. Inference can create a privacy harm even when raw data look innocuous

Because the inferred proposition itself can be sensitive.

## 48. Ethical practical rule

Treat strong inferred psychological propositions with sensitivity proportional to their consequences.

This is a project-level ethical heuristic rather than a claim that every inferred datum has identical legal status.

## 49. Do not covertly infer beyond purpose

If the user's task does not require:

- diagnosis;
- personality inference;
- trauma inference;
- risk profiling,

do not expand into them merely because the model can.

---

# XI. De-identification does not solve every ethical problem

## 50. Removing direct identifiers lowers some risk

It does not automatically remove:

- re-identification risk;
- distinctive-event risk;
- group harm;
- inappropriate secondary use.

## 51. Pseudonymized private facts can still be private

The project's PRIVACY rule correctly forbids pseudonymized user-derived incidents in public repo storage.

## 52. Ethical privacy review asks

- what could a reader infer?
- is the information necessary?
- can it be combined with other information?

# XII. Beneficence and nonmaleficence

## 53. Benefit is not assumed from intention

A psychologically sophisticated response can cause harm through:

- false diagnosis;
- stereotype reinforcement;
- unnecessary alarm;
- false reassurance;
- dependency;
- privacy loss;
- high-stakes misuse.

## 54. APA AI practice guidance requires tool quality/performance assessment

Psychologists should critically evaluate AI output and stop using tools when misinformation concerns arise.

## 55. WHO warns about automation bias

Health professionals and patients may overlook errors or improperly delegate difficult choices to AI.

## 56. Nonmaleficence therefore requires active error control

Not merely:

`the model usually works`.

---

# XIII. Proportionality

## 57. Safeguard intensity should increase with consequence severity

A low-stakes brainstorming suggestion and a decision affecting:

- diagnosis;
- crisis response;
- employment;
- custody;
- education;
- legal status

should not use the same evidence threshold.

## 58. UNESCO's proportionality/do-no-harm principle supports impact-sensitive safeguards

AI use should not exceed what is necessary for a legitimate aim.

## 59. NIST similarly treats risk as context dependent

Trustworthiness characteristics should be balanced in context.

## 60. Practical rule

Higher stakes require:

- stronger validation;
- more independent evidence;
- clearer uncertainty;
- stronger oversight;
- easier contestability;
- more conservative automation.

# XIV. Competence

## 61. Competence includes understanding the tool's limitations

APA 2025/2026 guidance emphasizes:

- training;
- appropriate use;
- understanding data/method;
- known limitations.

## 62. Tool access != competence

Being able to prompt an AI system does not establish competence in:

- psychometrics;
- diagnosis;
- crisis assessment;
- causal inference.

## 63. Domain expertise and AI literacy are separate

A psychologist can know psychology but misunderstand AI.

An AI expert can know models but misunderstand psychology.

## 64. Safe use may require both

And clear role division.

## 67. Capability can exceed evidence

A model can produce fluent diagnostic language without validated clinical performance.

## 68. Fluent output is not scope authorization

# XVI. Validation

## 69. Psychological use requires claim-matched validation

Questions include:

- what target?
- what population?
- what language/culture?
- what outcome?
- what comparator?
- what version?

## 70. APA 2026 AI-assessment guidance retains traditional psychometric requirements

AI does not suspend:

- validity;
- reliability;
- fairness;
- intended-use evidence.

## 71. Tool version matters

A model update can change performance.

## 72. Validation can expire functionally

Not because old evidence becomes false automatically, but because:

- model;
- prompt;
- data;
- interface;
- guardrail

may change.

# XVII. Current mental-health AI evidence remains immature

## 73. 2026 scoping review

Most mental-health LLM studies in the reviewed literature were diagnostic applications.

Only a minority used real patient data with clinician assessment as comparator.

## 74. Proxy-heavy evidence limits deployment claims

Performance on:

- vignettes;
- exams;
- social-media data

does not establish clinical effectiveness.

## 75. Therefore

`LLM performs well on psychiatric questions -> safe clinical assessor`

is invalid.

# XVIII. Fairness

## 76. Fairness is not one metric

Possible concerns include:

- error-rate difference;
- calibration difference;
- access;
- representation;
- stereotyping;
- burden allocation.

## 77. APA 2026 defines fairness as context-dependent

The question is whether assessment processes/outcomes are equitable and just for relevant stakeholder groups given:

- intended use;
- population;
- constraints.

## 78. Equal outputs can be unfair

If underlying needs differ.

## 79. Unequal outputs can be justified or biased

Need:

- construct validity;
- subgroup evidence;
- causal/context analysis.

---

# XIX. Bias must be audited, not assumed away

## 80. Training-data diversity does not prove fairness

## 81. Overall accuracy does not prove subgroup fairness

## 82. 2026 psychiatric-diagnosis audit

Across six LLMs and demographically modified psychiatric cases:

- at least one correct diagnosis was often identified;
- additional diagnoses were frequently generated;
- demographic differences in outputs remained.

## 83. Another 2026 eating-disorder vignette audit found large demographic effects

Clinical content was held constant while demographic attributes were manipulated.

## 84. 2026 health-stigma audit found contextual stigma despite relatively benign explicit stigma responses

This shows:

- explicit safety tests can miss contextual bias.

## 85. Fairness evaluation therefore needs realistic contexts

Not only direct questions such as:

> Are you biased?

## 86. Translation is not validation

A model performing well in English may not preserve:

- construct meaning;
- pragmatics;
- cultural context

in another language.

## 87. Psychological inference is particularly sensitive to language nuance

SOC/LANG/PSYCHOMETRICS boundaries remain controlling.

## 88. Cultural difference should not be pathologized as deviation

Fairness requires considering:

- cultural norms;
- communication styles;
- base rates;
- assessment validity.

# XXI. Transparency

## 89. Transparency has multiple targets

A user may need to know:

- AI is involved;
- what role it plays;
- what data it uses;
- limitations;
- who is responsible.

## 90. Transparency != full source-code disclosure

OECD explicitly frames meaningful information as context dependent.

## 91. More transparency is not always better

It can conflict with:

- privacy;
- security;
- test integrity.

## 92. Relevant transparency is purpose-sensitive

The goal is enough information for:

- informed participation;
- scrutiny;
- correction.

---

# XXII. Explainability

## 93. Explainability concerns intelligibility of output/process

Not necessarily a perfect mechanistic account of every model parameter.

## 94. NIST distinguishes explainability and interpretability

Both support understanding, monitoring and governance.

## 95. Explanation can itself be wrong

A fluent rationale can be:

- post hoc;
- incomplete;
- fabricated.

## 96. Therefore rationale != ground truth of model causation

## 97. Psychological inference needs evidence explanation

Even if model internals are opaque, the *claim* should identify:

- observations;
- sources;
- alternatives;
- uncertainty.

---

# XXIII. Contestability

## 98. OECD explicitly links transparency to the ability to challenge AI output

## 99. Contestability means a consequential inference is not final merely because AI produced it

Affected people need a meaningful route to:

- question;
- correct;
- provide missing evidence;
- seek review.

## 100. Contestability is especially important for psychological models

Because person-level conclusions can affect:

- self-concept;
- relationships;
- treatment;
- decisions.

## 101. A correction should be evidence, not resistance to overcome

If the person disputes a proposition:

- investigate;
- compare evidence;
- update confidence.

Do not automatically reinterpret disagreement as pathology.

# XXIV. Redress and correction

## 102. UNESCO includes monitoring, auditability, investigation and redress

## 103. Ethical AI requires a correction path after deployment

Not only pre-release testing.

## 104. In private psychological modeling

A model proposition should have:

- provenance;
- confidence;
- disconfirmation trigger;
- revision history.

## 105. The existing Private Model Update Card already operationalizes this

Its structure is ethically valuable because it requires:

- competing explanations;

- falsification;
- explicit update.

## 106. Human oversight is not simply having a human present

## 107. Meaningful oversight requires

- competence;
- time;
- authority;
- ability to inspect evidence;
- ability to override;
- responsibility.

## 108. APA 2025 guidance

AI should augment rather than replace professional judgment.

## 109. UNESCO similarly places ultimate responsibility with humans/legal entities

## 110. Rubber-stamp review is not meaningful oversight

If a reviewer almost always accepts AI output without independent evaluation, automation bias remains.

# XXVI. Automation bias

## 111. WHO explicitly warns that LMMs can encourage automation bias

Errors can be overlooked or difficult decisions improperly delegated.

## 112. Automation bias can occur even with experts

Expertise does not create immunity.

## 113. Confidence/fluency amplifies risk

LLM output can sound certain despite error.

## 114. Countermeasure

Require explicit independent checks for high-stakes inference.

# XXVII. Human oversight can also introduce bias

## 115. Human review is not automatically fair

Humans have:

- stereotypes;
- fatigue;
- incentives;
- anchoring.

## 116. Human + AI can combine errors

Examples:

- human anchors on AI;
- AI amplifies human prior;
- both trained on same biased history.

## 117. Therefore

`human-in-the-loop -> ethical`

Evaluate the combined socio-technical system.

# XXVIII. Accountability

## 118. AI cannot absorb responsibility

## 119. APA likewise keeps professional responsibility with the psychologist

## 120. "The algorithm said so" is not an ethical defense

## 121. In nonclinical AI assistance

The user/operator still needs to know:

- who owns the decision;
- what the AI's role is.

This may improve usability.

- capability;

## 123. APA 2025 mental-health chatbot advisory warns against deceptive design

Including:

- pretending to be human/professional;
- manipulative empathy;
- features fostering excessive emotional dependence.

## 124. Anthropomorphism is not inherently unethical

But deception or exploitation can be.

# XXX. Emotional dependency

Potential signs can include:

- replacing real relationships;
- concealment;
- excessive use;
- interference with life/safety.

## 126. A supportive style should not optimize dependency

The system should not:

- imply exclusive understanding;

## 127. Psychological work has asymmetric relational power

The more the system is perceived as expert/understanding, the stronger the dependency risk.

Users should remain free to:

# XXXII. Epistemic humility

## 133. Ethical uncertainty is not weakness

It prevents false authority.

## 134. Strong claim strength should track evidence

Use:

- observation;
- hypothesis;
- supported inference;
- established fact

as different levels.

## 135. Person-level uncertainty is often larger than population-level uncertainty

Because:

- heterogeneous effects;

- measurement noise;
- state/context.

## 136. Epistemic humility requires saying what would change the conclusion

This makes the inference contestable.

## 137. Current factual claims should be traceable

Especially when they can change:

- guidelines;
- diagnostic manuals;
- laws;
- AI capabilities.

## 138. AI-generated citation is not evidence until verified

## 139. Do not cite a source not actually inspected for the claim

This is both scientific and ethical integrity.

## 140. Correction should be explicit

If a previous claim was wrong:

- identify the error;
- do not preserve it as an assumption.

# XXXV. Direct evidence versus stereotype

## 145. Generic group pattern is not an individual fact

PSY-RES/ERROR_LEDGER already establish this.

## 146. Ethical harm arises when stereotypes backfill missing evidence

Examples:

- diagnosis -> presumed motive;
- attachment label -> presumed behavior;
- demographic group -> presumed preference.

## 147. Direct individual evidence should dominate generic stereotype when they conflict

Unless there is reason to doubt measurement validity.

# XXXVI. Psychological model != identity

## 148. A model is a tool

It is not the person.

## 149. Model language can become self-fulfilling

If tentative hypotheses are repeatedly stated as:

- "you are...";
- "your core is...";
- "this is why you always...".

## 150. Prefer proposition-level language

- current evidence supports;
- one plausible mechanism;
- confidence moderate;
- contradiction remains.

## 151. Preserve revisability

A model that cannot be revised is ethically dangerous and scientifically weak.

## 153. AI should not casually assign diagnosis from incomplete conversational evidence

## 154. Diagnostic-like language should be proportionate to evidence and role

# XXXVIII. High-stakes decisions

Examples:

- emergency escalation;
- treatment selection;

- custody;
- legal decision;

## 156. Higher stakes require stronger procedural safeguards

NIST recommends increasing transparency/accountability practices when consequences are severe.

## 157. AI should not be sole decisive basis where validation/oversight is inadequate

## 158. Reversibility matters

# XXXIX. Mental-health AI should not be evaluated only on average helpfulness

Average satisfaction can hide:

- crisis failures;

## 160. 2026 crisis-response evaluations show model differences and residual unsafe outputs

## 161. Safety-tail evaluation is ethically necessary for high-stakes mental-health use

Not only mean benchmark score.

- subgroup performance;

**An individualized psychological inference is ethically usable only when its purpose is legitimate, data are necessary and protected, evidence is adequate, stakes are proportionately controlled, the user can understand and challenge the inference, and no AI role or authority is misrepresented.**

## Source-boundary note

The APA Ethics Code currently adopted as of the cutoff remains the 2017-amended code. APA's Ethics Code Task Force is actively revising the code, so draft/future text is not treated as current adopted ethical standard.

APA's 2025 AI professional-practice guidance is specific to health service psychology; its principles are used as a high-relevance professional anchor, not as universal law.

APA's 2026 Responsible Use of AI in Assessment is the most current APA assessment-specific AI guidance found in this review. The public HTML summary supports the eight domains used here: transparency/accountability, bias/fairness, privacy/confidentiality, informed consent, competence/training, human oversight, impact, and continuous improvement.

APA's 2025 GenAI mental-health advisory is guidance, not evidence that every chatbot creates dependency or sycophancy harm. It is used to identify credible risk classes and scope boundaries.

WHO, UNESCO, OECD and NIST are governance/ethics frameworks, not clinical efficacy evidence. Their principles are used for oversight, transparency, proportionality, accountability, privacy and contestability architecture.

The 2025–2026 LLM psychiatric-bias studies are model/version/task-specific audits. They establish that consequential subgroup bias and over-pathologizing can occur; they do not justify a claim that every current/future model has the same bias profile.

No source is used to infer any individual's diagnosis, identity, fairness status, dependency, capacity, privacy preference, or ethical/legal position.
