# ANA-15 — Individual anatomy / anthropometry

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-18**  
Область: 02 — Anatomy

## 0. Decision

Создать structural individuality map, sufficient for exercise individualization without:

- segment-ratio determinism;
- one “normal” joint geometry;
- attachment variant → targeting claim;
- asymmetry → pathology;
- age → fixed tissue phenotype;
- sex/group average → personal-anatomy inference.

## 1. Core project model

1. Segment lengths and proportions vary materially.
2. Joint geometry varies materially.
3. Muscle/tendon attachment and belly/tendon proportions vary.
4. Small bilateral asymmetries can be normal.
5. Maturation and age change average tissue structure heterogeneously.
6. Sex/population group averages do not substitute for individual measurement.
7. Anatomy changes exercise geometry/options, not a single mandatory technique.

Project rule:

**measure the person; use anatomy to constrain options, not to force a stereotype.**

## 2. Segment lengths

Relevant segments:
- upper arm;
- forearm;
- thigh;
- shank;
- trunk;
- hand/foot where task-specific.

Relative lengths can alter:
- joint-center relations;
- segment inclinations;
- reach;
- external moment arms;
- clearance;
- comfortable ROM.

Absolute height alone is insufficient to describe exercise geometry.

## 3. Humerus / forearm ratio

Can affect:
- elbow position;
- wrist/hand reach;
- pressing/pulling geometry;
- curl/extension moment arms.

Project rule:

**no universal humerus:forearm ratio dictates one correct grip or exercise.**

## 4. Femur / tibia ratio

Can affect:
- knee travel;
- hip position;
- trunk inclination;
- squat/lunge depth strategy.

But movement also depends on:
- ankle ROM;
- stance;
- heel elevation;
- load position;
- motor strategy.

Project rule:

**femur:tibia ratio is one contributor, not a squat-technique classifier.**

## 5. Trunk length

Trunk length interacts with lower-limb proportions to influence:
- balance;
- torso inclination;
- reach;
- load-to-hip/knee geometry.

Project rule:

**trunk length must be interpreted as part of a multi-segment system.**

## 6. Pelvic / shoulder-girdle breadth

Relevant structural variables:
- pelvic breadth;
- acetabular spacing/orientation;
- shoulder breadth;
- ribcage width;
- scapular/clavicular geometry.

Can influence:
- stance comfort;
- arm path;
- grip width;
- segment clearance.

Breadth alone does not prescribe technique.

## 7. Joint geometry

Healthy humans show substantial variation in:
- femoral version;
- acetabular orientation;
- spinopelvic relation;
- glenoid/scapular morphology;
- knee/trochlear geometry;
- ankle/foot morphology.

Reported “normal” angles may depend on measurement method.

Project rule:

**one textbook angle is not universal personal anatomy.**

## 8. Joint geometry and comfortable path

Joint shape can alter:
- bony clearance;
- end-range orientation;
- comfortable rotation;
- stance/path options.

But:
- soft tissue;
- motor control;
- task setup

also matter.

Project rule:

**joint variation can change the option space without automatically establishing pathology or contraindication.**

## 9. Muscle attachment variability

Origins/insertions can vary normally.

Examples include:
- pes-anserinus variants;
- tibialis-anterior insertion patterns;
- pectoralis-minor variants;
- adductor-magnus architecture;
- accessory slips/tendons.

Project rule:

**atlas attachments are modal descriptions, not exact personal maps.**

Variant anatomy alone does not prove exercise targeting.

## 10. Muscle-belly / tendon proportions

Individuals can differ in:
- free-tendon length;
- intramuscular tendon length;
- muscle-belly length;
- MTJ position;
- fascicle architecture.

These may influence:
- visible contour;
- excursion;
- MTU geometry.

Project rule:

**visible “short/long muscle belly” is not a direct measure of force capacity or hypertrophy potential.**

## 11. Attachment site vs training-induced shape

Training can alter:
- muscle size;
- regional size;
- architecture.

Training does not ordinarily relocate:
- bone origin;
- tendon insertion.

Project rule:

**anatomical attachment constrains shape; training does not move insertions to reshape a muscle arbitrarily.**

## 12. Carrying angle

Carrying angle varies with:
- population/group;
- side;
- measurement method;
- elbow position.

It changes as the elbow flexes.

Project rule:

**carrying angle is not one fixed universal value and does not by itself diagnose deformity or dictate grip.**

## 13. Right/left asymmetry

Normal side differences can occur in:
- bone geometry;
- segment dimensions;
- muscle size;
- tendon morphology;
- ROM;
- dominant-limb adaptations.

Project rule:

**small asymmetry is not pathology by default.**

Interpretation requires:
- reliability;
- magnitude;
- symptoms/function;
- history;
- stability/progression.

## 14. Exercise geometry — valid inference

Anatomy can support conditional statements such as:
- segment proportions may change trunk/knee strategy;
- longer forearms can change reach/elbow position;
- breadth can alter clearance;
- joint morphology can change comfortable stance/rotation;
- tendon/belly proportions can change contour/MTU geometry.

Anatomy alone cannot establish:
- one universal stance;
- one universal grip;
- one perfect squat;
- injury risk from deviation;
- hypertrophy superiority of an exercise.

Project rule:

**geometry informs options; MEC/TEC evidence selects among them.**

## 15. Skeletal growth / maturation

Longitudinal bone growth occurs through epiphyseal growth plates.

Growth plates:
- are specialized cartilage structures;
- have zonal organization;
- are progressively replaced by bone with maturation;
- eventually fuse.

Project rule:

**skeletal maturity is not defined by one universal chronological age.**

Pediatric loading/safety → MED/PRO.

## 16. Growth-plate boundary

An immature physis is structurally different from adult fused bone.

ANA-15 does not infer:
- resistance training inherently damages growth plates;
- one age universally prohibits strength training.

Those are MED/PRO evidence questions.

## 17. Age-related muscle structure

Average aging trends can include:
- lower muscle volume/CSA;
- myofiber atrophy;
- changes in fast/type-II populations;
- fatty/fibrotic changes in some contexts;
- reduced regenerative capacity.

But the phenotype is heterogeneous.

Important retained boundary:

**intramuscular collagen accumulation is not a universal feature of healthy aging.**

## 18. Age-related tendon structure

Age-associated tendon changes can involve:
- cell responsiveness;
- ECM organization;
- cross-linking;
- regeneration;
- mechanical properties.

Direction/magnitude vary by:
- tendon;
- loading history;
- health;
- measurement method.

Project rule:

**do not describe all tendons as simply becoming uniformly stiffer or looser with age.**

## 19. Age-related bone structure

Average changes can include:
- lower bone mass after peak;
- trabecular deterioration;
- cortical porosity;
- altered remodeling.

Project rule:

**chronological age is not a direct personal bone-quality measurement.**

## 20. Sex-related structural differences

Group-level differences can exist in:
- body size;
- long-bone dimensions;
- articular breadths;
- pelvic morphology;
- shoulder/scapular dimensions;
- proportions.

Magnitude depends on:
- population;
- allometry/body size;
- secular trends;
- measurement definition.

Project rule:

**sex category is not a substitute for measuring the individual.**

## 21. Population / secular variation

Anthropometric proportions vary across:
- populations;
- cohorts;
- time;
- environments;
- measurement protocols.

Project rule:

**reference tables describe groups, not personal truth.**

## 22. Normal variation vs pathology

Normal variation can include:
- segment ratios;
- joint version;
- attachment variants;
- carrying angle;
- side asymmetry;
- body proportions.

Potential MED relevance depends on context such as:
- pain;
- neurological signs;
- progressive functional loss;
- instability;
- trauma/deformity history;
- large/progressive unexplained asymmetry;
- clinically significant ROM loss.

Project rule:

**variation ≠ pathology.**

## 23. Practical individualization priority

For later person-specific work, direct measurements generally have priority over demographic inference.

Useful sequence:
1. standing/sitting height;
2. segment lengths;
3. relevant breadths;
4. ROM;
5. symptom-free comfortable positions;
6. standardized photo/video;
7. specialized imaging only if a real decision requires it.

Project rule:

**measure what changes the decision.**

## 25. Canonical project rules

1. Anthropometry changes geometry, not destiny.
2. No single ratio uniquely determines technique.
3. Healthy joint geometry varies.
4. Measurement method can change reported “normal” values.
5. Attachment/belly/tendon variation does not prove targeting.
6. Small bilateral asymmetry can be normal.
7. Skeletal maturity is not one universal age.
8. Age changes tissue structure heterogeneously.
9. Sex/population averages do not substitute for individual measurement.
10. Variation alone is not pathology.
11. Anatomy informs options; downstream biomechanics/technique evidence selects among them.

## 26. Evidence / confidence

### HIGH
- segment-proportion variability;
- joint-shape variability;
- method dependence of some joint measurements;
- attachment/belly/tendon variability;
- growth-plate role in longitudinal growth;
- heterogeneous age-related structural change;
- group-level sex differences with overlap;
- non-diagnostic normal-variation boundary.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT
- exact effect size of one ratio on a given exercise;
- exact functional effect of a specific attachment variant;
- exact age-related tendon phenotype;
- exact population-level sex difference for a specific ratio.
