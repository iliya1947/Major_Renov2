# 2. Canonical scientific target

Oral History asks:

**How do people who directly lived through a past event, period, institution, community or practice remember, interpret and give meaning to that past, and how can their recorded testimony be critically contextualized and preserved as a historical primary source for present and future users?**

### Canonical project representation

historical question
+
preliminary documentary/context research
+
narrator selection
+
recorded in-depth interview
+
dialogic/co-created testimony
+
memory as evidence and meaning
+
critical verification/contextualization
+
narrator review / release / restrictions
+
preservation / metadata / archive
+
access and future reuse.

### Critical rule

Oral History is not simply:

> “ask someone what happened years ago.”

The durable historical-source lifecycle is methodology-level.

---

# 3. Current OHA scholarly definition

Current OHA evaluation guidance defines scholarly oral history as a:

- recorded;
- in-depth;
- research-informed interview;

whose recording is:

- preserved;
- made accessible to others;
- interpreted.

OHA explicitly treats oral history as both:

1. **the interview process**;
2. **the recorded product**.

### Critical rule

An unrecorded retrospective interview can be valuable qualitative data.

It does not satisfy the strongest current scholarly Oral History architecture.

---

# 4. Dual scientific product

Oral History often produces two outputs simultaneously:

## Immediate research output

Examples:

- article;
- book;
- exhibition;
- historical analysis.

## Durable primary source

The interview itself is preserved so that:

- other historians;
- communities;
- future researchers;

can reinterpret it later.

### Project implication

This dual-output architecture distinguishes Oral History from many qualitative interview methods where only the researcher's analysis is intended to survive.

---

# 5. Narrator terminology

Current OHA uses **narrator** rather than merely:

- interviewee;
- subject.

The term recognizes that the person:

- has agency;
- constructs an account;
- is not merely a passive source from whom data are extracted.

### Project rule

Store the authors' terminology.

When current OHA-compatible work uses narrator, preserve that distinction.

---

# 6. Narrator ≠ passive historical sensor

A narrator provides:

- memory;
- interpretation;
- perspective;
- meaning;
- emotion;
- retrospective understanding.

The oral historian is not downloading an untouched record from the narrator.

### Critical rule

Testimony is:

> historically situated and co-produced.

This does not make it useless as evidence.

It changes how evidence must be interpreted.

---

# 7. Interview is co-created

OHA and current sport-history methodology both treat Oral History as dialogic/co-creative.

The resulting account depends partly on:

- interviewer questions;
- relationship;
- language;
- setting;
- power;
- project purpose;
- narrator expectations;
- historical moment of interviewing.

### Critical rule

Researcher/interviewer influence is not noise that can simply be removed.

It is part of source provenance.

---

# 8. Historical question is central

Oral History normally seeks knowledge about:

- the past;
- historical change;
- lived historical conditions;
- communities/events not adequately represented in written archives.

### Example

Fitness/sport-relevant:

> How did women experience community weight training before commercial gyms became widely accessible?

This is an Oral History candidate.

By contrast:

> How do current gym users experience belonging today?

is more naturally phenomenology/QD/ethnography unless the historical dimension is central.

---

# 9. Preliminary historical research is required

Current OHA Best Practices explicitly recommends background research in:

- primary sources;
- secondary sources;
- community knowledge;
- relevant biographical/context sources;

before interviewing.

### Scientific function

Background research enables:

- informed questions;
- chronological orientation;
- identification of contradictions;
- follow-up;
- later contextualization.

### Critical rule

Oral History is not methodologically strongest when interviewer intentionally knows nothing about the past.

---

# 10. Narrator sampling

Potential narrators should be selected because their experiences are relevant to the historical question.

OHA also recommends seeking diverse voices where appropriate.

### Possible sampling logics

- direct witnesses;
- participants in an institution/event;
- different roles;
- marginalized/under-recorded voices;
- contrasting perspectives;
- snowball/community referral.

### Critical rule

Sampling is not designed to estimate population prevalence.

The goal is historical/experiential coverage and source diversity.

---

# 11. “History from below”

Oral History has strong historical connections to:

- social history;
- history from below;
- recovery of voices absent from institutional archives.

Sport-history literature particularly highlights:

- women;
- non-elite participants;
- marginalized communities;
- local clubs;
- athletes whose experiences were not formally documented.

### Critical rule

Marginalized voices do not become automatically “more factual.”

Their testimony still requires source criticism while also correcting archival silence.

---

# 12. Oral History and archive silence

Traditional sport archives can privilege:

- governing bodies;
- elite men;
- newspapers;
- official statistics;
- institutional records.

Oral History can add:

- embodied experience;
- informal practice;
- gendered experience;
- local sport culture;
- everyday training life.

### Scientific value

It can change what counts as historically visible evidence.

---

# 13. Memory is evidence

Oral History necessarily relies on memory.

Memory can provide evidence about:

- what narrator recalls happened;
- what mattered;
- perceived motivations;
- meanings at the time;
- meanings reconstructed later;
- social/cultural memory.

### Critical rule

Memory should not be reduced to:

> accurate vs inaccurate.

Its selectivity and reconstruction can itself be historically meaningful.

---

# 14. Memory is not a perfect event recorder

Retrospective memory can be affected by:

- forgetting;
- telescoping;
- hindsight;
- subsequent events;
- identity change;
- public narratives;
- repeated retelling.

### Critical rule

When claim = historical fact,

memory alone is weaker than:

> memory + corroboration/contextual evidence.

---

# 15. Factual error can itself be meaningful

If a narrator's memory conflicts with documentary chronology, two different questions exist:

1. **What most likely happened?**
2. **Why is it remembered this way now?**

### Project rule

Do not “correct away” memory as irrelevant.

But also do not report a contradicted recollection as established historical fact.

---

# 16. Factual verification

Current OHA Best Practices explicitly recommends verification where information is presented as factual.

Useful corroboration sources:

- newspapers;
- training logs;
- competition records;
- minutes;
- correspondence;
- photographs;
- medical/club records;
- other narrators;
- official results;
- archival documents.

### Critical rule

Corroboration serves historical factual claims.

It does not make narrator meaning secondary.

Both can be preserved.

---

# 17. Contradictory testimony

Two narrators can remember the same event differently.

Possible explanations:

- different vantage points;
- role differences;
- later identity;
- conflict;
- memory reconstruction;
- power.

### Weak handling

> select one as the “true” story without evidence.

### Strong handling

Distinguish:

- corroborated facts;
- unresolved factual disagreement;
- interpretive differences;
- historically meaningful conflict in memory.

---

# 18. Current OHA lifecycle

OHA Best Practices identifies four key elements:

1. **Preparation**;
2. **Interviewing**;
3. **Preservation**;
4. **Access**.

### Critical rule

Preservation/access are not optional afterthoughts in canonical Oral History.

Planning for them begins before interview.

---

# 19. Preparation

Preparation includes:

- define project purpose;
- historical/background research;
- identify narrators;
- identify repository;
- define recording process;
- consent;
- explain future uses;
- discuss review/release;
- plan preservation/access;
- prepare open-ended guide.

### Scientific function

The oral history source is designed with its future evidentiary life in mind.

---

# 20. Repository planning begins before recording

Current OHA guidance recommends identifying an appropriate repository during initial project preparation when feasible.

Repository should have:

- preservation capability;
- ability to enforce agreements/restrictions;
- access infrastructure;
- appropriate mission/context.

### Project rule

A project that intends archival Oral History but waits until after interviewing to consider preservation has weaker lifecycle planning.

---

# 21. Interviewing

The interview should generally use:

- open-ended questions;
- active listening;
- follow-up;
- clarification;
- elaboration;
- reflection.

Interviewer should balance:

- project aims;
- narrator's own priorities.

### Critical rule

A rigid questionnaire optimized only for standardized responses is usually poorly aligned with Oral History.

---

# 22. Narrator can redirect relevance

Current OHA guidance recommends allowing narrator to raise issues beyond the narrow project focus when those issues are meaningful to their account.

### Scientific value

Unexpected material can expose:

- archival blind spots;
- categories researcher did not anticipate;
- historically relevant context.

### Rule

Open-endedness does not mean lack of preparation.

Good Oral History combines:

> informed interviewer + narrator agency.

---

# 23. Recording metadata

OHA recommends contextual lead-in information such as:

- narrator/interviewer;
- date;
- general location;
- subject.

### Broader metadata

Archival guidance also distinguishes:

- administrative metadata;
- descriptive metadata;
- technical metadata;
- preservation metadata;
- rights/access metadata.

### Critical rule

Metadata are part of source provenance.

Without them, future interpretation becomes substantially weaker.

---

# 24. The original recording matters

Oral History is fundamentally an audiovisual/oral source.

The recording preserves information that a transcript cannot fully preserve:

- voice;
- pace;
- pauses;
- emotion;
- emphasis;
- laughter;
- hesitation;
- interaction.

### Critical rule

Transcript ≠ complete substitute for original recording.

---

# 25. Transcript is primarily an access/analysis aid

Current archival guidance treats transcripts, indexes and time-coded guides as tools improving:

- discoverability;
- accessibility;
- searching;
- analysis.

### Project rule

Store separately:

- original audio/video;
- transcript;
- edited transcript;
- index/abstract.

Do not assume these are evidentially identical.

---

# 26. Transcript transformation

Transcription necessarily makes decisions about:

- punctuation;
- pauses;
- false starts;
- dialect;
- nonverbal events;
- overlap;
- inaudible speech.

### Critical rule

A polished transcript can accidentally make spoken memory look more:

- coherent;
- grammatical;
- linear;

than the recording.

For analysis involving performance/hesitation/emotion, recording should remain accessible.

---

# 27. Editing transcript vs altering source

Narrator review may lead to:

- correction;
- clarification;
- deletion;
- restriction.

### Project rule

Preserve metadata documenting:

- what version exists;
- what was changed;
- whether original recording remains restricted/preserved;
- which version is authoritative for public use.

Do not silently overwrite provenance.

---

# 28. Preservation

OHA recommends:

- preservation planning from project conception;
- archival-quality file management;
- redundant copies;
- nonproprietary/cross-platform formats where feasible;
- migration planning for obsolescence;
- preserving associated documents/metadata.

### Critical rule

A USB drive in the researcher's desk is not a robust long-term preservation strategy.

---

# 29. Preservation is scientific, not only technical

Preservation allows:

- audit;
- reinterpretation;
- secondary research;
- methodological critique;
- future historical questions.

### Project implication

Loss of the original recording can remove evidence that cannot be reconstructed from a paper's quotes.

---

# 30. Access

Oral History commonly aims at future public/scholarly access, subject to:

- narrator permission;
- restrictions;
- ethical/legal constraints.

### Critical rule

Archive deposit ≠ unrestricted public release automatically.

Access can be:

- immediate;
- delayed;
- limited;
- conditional;
- closed.

---

# 31. Narrator review

Current OHA ethics strongly recommends that narrator, when possible, can review:

- recording;
- transcript;
- or both;

before public release/use.

Possible actions:

- clarify;
- correct;
- redact;
- restrict;
- delay release;
- refuse public release.

### Critical rule

This is not ordinary “member checking” aimed only at validating researcher's themes.

It is partly about:

- rights;
- stewardship;
- public record;
- future use.

---

# 32. Narrator review ≠ guarantee of historical truth

Narrator approval can establish:

- consent;
- desired representation;
- correction of obvious errors.

It cannot prove that every historical claim is factually accurate.

### Rule

Historical verification remains a separate operation.

---

# 33. Informed consent and release are different

Informed consent concerns agreement to:

- participate;
- understand risks/process.

A release/rights agreement concerns:

- preservation;
- publication;
- public access;
- reuse;
- copyright/permissions;
- restrictions.

### Critical rule

A generic research consent form does not necessarily settle future archival/public-use rights.

---

# 34. Rights and copyright require explicit documentation

OHA materials emphasize:

- rights agreements;
- legal releases;
- access restrictions;
- use permissions.

Current OHA materials also describe Oral History as co-created between narrator and interviewer.

### Legal caveat

Exact copyright ownership and transfer rules are jurisdiction-dependent and agreement-dependent.

### Project rule

Never infer ownership from methodology label.

Extract:

- who holds which rights;
- what was transferred/licensed;
- restrictions;
- jurisdiction/institution if relevant.

---

# 35. Public release can become effectively irreversible

Once material is:

- publicly archived;
- downloaded;
- quoted;
- copied;
- distributed online;

full withdrawal may become impossible.

### Ethical implication

Narrators should understand this before public release.

### Project rule

Withdrawal before access and withdrawal after public dissemination are different practical states.

---

# 36. Restriction may be ethically appropriate

Some projects involve:

- vulnerable people;
- political risk;
- employment risk;
- stigmatized identities;
- surveillance.

Appropriate preservation may include:

- delayed access;
- partial restriction;
- anonymization/pseudonym;
- controlled repository access.

### Critical rule

The ideal of open historical access does not override narrator/community safety.

---

# 37. Anonymity is often difficult

Oral history frequently concerns:

- specific people;
- recognizable events;
- institutions;
- public roles.

Even pseudonyms may not prevent re-identification.

### Project rule

Do not promise absolute anonymity when context makes it unrealistic.

This is especially important in:

- small teams;
- clubs;
- elite sport;
- coaching networks.

---

# 38. Future reuse ethics

Unlike many one-study qualitative datasets, oral history is often intentionally created for **future unknown users**.

Therefore consent/release should address:

- future scholarship;
- exhibition;
- broadcast;
- online access;
- educational use;
- derivative works where relevant.

### Critical rule

Future reuse is part of design, not an accidental secondary-data possibility.

---

# 39. Archival description is interpretive

Cataloging and metadata choices influence:

- discoverability;
- classification;
- how future users understand the interview.

### Risk

Archive description can:

- stereotype;
- erase preferred identity terms;
- reproduce institutional bias.

### Current OHA principle

Description/access should respect narrator personhood and privacy.

---

# 40. Oral History and community stewardship

Projects may involve:

- community archives;
- museums;
- local groups;
- Indigenous/community-specific knowledge governance.

### Critical rule

Institutional archive deposit is not always ethically superior.

Some communities may require:

- local control;
- culturally specific access restrictions;
- nontraditional stewardship.

---

# 41. Research ethics review boundary

Oral History can sit at the boundary between:

- historical scholarship;
- human-subjects research;
- public humanities;
- archival creation.

### Project rule

Whether formal institutional ethics/IRB review is required depends on:

- jurisdiction;
- institution;
- project purpose;
- legal/regulatory framework.

**Информация не проверена** for any universal cross-jurisdiction rule.

Do not claim that Oral History is always exempt or always regulated as human-subject research.

---

# 42. Oral History vs generic qualitative interview

## Generic qualitative interview

Primary product:
- data for current study.

May not require:
- historical question;
- archival preservation;
- future access;
- legal release for public source use.

## Oral History

Primary products:
- current interpretation;
- durable recorded historical primary source.

### Critical rule

A retrospective interview is not Oral History automatically.

---

# 43. Oral History vs narrative inquiry

There is substantial overlap:

- stories;
- temporality;
- co-construction;
- identity;
- memory.

## Narrative inquiry

Primary analytic object:
- story;
- plot;
- narrative identity;
- telling/performance.

## Oral History

Primary methodology adds:
- historical question/context;
- recorded testimony as primary source;
- source criticism/corroboration;
- preservation;
- access;
- release/rights;
- future reuse.

### Rule

Narrative analysis can be used **inside** an Oral History project.

Preserve both layers when both are explicit.

---

# 44. Oral History vs life history

The boundaries overlap and terminology varies.

A useful project distinction:

## Life history

Usually emphasizes:
- person's life trajectory;
- biography across life course;
- self-development over time.

## Oral History

Often begins from:
- historical event;
- institution;
- community;
- period;
- social change.

It may interview only the relevant segment of a person's life.

### Critical caveat

“Oral life history” is a legitimate hybrid/subtype.

### Project rule

Do not force a false binary.

Store:

- full-life vs delimited historical focus;
- historical/archive architecture;
- narrative/biographical analytic layer.

---

# 45. Oral History vs oral tradition

## Oral History

Typically:
- individual/dialogic recorded testimony;
- interviewer–narrator encounter;
- contemporary act of remembering.

## Oral tradition

Typically:
- stories/knowledge transmitted collectively across generations;
- may not originate in a single witness's personal memory.

### Rule

Oral tradition can be material within Oral History.

They are not synonyms.

---

# 46. Oral History vs journalism interview

Both can:

- record testimony;
- investigate past events.

But scholarly Oral History normally adds:

- extensive preparatory research;
- preservation plan;
- provenance/metadata;
- release/access framework;
- source criticism;
- historical interpretation;
- future archival use.

### Rule

A published interview transcript is not automatically an Oral History project.

---

# 47. Oral History vs testimony

Testimony can be:

- legal;
- therapeutic;
- political;
- journalistic;
- research-related.

Oral History specifically situates testimony within a historical and archival methodology.

### Rule

Testimony is a data/source type, not necessarily a design label.

---

# 48. Oral History vs case study

A project can focus on one:

- club;
- athlete;
- team;
- institution.

That does not automatically make it case study.

### Hybrid example

bounded history of one sport club
+
oral-history interviews
+
documents.

### Project rule

If the **bounded case** organizes the inquiry, preserve case-study parent layer.

If the primary product is recorded historical testimony/source creation, preserve Oral History layer.

---

# 49. Oral History vs ethnography

Oral History can include:

- community engagement;
- immersion;
- observation.

But:

## Ethnography

usually targets:
- contemporary culture/practice;
- field participation/observation.

## Oral History

targets:
- past experience/memory;
- historical source creation;
- archival preservation.

### Rule

Community immersion does not erase the distinction.

---

# 50. Data analysis is plural

Oral History does not prescribe one single downstream analysis.

Possible approaches:

- historical source criticism;
- thematic analysis;
- narrative analysis;
- discourse analysis;
- biographical analysis;
- chronological reconstruction;
- comparative historical analysis.

### Critical rule

The analytic method should match the claim.

The methodology remains Oral History because of its source-production/lifecycle architecture.

---

# 51. Historical reconstruction vs memory analysis

Two legitimate Oral History analytic targets:

## Reconstruction

Question:
> What most likely occurred?

Requires stronger:
- corroboration;
- source comparison;
- chronology checking.

## Memory/meaning analysis

Question:
> How is the past remembered and what does that memory mean now?

Factual discrepancy can itself be analytic material.

### Project rule

Extract which target the study is using.

Do not judge memory-focused work by reconstruction-only criteria.

---

# 52. Historical factual claim ladder

## Claim level 1

“Narrator remembers X.”

Supported by:
- recorded testimony.

## Claim level 2

“Several narrators independently remember X.”

Stronger but still:
- shared recollection.

## Claim level 3

“X most likely occurred.”

Needs:
- corroborating historical evidence;
- source criticism;
- contextual consistency.

### Critical rule

Multiple memories are not automatically independent if participants share later collective narratives.

---

# 53. Memory convergence can reflect shared culture

Agreement among narrators can come from:

- common direct experience;
- later conversations;
- media narratives;
- institutional commemorations;
- community mythology.

### Rule

Convergence increases interest but does not automatically establish independent factual confirmation.

---

# 54. Historical silence is also evidence

Narrators may avoid or omit:

- injury;
- doping;
- abuse;
- discrimination;
- financial conflict;
- institutional failure.

### Possible meanings

- forgetting;
- privacy;
- trauma;
- loyalty;
- cultural taboo;
- legal risk;
- narrative irrelevance.

### Critical rule

Silence is analytically suggestive but not self-explanatory.

Do not assign motive without evidence.

---

# 55. Interviewer expertise and positionality

Good oral historian needs enough context to:

- recognize significance;
- probe;
- detect chronological problems;
- understand terminology.

In sport history, insider expertise can help interpret:

- competition systems;
- coaching lineages;
- training culture.

### Risk

Insider can:

- assume shared knowledge;
- skip explanation;
- reproduce sport myths.

### Rule

Expertise and reflexivity are both required.

---

# 56. Sport and physical-culture relevance

Sport history literature establishes Oral History as a mature methodology for studying:

- athletes;
- coaches;
- clubs;
- teams;
- governing bodies;
- gender;
- race;
- sexuality;
- class;
- disability;
- local/community sport;
- physical culture.

### Scientific value

It can recover forms of sporting life poorly represented by:

- official records;
- newspapers;
- statistics.

---

# 57. Sport exemplar — women and archive silence

Sport-history reviews emphasize that traditional sporting archives have often centered:

- men;
- elite/public institutional records.

Oral History has become particularly important for reconstructing:

- women's sport;
- everyday participation;
- experiences omitted from formal collections.

### Methodological lesson

Oral History can function both as:

1. research method;
2. intervention in the archive.

---

# 58. Judo exemplar — historical genealogy

Nunes/Rubio used Oral History with:

- Brazilian Olympic judo medalists;
- their coaches;

and combined interviews with documentary material to reconstruct coaching/genealogical transmission.

### Methodological lesson

This is directly relevant to:

- athlete/coaching lineage;
- training-knowledge transmission;
- sport genealogy.

### Critical strength

The study did not rely on interviews alone; reported accounts were interpreted together with historical records.

---

# 59. Judo methodological development

Nunes/Martins later proposed sport-specific Oral History refinements based on Brazilian/Portuguese elite judo work, including:

- narrator selection;
- immersion in sport community;
- checking historical records;
- narrative markers;
- video analysis beyond transcript;
- photos/historical records;
- sport family trees.

### Project lesson

Sport Oral History can integrate:

recorded testimony
+
visual/documentary archives
+
community knowledge.

---

# 60. Fitness / physical-culture candidate questions

Oral History is appropriate for questions such as:

- How did bodybuilding culture in a city change from the 1980s to the 2000s?
- How did women enter historically male-dominated strength gyms?
- How were training methods transmitted through coaching lineages before digital media?
- How did rehabilitation practice for athletes change after a specific institutional reform?
- How did disabled athletes experience access to sport before current inclusion policies?
- How did older coaches understand the introduction of S&C or sport science into their sport?

### Critical boundary

It is not appropriate for:

> Which training method currently causes more hypertrophy?

That requires experimental/causal evidence.

---

# 61. Oral History and training knowledge

Oral testimony can reconstruct:

- what coaches believed;
- how methods were taught;
- how practices spread;
- how athletes experienced training.

It cannot independently establish:

- physiological effectiveness of those historical methods.

### Rule

Historical belief/practice
≠
biological efficacy.

---

# 62. Historical injury and health claims

Narrators may recall:

- injuries;
- symptoms;
- treatment;
- diagnoses.

### Critical rule

These can support:

- historical experience;
- perceived medical practice.

They do not automatically establish retrospective clinical diagnoses without records.

---

# 63. Ethical risk in elite sport

Elite sport Oral History can create high re-identification risk because:

- events are public;
- rosters are small;
- coaching relationships are known;
- competition timelines are searchable.

### Project rule

Pseudonymization may be ineffective.

Consent/release/access restrictions should be calibrated accordingly.

---

# 64. Institutional loyalty and retaliation

Narrators may face consequences when discussing:

- abuse;
- doping;

# 66. Adequacy / stopping logic

There is no universal Oral History participant N.

Adequacy depends on:

- historical question;
- number of relevant witnesses;
- diversity of roles;
- archival gaps;
- saturation/coverage of historical perspectives;
- time period;
- availability/survival of narrators.

### Critical rule

A rare surviving witness can be scientifically valuable even as N=1.

Historical source significance is not determined by sample size alone.

---

# 67. Representativeness boundary

Oral History samples are usually not probability samples.

Therefore:

10 of 12 narrators reporting X

does not establish:

> 83% prevalence in the historical population.

It supports:

> X was reported by 10 of the 12 interviewed narrators.

### Rule

Historical coverage ≠ population prevalence.

---

# 68. Generalization

Oral History supports:

- historical interpretation;
- source-based contextual claims;
- reconstruction;
- memory/meaning analysis;
- transfer of insight to similar historical settings.

It does not support statistical population generalization by itself.

---

# 69. Causal claim boundary

Narrators often explain:

> “that coaching change caused our success.”

Oral History can analyze:

- their causal belief;
- historical sequence;
- supporting context.

It cannot by itself estimate:

- causal treatment effect;
- counterfactual performance gain.

### Rule

Narrated historical causality
≠
causal identification.
