## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a sufficiently accurate model of neural adaptation to resistance/strength training for later technique, testing and programming without:

- treating all early strength gain as “purely neural”;
- assuming neural adaptation is one mechanism or one anatomical site;
- assuming motor-unit recruitment thresholds or firing rates must change in one universal direction;
- equating surface-EMG amplitude with neural drive;
- assuming antagonist coactivation always decreases with training;
- treating intermuscular coordination as a fixed “efficiency” score;
- ignoring task-specific skill and test familiarity in early strength gain;
- treating one TMS marker as proof of a cortical mechanism;
- treating one H-reflex/V-wave marker as proof of a spinal mechanism;
- assuming neural adaptations are retained or lost at one common rate;
- assuming rapid retraining proves a specific neural “muscle-memory” mechanism.

### Question type

Human chronic neuromuscular adaptation to resistance/strength training.

### Population/context

Healthy adults are the main transfer target. Evidence from older adults is used only when it clarifies mechanism and is labeled as population-specific.

### Critical outcomes

- maximal voluntary strength;
- motor-unit recruitment/discharge behavior;
- neural drive;
- agonist-antagonist/intermuscular coordination;
- task-specific strength transfer;
- corticospinal/cortical markers;
- spinal/motoneuron markers;
- retention/detraining/retraining.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 12 exact Section 6 items must be covered at content level with explicit measurement boundaries inherited from NEU-02/05 and detraining/retraining boundaries inherited from PHY-18. Where evidence does not support a universal direction of adaptation, the project rule must preserve task/protocol/population specificity rather than invent a single mechanism.

## 4. Core adaptation model

Strength training changes performance through interacting adaptations:

`task exposure / practice`
+
`motor-unit population control`
+
`descending drive and inhibition`
+
`intermuscular coordination`
+
`muscle / tendon / architecture / technique`
→
`task-specific strength and rapid-force performance`.

There is no single “neural adaptation” variable.

The relative contribution changes with:

- training age;
- training task;
- contraction mode;
- load and movement intent;
- testing task;
- muscle group;
- duration;
- measurement method.

Project rule:

**neural adaptation is a distributed, task-specific change in motor control, not one switch located in cortex or spinal cord.**

## 5. Early strength gain without large hypertrophy

Short-term strength gains can occur before large measurable hypertrophy.

The current J Physiol review (PMID 41361993) places early gains alongside supraspinal, spinal and motoneuron adaptations. Direct short-term motor-unit work (PMID 30727028) demonstrates force gain with altered recruitment thresholds and discharge rates after four weeks of isometric training.

However, “early strength = neural only” is too strong because early improvement can also contain:

- task learning/familiarization;
- technique changes;
- altered coordination;
- small or hard-to-detect muscle changes;
- measurement/test-specific learning.

Canonical rule:

**early strength gain can be predominantly neural/skill-mediated before large hypertrophy is detectable, but it is not proof of a purely neural mechanism.**

Confidence: **HIGH for early non-hypertrophic contribution; MODERATE for exact share in a specific exercise/person.**

---

## 6. Motor-unit recruitment adaptation

The 2019 longitudinal motor-unit study (PMID 30727028) tracked the same tibialis-anterior motor units and found lower normalized recruitment thresholds after four weeks of isometric strength training.

This is direct evidence that recruitment behavior can adapt.

But the 2024 specificity review (PMID 38686581) and 2026 training-comparison work (PMID 42323749) show that motor-unit behavior depends on:

- training task;
- tested contraction;
- force level;
- hypertrophic change;
- motor-unit population sampled.

Therefore the project does **not** retain:

> training universally recruits “more motor units” at every submaximal force;

or:

> recruitment thresholds always shift downward.

Canonical rule:

**strength training can reorganize recruitment thresholds and population recruitment behavior, but the direction and observable magnitude are task- and measurement-specific.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH.**

---

## 7. Firing-rate adaptation

Longitudinal tracking in PMID 30727028 found increased discharge rates during submaximal plateau contractions after short-term training.

The 2026 young/older study (PMID 41823343) also linked strength gains to increased motor-unit discharge and motoneuron-related changes under its protocol.

However, the systematic review/meta-analysis of motor-unit firing properties (PMID 35295567) found:

- only seven eligible trials;
- no significant pooled change in motor-unit discharge rate in the main meta-analysis;
- substantial heterogeneity;
- generally low-to-moderate certainty for several motor-unit outcomes.

The 2026 comparison of high-load vs combined high/low-load training (PMID 42323749) further shows that firing-rate responses can differ between protocols even when strength gains are similar.

Canonical rule:

**firing rates can increase with strength training, but “strength training always increases firing rate” is not a defensible universal rule.**

Confidence: **MODERATE.**

## 8. Neural-drive adaptation

NEU-05 remains authoritative:

> neural drive = ensemble discharge of active motoneurons, not surface-EMG amplitude.

Strength training can increase effective neural drive through changes in recruitment, discharge rates, common synaptic input and motoneuron properties.

PMID 41309067 is especially important methodologically:

- absolute multichannel EMG amplitude can partially capture within-person early changes in motor-unit discharge/recruitment under constrained conditions;
- normalization to peak/MVC values can hide those changes;
- EMG remains an indirect proxy.

Therefore:

**increased neural drive is a real possible training adaptation, but “higher normalized EMG after training” is neither necessary nor sufficient evidence.**

Confidence: **HIGH for construct boundary; MODERATE-HIGH for adaptation in short-term trained tasks.**

---

## 9. Antagonist coactivation

A simple classic story is:

`training → less antagonist coactivation → more net torque`.

Current evidence does not support that as a universal pathway.

Examples:

- the 2026 short isokinetic intervention (PMID 42555436) found a mean decrease in one coactivation condition but individual changes did not exceed the study's minimal-difference threshold;
- the 2026 older-adult comparison (PMID 42088684) found reduced antagonist-to-agonist coordination ratio while absolute antagonist activation did not significantly change;
- the 2025 training+detraining study (PMID 40888935) observed transient coactivation changes rather than a simple monotonic reduction.

Because coactivation can contribute to joint stiffness/control as well as oppose net torque, its optimal level is task dependent.

Canonical rule:

**strength training may change agonist-antagonist coordination, but lower absolute antagonist activation is not a universal neural adaptation or training goal.**

Confidence: **MODERATE.**

---

## 10. Intermuscular coordination

Early strength training can change how activity is distributed and coupled across muscles.

PMID 41945585 found, after six weeks of squat training:

- improved maximal and rapid force in the tested leg-press task;
- muscle-specific activation changes;
- changes in intermuscular coherence across selected synergist and agonist-antagonist pairs.

PMID 42516016 extends the picture with squat muscle-synergy analysis across loads and training-status groups.

These data support adaptation of coordination, but not a generic “more synchronized = better” rule.

Canonical rule:

**intermuscular adaptation is a task-specific reorganization of timing, weighting and coupling across muscles; it should be judged against task performance, not against one universal coordination pattern.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for adaptability; MODERATE for causal contribution of any one coherence/synergy metric.**

---

## 11. Task-specific skill

Strength is partly a skill expressed in a specific task.

The 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID 40314751; 43 studies, 1660 participants) found that dynamic resistance training produced a substantially larger improvement in trained dynamic strength than transfer to non-trained isometric strength.

This supports a major project rule:

**a portion of strength adaptation is specific to the trained movement/test solution.**

Mechanisms can include:

- better force direction;
- timing;
- stabilization;
- intermuscular coordination;
- familiarity with maximal effort;
- reduced unnecessary degrees of freedom;
- strategy selection.

Therefore early “neural gain” should often be interpreted partly as **task learning**, not only as increased neural drive.

Confidence: **HIGH.**

---

## 12. Cortical / corticospinal adaptations

Cortical and corticospinal adaptation is supported, but no single TMS marker behaves as a universal readout.

The 2020 site-of-adaptation meta-analysis (PMID 31993949) found:

- increased MEP amplitude during voluntary contraction;
- reductions in cortical silent period and active SICI;
- no consistent change in resting MEP, resting SICI or TMS voluntary activation.

The 2023 multivariate meta-analysis (PMID 37353049) found that corticospinal changes and their relationship with strength depend on training type.

The 2026 TMS meta-analysis (PMID 42460308) reports an overall increase in corticospinal excitability, but with substantial between-study heterogeneity.

Recent 2026 experiments reinforce protocol specificity:

- PMID 41845576 found tempo-dependent corticospinal/reticulospinal responses;
- PMID 42690424 found strength gains with reduced MEP area and silent-period duration after isometric training, showing that the sign of one marker cannot be read as “more/less neural adaptation” in isolation.

Canonical rule:

**resistance training can alter cortical/corticospinal excitability and inhibition, but TMS outcomes are state-, task-, intensity- and protocol-dependent; one marker is not a direct measure of the cortical share of strength gain.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for presence of adaptation; MODERATE for exact locus/direction by protocol.**

---

## 13. Spinal / motoneuron adaptations

Evidence supports adaptation below the cortical level, but “spinal adaptation” is not one measure.

The 2020 meta-analysis (PMID 31993949) found:

- increased V-wave amplitude after resistance training;
- no consistent change in H-reflex at rest or during contraction.

Direct motor-unit studies support altered motoneuron output and possibly intrinsic motoneuron properties/synaptic input (PMIDs 30727028, 41823343).

Therefore:

- an unchanged H-reflex does not mean “no spinal adaptation”;
- an increased V-wave cannot uniquely localize the adaptation to spinal circuitry because it reflects descending drive interacting with spinal motoneuron output;
- motor-unit discharge/recruitment changes provide functional evidence of altered motor-neuron-pool output but do not by themselves identify one causal site.

Canonical rule:

**spinal/motoneuron adaptation is supported, but precise anatomical attribution requires converging measures; H-reflex, V-wave and motor-unit metrics are not interchangeable.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH.**

---

## 14. Retention of neural adaptations

Neural adaptations do not appear to have one retention clock.

In PMID 28223941, after four weeks of contraction-specific strength training followed by two weeks of detraining:

- strength and V-wave-related volitional drive were maintained;
- corticospinal excitability (MEP) decreased.

This demonstrates directly that different neural markers can decay at different rates while performance remains relatively preserved.

PMID 40888935 similarly found partial strength retention after four weeks of detraining with changes in corticospinal measures.

Canonical rule:

**retention is marker- and task-specific; preserved strength does not prove every neural adaptation is preserved, and loss of one neural marker does not prove the learned strength task has been erased.**

Confidence: **MODERATE.**

---

## 15. Neural detraining

Neural detraining is best treated as differential regression.

Potential changes include:

- reduced corticospinal excitability/inhibition changes;
- altered agonist-antagonist coordination;
- reduced motor-unit output;
- reduced task familiarity/skill;
- loss of rapid-force expression.

But available studies do not establish one universal neural-decay curve.

This aligns with PHY-18:

> detraining is not one biological clock.

Neural loss must also be distinguished from:

- muscle atrophy;
- tendon/architecture regression;
- general inactivity;
- severe disuse/bed rest.

Canonical rule:

**neural detraining can contribute to performance loss before or without large structural loss, but its time course is task-, marker- and population-specific.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for differential loss; LOW-MODERATE for exact rates.**

---

## 16. Relearning / retraining after a break

Functional strength can be reacquired rapidly after a training break.

The 2024 randomized periodic-vs-continuous RT trial (PMID 39364857) used:

- 10 weeks RT;
- 10 weeks detraining;
- 10 weeks retraining.

After detraining, strength and muscle size decreased, but five weeks of retraining restored previous post-training levels for the measured strength/size outcomes.

This establishes **rapid functional reacquisition** in that protocol.

It does **not** establish that the faster regain was caused by one neural-memory mechanism because retained contributors may include:

- task familiarity / motor skill;
- residual strength;
- residual muscle size/architecture;
- retained neural coordination;
- cellular/molecular history.

PHY-18 remains authoritative for the broader muscle-memory construct.

Canonical rule:

**previously trained people can regain recent strength rapidly after a break; retained neural skill is a plausible contributor, but a unique causal neural-memory mechanism is not established.**

Confidence: **HIGH for rapid reacquisition being possible; LOW-MODERATE for causal neural share.**

## 17. Integrated temporal model

For project use, a safer model is:

### Early training
- task learning/familiarization;
- recruitment/discharge changes;
- neural-drive changes;
- agonist-antagonist/intermuscular coordination changes;
- cortical/corticospinal/spinal plasticity;
- small/early structural changes may already coexist.

### Continued training
- neural adaptation continues but becomes increasingly integrated with:
  - hypertrophy;
  - architecture;
  - tendon/mechanics;
  - technique and task specialization.

### Detraining
- different neural and structural variables regress at different rates.

### Retraining
- previously learned task solutions and residual biological adaptations can accelerate return to prior performance;
- no one “memory” mechanism is sufficient to explain the whole effect.

## 18. Practical downstream rules

1. **Do not diagnose a “neural plateau” from one EMG/TMS measure.**
2. **Do not assume early strength gain proves hypertrophy or excludes it.**
3. **Do not assume strength training always lowers antagonist activity.**
4. **Treat training/test specificity as part of strength adaptation, not as measurement noise.**
5. **Use motor-unit recruitment/firing changes as task-specific adaptations, not universal direction rules.**
6. **Do not equate normalized surface EMG with neural drive.**
7. **Do not localize adaptation to cortex or spinal cord from one evoked-response measure.**
8. **Expect transfer to untrained strength tasks to be smaller than gains in the practiced task.**
9. **After detraining, assess performance and capacity directly rather than inferring them from elapsed time.**
10. **Rapid regain after a break is plausible; do not prescribe a universal retraining multiplier.**
11. **Programming implications for load/velocity/volume remain PRO/GPP, not NEU-06.**
12. **Measurement selection remains TST; individualized neural limitation remains IND.**

## 20. Confidence / unresolved issues

### HIGH

- early strength gain can substantially precede large measurable hypertrophy;
- task/test specificity is a major part of strength adaptation;
- neural drive is not identical to surface EMG;
- neural adaptation involves more than one level of the nervous system;
- rapid functional reacquisition after a break can occur.

### MODERATE-HIGH

- motor-unit recruitment/discharge behavior can adapt with strength training;
- intermuscular coordination can adapt during early strength training;
- cortical/corticospinal measures change with resistance training;
- spinal/motoneuron output can adapt;
- neural variables can be retained/lost at different rates during detraining.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- direction/magnitude of motor-unit firing-rate change;
- exact direction of recruitment-threshold change across tasks;
- exact antagonist coactivation change;
- exact causal contribution of coherence/synergy changes;
- exact cortical vs spinal share of strength gain;
- exact retention time of a specific neural adaptation.

### LOW / NOT ESTABLISHED AS UNIVERSAL

- one universal neural mechanism explaining early strength gain;
- one universal cortical or spinal biomarker of adaptation;
- one neural detraining half-life;
- a unique neural “muscle memory” mechanism that explains faster retraining;
- a universal retraining advantage magnitude.
