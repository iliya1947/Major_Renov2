## 1. Qualification purpose / Question Card

A fitness trainer needs a correct interaction model to avoid:

- teaching phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative pathways as sequential systems that switch on/off one after another;
- calling an exercise “100% anaerobic” or “100% aerobic” without method/context;
- assuming the dominant pathway is the only active pathway;
- using one universal time chart for energy-system contribution;
- assuming oxidative metabolism is irrelevant during short maximal work;
- assuming glycolysis becomes irrelevant during long work;
- assuming repeated sprints have the same pathway contribution as the first sprint;
- assuming recovery intervals are metabolically passive;
- assuming resistance exercise is purely phosphagen/glycolytic;
- using blood lactate or VO2 alone as complete quantification of total ATP contribution;
- treating method-derived percentages as direct, exact physiological measurements.

Primary trainer-level decisions supported later:

- interpret why identical external work can have different metabolic composition across sets/sprints;
- understand why short rests change the next bout;
- distinguish acute ATP supply during work from ATP-dependent restoration during recovery;
- avoid “train one energy system in isolation” language;
- interpret laboratory contribution estimates with method limitations.

Target context:

- healthy adults;
- single maximal efforts;
- repeated sprints;
- resistance exercise;
- prolonged submaximal exercise;
- recovery intervals.

Question type:

- primarily **mechanism + evidence mapping + measurement interpretation**.

Closure criterion for PASS-01:

- all 10 exact Section 8 items covered at content/scope level;
- interaction and measurement boundaries explicit;
- no content-level blocker preventing an independent closure audit.

## 2. Core model — ATP supply is integrated

PASS at content level.

Skeletal muscle ATP turnover during exercise is supported by interacting pathways:

1. stored ATP / phosphagen transfer, especially PCr/CK;
2. glycolytic substrate-level phosphorylation;
3. oxidative phosphorylation.

These pathways differ in:

- activation kinetics;
- maximal ATP-production rate;
- sustainable capacity;
- substrate dependence;
- recovery requirements.

But they do not behave as mutually exclusive engines.

Hargreaves & Spriet (PMID **32747792**) describe ATP resynthesis during exercise as coordinated contributions from PCr, glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation.

The 2026 systematic review PMID **41965479** explicitly treats phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative pathways as interacting systems whose relative shares vary with exercise duration and intensity.

Canonical rule:

**all major ATP-resynthesis pathways can contribute concurrently; exercise changes their relative contribution rather than turning whole systems absolutely on or off.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 3. “Dominant” does not mean “only active”

PASS at content level.

A pathway is **dominant** when it supplies a larger share of ATP over a specified interval/task than the alternatives.

Dominance does not imply:

- exclusive ATP supply;
- zero contribution from other pathways;
- absence of simultaneous regulation;
- one fixed contribution across the whole bout.

Examples:

- a 5–10 s maximal sprint is strongly phosphagen/anaerobic dominated but oxidative ATP production is already increasing;
- a 30–60 s maximal effort can have large glycolytic contribution while PCr and oxidative metabolism remain active;
- prolonged submaximal work is oxidative dominated while phosphagen/glycolytic flux still participates in transitions, accelerations and local ATP buffering.

Canonical rule:

**dominant system ≠ only active system.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 4. Relative contribution by intensity

PASS at content level.

Exercise intensity changes both total ATP demand and the rate at which ATP must be regenerated.

At low/moderate steady-state intensity:

- oxidative phosphorylation can meet most ATP demand;
- phosphagen/glycolytic pathways still buffer transitions and fluctuations.

As intensity rises:

- ATP turnover rate rises;
- PCr breakdown increases rapidly at onset;
- glycolytic ATP supply becomes more important;
- oxidative ATP supply also rises, but kinetics/capacity may not immediately match total demand.

During very short maximal work:

- phosphagen contribution is large;
- glycolytic contribution develops rapidly;
- oxidative contribution is non-zero and increases with continued work.

During severe/maximal work lasting longer:

- phosphagen share falls from its initial high contribution;
- glycolytic contribution often rises early and subsequently falls;
- oxidative contribution progressively rises.

Canonical rule:

**higher intensity increases the need for high-rate phosphagen/glycolytic ATP supply, but does not remove oxidative contribution.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 5. Relative contribution by duration

PASS at content level.

Duration changes contribution even when maximal intent remains.

The 2026 systematic review (PMID **41965479**) synthesized **102 studies / 311 data points** of maximal exercise.

Its model estimated:

- anaerobic predominance during short maximal work;
- progressively increasing aerobic contribution with duration;
- approximately equal modeled anaerobic/aerobic contributions at **78.6 s**.

This number is not retained as a universal physiological switch point because:

- exercise mode varies;
- pacing varies;
- training status/populations vary;
- measurement method varies;
- the review pools study means;
- anaerobic contribution is estimated indirectly.

Older direct/indirect work also demonstrates progressive changes:

- PMID **3253239** estimated substantial oxidative contribution already by 30 s and greater contribution by 90 s;
- PMID **8776206** showed aerobic contribution rising substantially during exhaustive supramaximal running.

Canonical rule:

**duration redistributes contribution continuously; no universal “X seconds = system switch” rule is retained.**

Confidence: **HIGH for direction / MODERATE-HIGH for exact percentage-time estimates**.

## 6. Transition between systems

PASS at content level.

“Transition” should mean a **change in relative flux**, not a discrete handoff.

At exercise onset:

1. ATP demand increases almost immediately;
2. stored ATP/PCr buffers the earliest mismatch;
3. glycolytic ATP production increases rapidly;
4. oxidative ATP production accelerates through VO2/mitochondrial kinetics;
5. the relative contribution evolves continuously.

31P-MRS work (PMID **9124295**) showed oxidative ATP synthesis can rise rapidly during brief maximal exercise while PCr is being depleted.

Short maximal running/cycling studies likewise show meaningful oxidative contribution before traditional textbook “aerobic onset” times.

Canonical rule:

**systems overlap during transition; one pathway does not wait for another to finish.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

Detailed VO2 kinetics / oxygen deficit remain BIO-09.

## 7. No universal contribution chart

Energy-system contribution depends on more than duration.

Important moderators include:

- absolute intensity;
- relative intensity;
- exercise mode;
- active muscle mass;
- pacing;
- training status;
- oxygen availability;
- recovery duration;
- prior PCr/glycogen state;
- nutritional/substrate state;
- measurement method.

Therefore charts such as:

- “0–10 s = ATP-PCr”;
- “10 s–2 min = glycolysis”;
- “>2 min = aerobic”;

are acceptable only as crude teaching heuristics if explicitly labelled, not physiological boundaries.

Canonical rule:

**time-domain charts cannot be treated as exact pathway ownership.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 8. Quantification is method dependent

PASS at content level.

A major limitation in this field is that pathway contributions are usually **estimated**, not directly measured as complete in-vivo ATP flux.

The 2025 systematic review/best-evidence synthesis (PMID **41078873**) evaluated five approaches:

1. maximal accumulated oxygen deficit (MAOD);
2. PCr-La-O2;
3. critical power;
4. gross efficiency;
5. bioenergetic models.

Key findings:

- MAOD had the strongest evidence for reliability/validity among evaluated total aerobic-vs-anaerobic approaches;
- MAOD does not separate alactic/phosphagen from glycolytic anaerobic contribution;
- PCr-La-O2 can separate these components but has a smaller reliability evidence base;
- gross-efficiency and some model-based methods had weaker/conflicting evidence.

The 2024 resistance-exercise scoping review PMID **38896201** likewise shows that indirect calorimetry alone does not capture glycolytic contribution well in resistance exercise.

Canonical rule:

**energy-system percentages are method-conditioned estimates; compare studies only after checking how each component was estimated.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 9. Repeated sets — general interaction

PASS at content level.

A later set does not begin from the same metabolic state as the first set unless recovery is nearly complete.

Across repeated work:

- PCr may remain incompletely restored;
- Pi and other metabolites may remain altered;
- oxygen uptake can remain elevated;
- glycolytic response can change;
- oxidative contribution may become relatively more important;
- power/work can fall.

Therefore:

**same external load/repetition target ≠ same internal energy-system contribution across sets.**

The exact shift depends on:

- set duration;
- repetitions;
- load;
- rest interval;
- proximity to failure;
- active muscle mass;
- training status.

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual / MODERATE-HIGH exact quantitative partition**.

Detailed resistance-set physiology remains BIO-12.

## 10. Resistance-set metabolic evidence

Human biopsy work PMID **22808209** compared:

- 5 × 10 repetitions;
- 10 × 5 repetitions;

with the same initial load/rest structure.

Higher repetitions per set produced:

- greater PCr depletion;
- greater ATP disturbance;
- higher muscle lactate;
- larger power loss.

Classic heavy-resistance biopsy work PMID **3758035** likewise showed:

- substantial PCr decline;
- glycogen use;
- lactate accumulation.

These studies demonstrate simultaneous phosphagen + glycolytic participation during demanding resistance work.

They do not quantify a universal pathway percentage for every resistance set.

Canonical rule:

**resistance exercise is not one fixed metabolic category; set structure materially changes PCr/glycolytic disturbance.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 11. Repeated sprints

PASS at content level.

Repeated-sprint exercise shows especially clear pathway redistribution.

Classic human study PMID **8964751**:

- after a first 30-s sprint, PCr was markedly depleted;
- after ~4 min recovery, PCr had only partially recovered;
- glycolytic ATP turnover in the second 30-s sprint fell substantially;
- oxidative contribution increased and compensated part of the reduced anaerobic contribution.

Modern 2024 repeated-sprint work (PMCID **PMC11549394**) using 5, 10 and 15 × 20 m sprints with short recovery likewise found changing contributions as sprint number increased, including greater oxidative contribution and altered glycolytic contribution.

Canonical rule:

**repeated sprints are not repetitions of the first sprint's metabolic profile; incomplete recovery changes pathway availability and relative contribution.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 12. Repeated-sprint glycolysis can fall despite maximal intent

PASS at content level.

Maximal effort does not guarantee constant maximal glycolytic flux.

During repeated sprints:

- glycolytic contribution can fall;
- PCr availability before each sprint can fall;
- oxidative contribution can rise;
- mechanical output can decline.

PMID **8964751** found a ~45% reduction in glycolysis in the second 30-s sprint despite continued maximal effort.

Older repeated-exercise data (PMID **10225413**) similarly showed lower lactate accumulation in later supramaximal bouts.

Canonical rule:

**maximal intent ≠ constant pathway flux.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 13. PCr availability and repeated high power

PCr availability is especially relevant to high instantaneous power at the start of a subsequent short bout.

PMID **8964751** reported strong associations between PCr resynthesis and recovery of initial power/pedalling speed during the second sprint.

PCr resynthesis itself is primarily oxidative during recovery.

But repeated-bout performance is not determined by PCr alone.

Other contributors include:

- pH;
- Pi;
- K+/ionic state;
- neural factors;
- glycolytic regulation;
- oxygen delivery;
- task mechanics.

Canonical rule:

**PCr restoration is important for repeated high-power output, but PCr alone is not a complete repeated-sprint fatigue model.**

Confidence: **HIGH for PCr role / HIGH against sole-cause model**.

Fatigue integration remains BIO-11.

## 14. Long low-intensity activity

PASS at content level.

During prolonged low/moderate-intensity steady-state activity:

- oxidative phosphorylation supplies most ATP;
- carbohydrate and fat oxidation provide reducing equivalents/substrates;
- relative substrate contribution can shift with duration and feeding state.

Hargreaves & Spriet (PMID **32747792**) retain oxidative metabolism as the dominant ATP source during prolonged submaximal work.

However:

- phosphagen reactions continue to buffer ATP/ADP locally;
- glycolysis continues to provide pyruvate and can supply some substrate-level ATP;
- transient accelerations/hills/changes in force can increase non-oxidative contribution.

Canonical rule:

**long low-intensity activity is oxidative-dominant, not literally oxidative-only.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 15. Resistance training — global contribution

PASS at content level.

Resistance exercise is usually intermittent and non-steady-state.

A single demanding set can involve substantial:

- phosphagen ATP transfer;
- glycolysis;
- oxidative metabolism.

The relative mix varies strongly with:

- load;
- repetitions;
- time under tension;
- proximity to failure;
- exercise selection;
- muscle mass;
- inter-set rest;
- total session density.

PMID **23487002** estimated large anaerobic shares during exhaustive resistance exercise at 80% 1RM, but the authors themselves noted method limitations.

The 2024 scoping review PMID **38896201** concluded that resistance-exercise energy expenditure is difficult to quantify because anaerobic contribution is substantial and indirect calorimetry alone can underestimate total energy expenditure.

Canonical rule:

**resistance training is metabolically mixed and protocol dependent; no universal “X% phosphagen / Y% glycolytic / Z% oxidative” split is retained.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 16. Time under tension / repetition configuration

Time under tension can shift total energetic demand even with matched external work.

PMID **22401781** found different anaerobic energy expenditure across bench-press cadences despite matched submaximal work, while aerobic exercise energy expenditure did not differ in that protocol.

PMID **24552376** found both mechanical power and total work influenced measured oxidative/glycolytic responses across resistance-exercise conditions.

Canonical boundary:

**repetition count alone does not define metabolic cost; duration, cadence and work also matter.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

Detailed programming remains BIO-12/PRO.

---

## 17. Recovery intervals are metabolically active

PASS at content level.

Recovery between bouts is not “no metabolism.”

During recovery, oxidative metabolism supports restoration processes including:

- PCr resynthesis;
- restoration of oxygen stores;
- ion-gradient/homeostatic recovery;
- lactate oxidation/redistribution;
- continued ATP demand for cellular restoration.

PCr recovery measured by 31P-MRS is widely used as an in-vivo indicator of muscle oxidative capacity under appropriate conditions.

Canonical rule:

**the oxidative system is central to recovery between high-intensity bouts, even when the preceding work was anaerobic dominated.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 18. PCr resynthesis is oxidative but context dependent

Human evidence:

- PMID **8457428** links post-exercise PCr recovery to oxidative ATP synthesis control;
- PMID **10368368** showed altered O2 availability changes PCr recovery kinetics;
- PMID **9124295** demonstrated rapid oxidative ATP synthesis and PCr recovery after maximal exercise;
- PMID **9241025** showed PCr remains incompletely restored after short/repeated sprint work even after several minutes in some protocols.

PCr recovery is affected by:

- oxygen availability/perfusion;
- mitochondrial oxidative capacity;
- degree of depletion;
- intracellular pH;
- exercise mode;
- training status.

Therefore:

**PCr resynthesis is not one fixed percentage-per-minute law.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 19. Recovery duration changes the next bout's energy profile

Shorter recovery can leave:

- lower starting PCr;
- higher Pi;
- altered pH;
- elevated VO2;
- incomplete metabolic restoration.

Consequences may include:

- lower peak power;
- altered glycolytic contribution;
- greater relative oxidative contribution;
- reduced total work.

Longer recovery generally allows more PCr restoration but does not guarantee complete normalization of all metabolic variables.

Canonical rule:

**rest interval is part of the energetic state of the next bout, not merely empty time between work periods.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

Exact rest prescription remains BIO-12/13/PRO.

---

## 20. Oxidative contribution during recovery ≠ EPOC explanation

BIO-08 retains only the foundational statement that oxidative metabolism supports recovery.

It does not equate:

- PCr resynthesis;
- post-exercise VO2;
- EPOC;
- total recovery energy cost.

These constructs overlap but are not identical.

Detailed EPOC and VO2 off-kinetics remain BIO-09.

Canonical boundary:

**recovery oxidative metabolism ≠ EPOC as a complete construct.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual boundary**.

---

## 21. Aerobic contribution to short maximal exercise

Short maximal exercise is not aerobically silent.

The 2026 systematic review PMID **41965479** found measurable oxidative contribution across short maximal durations, increasing as duration extends.

Historical direct/indirect estimates (e.g. PMID **3253239**) likewise show non-zero oxidative contribution during 10–30 s work.

The precise percentage is method/protocol dependent.

Canonical rule:

**brief maximal exercise can be anaerobic-dominant while oxidative ATP production is already active.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

Detailed VO2 kinetics remain BIO-09.

---

## 22. Glycolysis during prolonged activity

Long-duration exercise is not “glycolysis off.”

Glycolysis remains necessary because:

- carbohydrate oxidation requires glycolytic production of pyruvate;
- substrate-level phosphorylation can contribute;
- changes in pace/intensity can acutely increase glycolytic ATP production.

Therefore:

**oxidative dominance ≠ absence of glycolysis.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 23. System names are pathway shorthand, not isolated anatomical compartments

“Phosphagen system,” “glycolytic system,” and “oxidative system” are useful educational labels.

But ATP homeostasis is integrated through:

- shared ADP/Pi demand;
- creatine kinase equilibrium;
- substrate flux;
- mitochondrial ATP synthesis;
- cytosolic/mitochondrial redox interactions;
- metabolite transport.

Canonical rule:

**energy systems are interacting biochemical pathways, not three independent engines.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 24. Practical trainer-level model

For any bout, ask:

1. What is the ATP-demand rate?
2. How long does the bout last?
3. What is the starting PCr/metabolic state?
4. Is the task continuous or intermittent?
5. How long was the preceding recovery?
6. Is the effort maximal or submaximal?
7. What is the active muscle mass/mode?
8. Is work/power maintained or falling?
9. How was “system contribution” measured?
10. Are we discussing instantaneous flux, integrated bout contribution, or recovery metabolism?

Only then should pathway dominance be described.

## 25. Measurement boundary — percentages are integrated estimates

A reported value such as:

“40% aerobic / 60% anaerobic”

can refer to:

- integrated energy over a whole bout;
- a specific time segment;
- modelled oxygen deficit;
- VO2-derived oxidative energy;
- lactate-derived glycolytic equivalent;
- EPOC-derived phosphagen estimate.

These are not interchangeable.

Canonical rule:

**time-integrated contribution ≠ instantaneous ATP-production rate.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 26. Important non-equivalences

1. dominant system ≠ only active system.
2. anaerobic-dominant ≠ zero oxidative ATP production.
3. oxidative-dominant ≠ zero glycolysis/phosphagen flux.
4. pathway activation ≠ pathway dominance.
5. pathway dominance ≠ fixed pathway percentage.
6. exercise duration ≠ sole determinant of contribution.
7. maximal effort ≠ constant maximal glycolytic flux.
8. first sprint contribution ≠ later sprint contribution.
9. first set contribution ≠ later set contribution.
10. same external load ≠ same internal metabolic state.
11. PCr depletion ≠ complete fatigue explanation.
12. PCr recovery ≠ complete metabolic recovery.
13. recovery interval ≠ metabolically inactive period.
14. recovery VO2 ≠ PCr resynthesis alone.
15. oxidative recovery metabolism ≠ EPOC as a whole.
16. blood lactate ≠ glycolytic ATP flux.
17. VO2 ≠ total ATP production.
18. indirect calorimetry ≠ complete resistance-exercise energy expenditure.
19. MAOD anaerobic estimate ≠ direct split of phosphagen vs glycolytic contribution.
20. integrated bout percentage ≠ instantaneous ATP flux.
21. one study's pathway percentages ≠ universal physiological constants.
22. resistance training ≠ purely anaerobic.
23. prolonged low-intensity activity ≠ purely aerobic.
24. “energy system” ≠ independent engine.

## 28. Established content-level findings

### HIGH

1. Phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative ATP-resynthesis pathways operate concurrently.
2. Dominant pathway is not the only active pathway.
3. Relative contribution changes continuously with intensity and duration.
4. Oxidative ATP production contributes even during brief maximal exercise.
5. There is no universal time point at which one energy system switches off and another switches on.
6. Short maximal exercise is generally more anaerobic dominated than longer maximal exercise.
7. Oxidative contribution rises as maximal exercise duration increases.
8. Repeated bouts change the metabolic starting state and therefore change pathway contribution.
9. Repeated-sprint glycolytic contribution can decline despite continued maximal effort.
10. PCr restoration is important for repeated high-power output.
11. PCr resynthesis during recovery is largely supported by oxidative metabolism.
12. Recovery intervals are metabolically active.
13. Prolonged low/moderate-intensity exercise is oxidative dominated but not oxidative exclusive.
14. Resistance exercise uses mixed phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative metabolism.
15. Set duration/repetitions/time under tension/rest materially affect resistance-exercise metabolism.
16. Quantitative pathway contributions depend strongly on the measurement/model used.
17. Time-integrated pathway percentage is not equivalent to instantaneous ATP-production rate.

### MODERATE-HIGH

18. The average anaerobic-to-aerobic crossover in maximal whole-body exercise occurs around ~75–80 s in the 2026 pooled model, but this is not an individual universal switch.
19. Relative oxidative contribution often increases across repeated sprints/sets when recovery is incomplete.
20. Different resistance-set configurations can shift phosphagen/glycolytic disturbance and measured oxidative demand.
21. Training status/oxidative capacity can influence recovery kinetics and repeated-bout contribution.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT DEPENDENT

22. Exact phosphagen/glycolytic/oxidative percentage in a specific exercise.
23. Exact crossover duration for an individual.
24. Exact oxidative increase from one repeated sprint/set to the next.
25. Exact PCr recovery fraction after a given rest interval.
26. Exact resistance-exercise energy split.
27. Whether one contribution-estimation method is optimal for a specific intermittent field task.

### NOT RETAINED AS UNIVERSAL RULE

28. First 10 s are 100% ATP-PCr.
29. Glycolysis starts only after PCr is exhausted.
30. Aerobic metabolism starts only after ~2 min.
31. Long endurance work is 100% aerobic.
32. Resistance training is purely anaerobic.
33. Repeated sprints have the same energy-system split.
34. Maximal effort maintains maximal glycolytic ATP production throughout.
35. Three minutes always fully restore PCr.
36. Blood lactate directly equals glycolytic energy contribution.
37. VO2 directly equals total exercise ATP production.
38. One energy-system percentage chart applies to every athlete/task.

## 29. Evidence-ledger candidates

1. Phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative ATP-resynthesis pathways contribute concurrently; pathway dominance is relative rather than exclusive.
2. Exercise intensity and duration redistribute pathway contribution continuously; no universal switch time exists.
3. Short maximal exercise can be anaerobic dominated while oxidative ATP production is already active and increasing.
4. Quantitative energy-system contribution estimates are method dependent; no single approach directly measures all pathway ATP flux in vivo.
5. Repeated sets begin from altered PCr/metabolite/VO2 states, so later-set contribution is not equivalent to the first set.
6. Repeated-sprint glycolytic contribution can decline while oxidative contribution rises, despite continued maximal effort.
7. Prolonged low/moderate-intensity exercise is oxidative dominated but phosphagen/glycolytic pathways remain active for ATP buffering and transitions.
8. Resistance exercise has mixed phosphagen/glycolytic/oxidative demands whose relative contributions depend on set structure and recovery.
9. Oxidative metabolism supports PCr resynthesis and other recovery processes between bouts; recovery intervals are metabolically active.
10. Integrated bout-level pathway percentages are non-equivalent to instantaneous pathway ATP-production rates.
