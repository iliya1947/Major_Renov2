## 3. PCr depletion / repletion audit

PASS.

Retain:

- PCr can fall rapidly during high-intensity work;
- PCr resynthesis during recovery is primarily oxidative;
- repeated bouts can begin before full PCr restoration;
- end-exercise pH, depletion state, age/health and methodology affect estimated recovery.

PMID **40189235** pooled **128 studies** and found substantial methodological/biological heterogeneity in 31P-MRS PCr recovery estimates.

PMID **9241025** showed ~90% recovery by 3 min after one 6-s sprint and ~84% after repeated 6-s sprints in the studied groups.

PMID **8964751** showed only ~79% restoration after ~3.8 min following a 30-s sprint with severe acidification.

Reject:

- PCr is always fully restored by 3 min;
- one PCr recovery constant applies to every muscle/protocol;
- incomplete PCr alone is a complete fatigue diagnosis.

Canonical rule:

**PCr recovery is rapid relative to substantially depleted glycogen stores, but exact kinetics are state-, muscle- and method-dependent.**

No blocker.

---

## 4. PCr vs glycogen / lipid time-course comparison

PASS AFTER NARROWING.

PASS-01 correctly rejected one universal recovery clock, but its approximate ordering needs tightening.

Retain:

- PCr commonly recovers on a minutes scale;
- muscle/liver glycogen recovery depends strongly on depletion and carbohydrate availability;
- intramyocellular lipid recovery is highly protocol/fibre/diet dependent.

Do not retain a rigid global sequence such as:

PCr → liver glycogen → muscle glycogen → IMTG.

Reason:

- liver glycogen was restored within 6 h in one high-carbohydrate recovery protocol while muscle glycogen remained below baseline at 12 h (PMID **40836481**);
- type-I intramyocellular lipid returned to baseline within ~2 h after one resistance-exercise protocol in untrained men (PMID **16369816**);
- after prolonged endurance exercise, IMTG restoration required ~24–48 h in other trained male cohorts depending on diet/protocol (PMIDs **31935113**, **12783774**).

Canonical rule:

**substrate recovery is pool/task specific; PCr is generally fast, but glycogen and intramyocellular-lipid time courses overlap and cannot be arranged into one universal hierarchy.**

No blocker.

## 5. Muscle glycogen depletion audit

PASS.

Retain:

- muscle glycogen use depends on intensity, duration, starting glycogen, recruitment and feeding;
- whole-muscle glycogen can decline substantially during endurance, sprint/interval and resistance exercise;
- depletion can be spatially heterogeneous across fibres and compartments.

PMID **29923148** supports exercise intensity and starting glycogen as important determinants during endurance exercise.

No fixed percentage per workout is retained.

## 6. Resistance-exercise glycogen audit

PASS WITH STRONG GENERALIZATION BOUNDARY.

PMID **41420384**:

- 20 study clusters / 28 effect sizes;
- significant average vastus-lateralis glycogen decrease;
- mean difference ~−104 mmol/kg dry mass;
- 95% prediction interval ~−244 to +36 mmol/kg dry mass;
- greater depletion associated with more sets and longer sessions;
- substantial protocol heterogeneity.

Important closure boundary:

**the pooled average does not authorize a universal resistance-session depletion value.**

The evidence base is concentrated in vastus lateralis and is predominantly male.

Canonical rule:

**resistance exercise can materially deplete local muscle glycogen, but magnitude is muscle-, protocol- and participant-dependent.**

No blocker.

---

## 7. Muscle glycogen heterogeneity audit

PASS.

Keep explicit:

- whole-muscle biopsy/MRS concentration is an average;
- fibre-specific and subcellular pools can differ;
- local recruitment changes local substrate use;
- measurable glycogen can remain at task failure.

Reject:

- low glycogen = zero glycogen;
- one biopsy = every fibre/subcellular pool;
- glycogen depletion alone = complete fatigue mechanism.

Detailed glycogen-localization/fatigue causality remains BIO-11.

No blocker.

## 8. Carbohydrate ingestion and glycogen sparing audit

PASS WITH SMALL-EFFECT BOUNDARY.

PMID **41363659**:

- 31 studies;
- 48 crossover effect sizes;
- small average glycogen-sparing effect;
- SMD −0.16;
- estimated ~24 mmol/kg dry mass over ~100 min;
- no consistent moderator identified for mode, carbohydrate type, ingestion rate or preexercise glycogen.

PMID **42552237** showed ~40% lower muscle glycogen utilization with carbohydrate in one specific HIIT protocol, while liver glycogen utilization was not significantly spared.

Therefore:

**carbohydrate can reduce net muscle glycogen use in some exercise contexts, but the average effect is small and study-specific effects should not be universalized.**

No fueling prescription is authorized here.

---

## 9. Muscle glycogen repletion audit

PASS.

PMID **33507402** supports faster short-term glycogen resynthesis with carbohydrate vs non-nutritive recovery in recovery windows ≤8 h.

PMID **40836481** shows that even very large carbohydrate provision can leave muscle glycogen below baseline at 12 h after substantial depletion.

PMID **42828472** independently reinforces a strong carbohydrate–muscle-glycogen relationship but also very large heterogeneity across studies.

Canonical rule:

**rapid muscle glycogen restoration is strongly carbohydrate-availability dependent, but no fixed intake guarantees complete restoration by a universal time.**

Exact dose/form belongs NUT.

---

## 10. Carbohydrate timing audit

PASS WITH WINDOW-SPECIFIC BOUNDARY.

PMID **3132449** supports faster early glycogen storage when carbohydrate is provided immediately rather than after a 2-h delay.

PMID **39263899** found:

- similar muscle glycogen across conditions by 24 h when total carbohydrate was matched;
- worse next-day HIIE capacity after delayed carbohydrate.

Therefore:

**early carbohydrate availability can matter when recovery time is short, but early timing is not the sole determinant of 24-h glycogen status or next-session performance.**

No universal postexercise timing prescription is retained.

---

## 11. Liver glycogen audit

PASS.

Retain:

- liver glycogen is distinct from muscle glycogen;
- its exercise role includes support of hepatic glucose output and blood-glucose homeostasis;
- liver and muscle glycogen can deplete differently in the same session;
- their recovery kinetics can diverge.

PMID **42552237** directly supports different liver-vs-muscle glycogen utilization during HIIT.

PMID **40836481** supports rapid liver restoration under one high-carbohydrate recovery protocol while muscle glycogen remained incompletely restored.

Canonical rule:

**muscle and liver glycogen are different stores with different exercise roles and potentially different depletion/repletion kinetics.**

---

## 12. Liver glycogen timing audit

PASS AFTER NARROWING.

Do not retain:

**liver glycogen always recovers faster than muscle glycogen.**

Retain only:

**liver glycogen can recover faster than muscle glycogen under some carbohydrate-fed conditions.**

The 6-h liver restoration / incomplete 12-h muscle restoration pattern from PMID **40836481** is protocol specific.

No blocker.

## 13. Liver glycogen concentration vs content audit

PASS.

PMID **41115061**:

- liver glycogen concentration rose after a carbohydrate-rich breakfast;
- liver volume simultaneously fell;
- total liver glycogen content did not materially increase.

Canonical rule:

**liver glycogen concentration and total liver glycogen content are non-equivalent when liver volume changes.**

This distinction is retained in the canonical finding.

---

## 14. IMTG / IMCL terminology audit

PASS AFTER TERMINOLOGY TIGHTENING.

Do not use IMTG and IMCL as perfectly interchangeable measurement labels.

Operational distinction:

- **IMTG/IMTAG** refers to intramyocellular triacylglycerol storage measured/defined biochemically or morphologically;
- **IMCL** is commonly an MRS/imaging-oriented intramyocellular-lipid construct;
- intramyocellular lipid must remain distinct from extramyocellular/intermuscular adipose tissue.

PMID **42816429** independently reinforces terminology separation.

Canonical wording:

**intramyocellular lipid storage is a real exercise substrate pool, but exact IMTG/IMCL labels and values are method dependent.**

---

## 15. Intramyocellular lipid depletion audit

PASS.

PMID **36656983**:

- 44 cycling studies;
- mean net IMCL reduction ~23.7%;
- larger type-I depletion with immunofluorescence;
- analytical method materially affected estimated change.

PMID **37877795**:

- severe repeated high-intensity intermittent exercise produced no net decline in major intramuscular lipid-droplet pools;
- subcellular remodeling still occurred.

Canonical rule:

**endurance-related intramyocellular-lipid utilization cannot be generalized to every high-intensity intermittent session.**

---

## 16. Intramyocellular lipid repletion / dietary-fat audit

PASS WITH POPULATION/PROTOCOL BOUNDARY.

PMID **12783774** in trained male cyclists:

- ~21% IMTG decline after prolonged endurance cycling;
- restoration within 48 h with a normal-fat recovery diet;
- impaired restoration with the studied carbohydrate-rich lower-fat diet.

PMID **31935113** in elite male triathletes:

- large type-I fibre IMTG use during prolonged cycling;
- recovery toward baseline by 24 h.

PMID **16369816** demonstrates that in a very different resistance-exercise model, type-I intramyocellular lipid returned to baseline within ~2 h.

Therefore:

**dietary fat availability can influence IMTG restoration after prolonged endurance exercise, but neither the effect nor the time course is universal across exercise modes or populations.**

Exact fat prescription remains NUT.

---

## 17. Whole-body fat oxidation vs IMTG audit

PASS.

PMID **12651914** showed elevated recovery fat oxidation without net IMTG utilization while muscle glycogen was being restored.

Canonical rule:

**whole-body fat oxidation does not identify which lipid pool supplied the oxidized fat and does not prove net IMTG depletion.**

---

## 18. Blood glucose audit

PASS.

Blood glucose is retained as a regulated circulating concentration and flux system, not a large storage depot analogous to muscle/liver glycogen.

PMID **12169451** showed plasma glucose remained near preexercise values during prolonged exercise with carbohydrate feeding while glucose appearance and disposal rates rose markedly.

Canonical rule:

**blood glucose concentration ≠ glucose turnover.**

Stable concentration does not imply low flux.

---

## 19. Hypoglycemia / fatigue audit

PASS WITH CAUSAL NEUTRALITY.

Current 2026 literature contains an active interpretive dispute:

- PMID **41562187** argues strongly for exercise-induced hypoglycemia as a major endurance termination mechanism;
- PMID **42822023** explicitly asks what limits performance when blood glucose is maintained by carbohydrate ingestion.

BIO-10 does not need to adjudicate that causal hierarchy.

Retain:

- exercise-induced hypoglycemia can occur and can matter;
- maintained glucose does not guarantee unlimited performance;
- blood glucose is not a universal sole fatigue explanation.

Defer the causal fatigue model to BIO-11.

Canonical rule:

**BIO-10 describes glucose availability/state; it does not canonize hypoglycemia as the universal endurance-fatigue mechanism.**

## 20. Recovery time-course architecture

PASS AFTER REMOVING FIXED CLOCKS.

Closure retains:

### PCr
Often minutes-scale recovery; exact kinetics vary.

### Blood glucose
Dynamic regulated concentration/flux; no single repletion curve.

### Liver glycogen
Can change materially over hours and can be restored rapidly in some carbohydrate-fed protocols.

### Muscle glycogen
Often requires many hours and sometimes >12–24 h after large depletion; exact kinetics vary with depletion, nutrition and task.

### Intramyocellular lipid
Can recover within hours in some resistance contexts or require ~24–48 h after prolonged endurance exercise depending on fibre/diet/protocol.

Canonical rule:

**there is no universal substrate-recovery clock and no universal ordering among liver glycogen, muscle glycogen and IMTG.**

## 21. Nutritional dependence audit

PASS WITH NON-PRESCRIPTIVE BOUNDARY.

Retain only substrate-specific dependencies:

- PCr resynthesis is primarily oxidative and is not a direct carbohydrate replacement process;
- rapid muscle/liver glycogen restoration depends strongly on carbohydrate availability;
- intramyocellular-lipid restoration can depend on dietary fat availability in prolonged-endurance recovery;
- blood glucose reflects hepatic and exogenous appearance versus tissue disposal.

Reject:

- one nutrient refills every energy system;
- exact dose/timing prescriptions inside BIO-10.

NUT owns the prescription layer.

---

## 22. Repeated-session implications audit

PASS WITH TASK-SPECIFIC BOUNDARY.

A later session can begin with altered:

- PCr;
- muscle glycogen;
- liver glycogen;
- intramyocellular lipid;
- blood-glucose flux;
- non-substrate recovery variables.

Which state matters depends on the next task.

PCr studies show repeated bouts can begin before complete restoration.

Carbohydrate-timing studies show next-day performance can differ even when 24-h muscle glycogen is similar.

Therefore:

**repeated-session outcome is substrate- and task-specific and cannot be inferred from one recovered store.**

No specific scheduling rule is authorized here.

## 23. Substrate restoration vs functional recovery audit

PASS.

PMID **32187403**:

- carbohydrate accelerated glycogen resynthesis;
- did not accelerate recovery of prolonged low-frequency force depression.

PMID **39263899**:

- 24-h muscle glycogen was similar;
- next-day HIIE capacity differed between timing conditions.

Canonical rule:

**substrate restoration and functional/performance recovery are related but non-equivalent.**

This is a boundary statement, not a readiness model.

REC/PRO own broader readiness/programming.

## 24. Training-mode depletion patterns audit

PASS WITH PROFILE-NOT-LAW BOUNDARY.

### Endurance
Can materially use muscle glycogen, liver glycogen and intramyocellular lipid over prolonged exercise.

### Sprint / HIIT
Can produce rapid PCr depletion and substantial local muscle-glycogen use without reproducing endurance-style IMTG depletion.

### Resistance
Can produce local PCr and glycogen depletion; lipid use is protocol/fibre dependent.

Supporting current evidence:

- resistance glycogen meta-analysis PMID **41420384**;
- HIIT liver/muscle 13C-MRS PMID **42552237**;
- sprint-vs-continuous crossover PMID **41830465**;
- cycling IMCL meta-analysis PMID **36656983**;
- severe intermittent lipid-droplet study PMID **37877795**.

Canonical rule:

**training mode changes the likely depletion profile, but no mode has one fixed substrate signature.**

## 31. Closure decision

**BIO-10 = CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0.**

Meaning:

1. substrate depletion/repletion is pool specific;
2. PCr recovers rapidly but without one universal full-repletion time;
3. muscle glycogen depletion is task/recruitment/muscle dependent;
4. resistance exercise can materially deplete local glycogen, but exact magnitude is highly variable;
5. muscle and liver glycogen are distinct stores;
6. liver and muscle glycogen can have different depletion/repletion kinetics;
7. liver glycogen concentration must be separated from total content when organ volume changes;
8. intramyocellular lipid is a real exercise substrate, but IMTG/IMCL terminology and measured change are method dependent;
9. IMTG use/recovery is fibre-, mode-, diet- and protocol-dependent;
10. blood glucose is a regulated concentration/flux, not a storage depot;
11. stable blood glucose can coexist with high glucose turnover;
12. no universal substrate-recovery clock or fixed liver/muscle/IMTG hierarchy is retained;
13. nutrition affects substrate pools differently;
14. early carbohydrate timing can matter in short recovery windows without being the sole determinant of 24-h glycogen/performance;
15. substrate restoration does not certify functional recovery/readiness;
16. endurance, sprint/HIIT and resistance exercise have different likely depletion profiles without fixed mode signatures;
17. session duration or mechanical work alone cannot determine substrate depletion.
