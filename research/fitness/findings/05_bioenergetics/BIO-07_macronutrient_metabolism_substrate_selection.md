# BIO-07 — Macronutrient metabolism / substrate selection

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-19**  
Область: 05 — Bioenergetics / Exercise Metabolism

## 0. Core decision

Exercise fuel selection is a dynamic distribution problem governed by:

- ATP demand / intensity;
- exercise duration;
- substrate availability;
- training status/adaptation;
- feeding state;
- measurement context.

Project rule:

**carbohydrate, fat and amino-acid use are concurrent and context dependent; there is no binary carbohydrate-vs-fat switch and no universal “fat-burning zone.”**

---

## 1. Carbohydrate oxidation

Carbohydrate can support:

- substrate-level ATP production through glycolysis;
- oxidative ATP production through pyruvate → PDH → acetyl-CoA → TCA/OXPHOS.

Project rule:

**carbohydrate metabolism ≠ anaerobic metabolism.**

---

## 2. Carbohydrate source pools

Keep separate:

- muscle glycogen;
- liver-derived/blood glucose;
- exogenous carbohydrate;
- oxidizable carbohydrate-derived intermediates.

Project rule:

**muscle glycogen use ≠ total carbohydrate oxidation.**

Low muscle glycogen can reduce endogenous carbohydrate oxidation while exogenous carbohydrate oxidation remains available.

---

## 3. Fat oxidation

Exercise fat oxidation involves multiple stages:

`triglyceride lipolysis → FA availability/delivery → muscle uptake → intracellular partitioning → mitochondrial entry → beta-oxidation → acetyl-CoA/TCA/OXPHOS`.

Project rule:

**lipolysis ≠ FFA availability ≠ uptake ≠ mitochondrial entry ≠ beta-oxidation ≠ whole-body fat oxidation.**

---

## 4. Beta-oxidation

Long-chain fatty-acid oxidation requires mitochondrial transport involving the carnitine shuttle.

Beta-oxidation then generates:

- acetyl-CoA;
- NADH;
- FADH2.

Project rule:

**beta-oxidation is mitochondrial fatty-acid degradation; it is not synonymous with adipose lipolysis or body-fat loss.**

No single step such as CPT1/carnitine is retained as a universal sole rate limiter across all exercise states.

---

## 5. Protein / amino acids

Protein/amino acids can contribute to exercise energy.

Current endurance meta-analysis supports a small average contribution in prolonged exercise, but exact values depend on:

- intensity;
- duration;
- carbohydrate/glycogen availability;
- energy state;
- method.

Project rule:

**protein contribution is usually subsidiary, not zero.**

---

## 6. Intensity

As exercise intensity rises:

- carbohydrate contribution generally increases;
- absolute fat oxidation often rises from low to moderate intensity;
- fat oxidation commonly peaks at an individual submaximal intensity;
- fat oxidation then tends to decline at high workloads.

Project rule:

**higher intensity generally shifts substrate use toward carbohydrate without switching fat oxidation abruptly to zero.**

---

## 7. Fatmax

Fatmax is:

- individualized;
- nutrition dependent;
- protocol dependent;
- exercise-mode dependent;
- method dependent.

Project rule:

**no universal Fatmax or crossover percentage of VO2max/VO2peak/HRmax is retained.**

Fatmax and LT/GET can be related but are not the same construct by definition.

---

## 8. Duration

During prolonged submaximal exercise, substrate sources shift.

Plasma-derived fuels can become more important while local stores are used.

Project rule:

**duration changes both macronutrient proportions and the source pool supplying a macronutrient.**

There is no universal “after X minutes fat takes over” rule.

---

## 9. Training status

Endurance training changes:

- oxidative capacity;
- fatty-acid handling;
- glucose/glycogen flux;
- substrate selection at a given task.

Interpretation requires both:

- absolute workload;
- relative intensity.

Project rule:

**training status modifies substrate handling, but there is no single universal fuel-shift rule at matched relative intensity.**

---

## 10. Glycogen availability

Starting glycogen materially affects substrate use.

Low glycogen can produce:

- lower endogenous carbohydrate oxidation;
- higher fat oxidation;
- altered PFO/Fatmax.

But it does not necessarily eliminate exogenous carbohydrate oxidation.

Project rule:

**low glycogen ≠ carbohydrate-free metabolism.**

Also:

**low glycogen ≠ low energy availability.**

---

## 11. Glycogen state vs mitochondrial function

Short-term glycogen/carbohydrate manipulation can shift:

- PFO;
- Fatmax;
- endogenous carbohydrate oxidation;

without requiring a change in intrinsic mitochondrial coupling/function.

Project rule:

**fuel-selection phenotype can change because substrate availability changed, not because mitochondrial machinery intrinsically changed.**

---

## 12. Feeding state

Fed vs fasted state changes acute substrate selection.

Pre-exercise carbohydrate can:

- increase glucose/insulin;
- suppress lipolysis;
- reduce fat oxidation in many submaximal protocols.

Fasted exercise often raises acute fat oxidation.

Project rule:

**fasted exercise can alter acute fuel use; it is not automatically a superior long-term fat-loss strategy.**

---

## 13. Acute fat oxidation vs fat loss

Keep separate:

- within-session fat oxidation;
- 24-h substrate balance;
- chronic fat-mass change.

Project rule:

**acute fat oxidation ≠ chronic adipose-fat loss.**

---

## 14. Metabolic flexibility

Metabolic flexibility is the capacity to adjust substrate use to:

- changing ATP demand;
- feeding/fasting;
- glycogen/substrate availability;
- exercise intensity/duration.

Project rule:

**metabolic flexibility = appropriate switching/adaptation, not maximal fat oxidation.**

---

## 15. Measurement of metabolic flexibility

No single standardized exercise protocol or score is retained.

Different studies vary in:

- stage duration;
- maximal/submaximal testing;
- averaging windows;
- equations;
- nutritional state;
- exercise mode.

Project rule:

**one RER, PFO or Fatmax value does not define metabolic flexibility.**

Testing protocol remains TST.

---

## 16. RER / indirect calorimetry

RER-based substrate equations can estimate whole-body carbohydrate/fat oxidation under suitable steady-state conditions.

Important limitations include:

- bicarbonate-pool changes at high intensity;
- non-metabolic CO2 from buffering;
- glucose-vs-glycogen equation assumptions;
- omission of protein oxidation;
- no direct identification of local muscle source pools.

Project rule:

**RER is a method-dependent whole-body estimator, not a direct local-muscle substrate-flux measurement.**

---

## 17. Fuel phenotype vs performance

Low-carbohydrate/high-fat or ketogenic adaptation can markedly increase fat oxidation.

This does not establish universal superiority or inferiority for performance.

Project rule:

**higher fat oxidation ≠ superior exercise performance.**

Dietary strategy remains NUT; performance prescription remains PRO.

---

## 18. Canonical non-equivalences

1. carbohydrate metabolism ≠ anaerobic metabolism.
2. muscle glycogen use ≠ total carbohydrate oxidation.
3. endogenous carbohydrate oxidation ≠ exogenous carbohydrate oxidation.
4. lipolysis ≠ fat oxidation.
5. plasma FFA concentration ≠ fatty-acid flux.
6. fatty-acid availability ≠ uptake.
7. uptake ≠ mitochondrial entry.
8. mitochondrial entry ≠ beta-oxidation.
9. beta-oxidation ≠ body-fat loss.
10. fat oxidation ≠ chronic fat loss.
11. Fatmax ≠ universal intensity percentage.
12. Fatmax ≠ LT/GET by definition.
13. low glycogen ≠ zero carbohydrate oxidation.
14. low glycogen ≠ inability to oxidize exogenous carbohydrate.
15. low glycogen ≠ low energy availability.
16. fasted exercise ≠ superior chronic fat loss.
17. trained status ≠ one universal fuel shift at matched relative intensity.
18. protein contribution is small ≠ zero.
19. metabolic flexibility ≠ maximal fat oxidation.
20. one RER/Fatmax value ≠ metabolic flexibility.
21. RER ≠ direct muscle substrate flux.
22. higher fat oxidation ≠ superior performance.
23. acute substrate selection ≠ chronic adaptation.
24. acute substrate selection ≠ body-composition outcome.

---

## 19. Confidence

### HIGH

- carbohydrate and fat are concurrently available oxidative fuels;
- carbohydrate can support both glycolytic and oxidative ATP production;
- carbohydrate source pools are distinct;
- lipolysis/availability/uptake/oxidation are non-equivalent;
- beta-oxidation is mitochondrial fatty-acid degradation;
- protein contribution is non-zero but usually subsidiary;
- higher intensity generally increases carbohydrate reliance;
- no universal Fatmax percentage exists;
- duration alters substrate selection/source pools;
- glycogen availability changes substrate selection;
- fasted/fed state changes acute substrate use;
- acute fat oxidation does not determine chronic fat loss;
- metabolic flexibility means adaptive switching;
- RER is a context-limited whole-body estimator.

### MODERATE-HIGH

- endurance training commonly increases fat oxidation/reduces endogenous carbohydrate reliance in defined workloads;
- training effects at matched relative intensity are protocol dependent rather than fixed;
- low glycogen can shift PFO/Fatmax without intrinsic mitochondrial-function change.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT DEPENDENT

- exact Fatmax intensity for an individual;
- exact protein energy contribution in a specific session;
- exact IMTG vs plasma-FFA contribution;
- exact fed-vs-fasted effect size;
- any single numerical metabolic-flexibility score;
- performance effects of high-fat/low-carbohydrate adaptation.

### NOT RETAINED AS UNIVERSAL

- carbohydrate = anaerobic;
- fat = aerobic only;
- fat oxidation becomes zero at high intensity;
- one universal crossover/Fatmax percentage;
- longer exercise automatically becomes “fat only”;
- endurance training always reduces carbohydrate use at matched relative intensity;
- low glycogen prevents carbohydrate oxidation;
- fasted cardio is automatically better for fat loss;
- protein contributes zero energy;
- high fat oxidation alone proves metabolic flexibility;
- one RER value directly measures local substrate flux;
- maximizing fat oxidation is automatically the optimal performance strategy.
