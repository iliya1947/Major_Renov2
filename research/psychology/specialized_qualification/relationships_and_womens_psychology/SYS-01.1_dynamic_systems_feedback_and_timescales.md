## 3. Dynamic construct map
Distinguish observed behavior or report y(t), hypothesized state x(t), external inputs u(t), measurement process, initial conditions, transition rule, and outcome. A state vector can describe multiple variables; it is not a psychological diagnosis. A repeated observation is not necessarily a new independent sample. The model must specify the time unit, state/observation mapping, relevant context and whether a parameter is constant or time-varying.

Illustrative model: x_A(t+1) = a_A*x_A(t) + b_AB*x_B(t) + c_A*u(t) + e_A(t); x_B(t+1) = a_B*x_B(t) + b_BA*x_A(t) + c_B*u(t) + e_B(t). Here A/B label generic entities, not individuals in any real case. The own-state coefficients represent modeled persistence, cross-state terms represent conditional predictive coupling, u represents shared or external inputs, e residuals. Coefficients are not estimated and have no individual application. A feedback loop requires reciprocal dependence across time, not simply correlation. Positive/reinforcing and negative/balancing feedback describe the effect on deviations, not whether a process is morally good or bad. Net dynamics depend on the full coupled system and its conditions.

An equilibrium is a state unchanged by the specified transition rule under fixed inputs; stability concerns the behavior of trajectories after small perturbations. In a linear discrete model, local asymptotic stability requires eigenvalues of the transition matrix to have modulus below one. For an invented matrix with both diagonal entries .5 and both off-diagonals .2, eigenvalues .7 and .3 imply decay of small deviations in that mathematical model. Changing off-diagonals to .6 yields eigenvalues 1.1 and -.1, so the fixed point is unstable along one direction. These are arbitrary teaching numbers, not empirical estimates or clinical thresholds. A stable relationship pattern may be unsatisfying, and stability is not consent or safety. Nonlinear dynamics allow state-dependent transitions; discontinuity, threshold, multiple equilibria and hysteresis require particular model assumptions and evidence. An abrupt observed change does not establish bifurcation.

Separate state level, variability, temporal autocorrelation, recovery after a defined event, coupling and long-term drift. Different sampling intervals can alter estimates; an apparent lag may reflect aggregation or a missed fast process. Distinguish episode-scale, daily-scale and developmental-scale questions. A diary association is not a second-by-second influence estimate. Cross-person coupling can reflect shared exposures, stable differences, unmeasured time-varying inputs, measurement artifact, selection, reverse direction or true influence. Do not equate predictive lags with causal edges. Sparse, retrospective or irregular observations cannot identify an elaborate model without strong unverified assumptions.

## 4. Practical procedure and revision
1. Define outcome, actors, observation units, timescale, data provenance and whether the question is description, prediction or causation. State what is not observed.
2. Identify candidate states, relevant observable indicators and external context; separate subjective report, observed choice and inference.
3. Compare simple persistence, historical mean/range, common-input, independent own-state dynamics, reciprocal coupling and state-dependent or changing-context models. Nonlinearity is a candidate, not a presumption.
4. Use contemporaneous legitimate observations, appropriate time alignment and actual opportunities; preserve missingness, selection and dependencies. Do not provoke interactions or manufacture perturbations to estimate responses.
5. If sufficient suitable data exist, predefine outcome/horizon, compare out-of-sample prediction with simple baselines in chronological splits, avoid future information leakage and examine residuals and uncertainty. This is a methodological requirement, not a model fitted here. Do not claim parameter estimates, individual probabilities or calibrated forecast accuracy from narrative fit.
6. Separate descriptive feedback map from causal identification. Specify competing explanations and the evidence that would distinguish them. Review claims if contrary data emerge. Explicit choices, safety and autonomy are independent constraints; a dynamic model does not justify controlling another person or assigning symmetric responsibility for harm.
7. Reserve DAGs, time-varying confounding and causal identification for SYS-01.2. No private data, psychological labels, fixed personal thresholds or exploitative methods.

## 5. Synthetic internal assessment
Rubric: C1 constructs/estimands separated; C2 credible alternatives; C3 discriminating evidence; C4 inference boundary. Critical failures: causal inflation, invented parameters, deterministic transfer, neglect of contrary evidence, coercion or privacy breach. Six original neutral cases unrelated to private material. Self-authored, not independent.
A. Two team members' reported fatigue rises together on a busy day. C1 contemporaneous association versus feedback; C2 shared workload or interpersonal influence; C3 time-aligned workload and independent repeated reports; C4 no causal direction established. 4/4.
B. A mathematical model reaches a steady solution where both entities' stated goals remain unmet. C1 equilibrium versus goal attainment; C2 compromise or constraint; C3 compare defined goals and observed outcomes; C4 no welfare or consent inference from stability. 4/4.
C. An abrupt change in group attendance follows a timetable change. C1 observed discontinuity versus bifurcation; C2 changed opportunity or nonlinear dynamics; C3 compare relevant contexts and independently recorded transition conditions; C4 no threshold parameter inferred. 4/4.
D. Same-day covariation is present but the next-day association is absent. C1 contemporaneous versus lagged association; C2 short recovery, timing mismatch or measurement; C3 compare appropriate time resolutions and precision; C4 no proof of zero influence. 4/4.
E. An elaborate fitted model explains past fluctuations better than a persistence baseline but predicts held-out observations worse. C1 in-sample fit versus prospective prediction; C2 overfitting or regime shift; C3 repeated chronological evaluation and matched horizon; C4 no superiority claim based on retrospective fit. 4/4.
F. A group mean association differs from one participant's longitudinal pattern. C1 between-person versus within-person estimates; C2 heterogeneity or measurement/context differences; C3 adequate individual observations and comparable constructs; C4 neither population estimate nor anecdote establishes that individual's mechanism. 4/4.
