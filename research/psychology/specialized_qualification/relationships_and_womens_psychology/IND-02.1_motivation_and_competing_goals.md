# IND-02.1 — Motivation: goal value, regulation, competing goals and action

Date: 2026-11-25. Status: bounded research note completed; IND-02 remains IN_PROGRESS.
Scope: adult nonclinical motivation and goal pursuit; not a comprehensive review, individual diagnosis or validated prediction system.

## 1. Question card and baseline

Question: What evidence distinguishes wanting an outcome, valuing it, endorsing a goal, prioritizing it now, expecting success, intending an action, having the opportunity and actually acting? How do other goals and feedback change these relations?
Purpose: avoid reverse inference from an isolated choice to a unique motive or stable value hierarchy. Claim types: construct distinction, association, conditional prediction and bounded experimental effects. Competing hypotheses include low value, low expectancy, competing obligations or goals, lack of resources/opportunity, controlled regulation, alternative means, changed appraisal, disengagement and combinations.
Reviewed MOT-01–04 summaries in RESEARCH_QUEUE and directly inspected MOT-03 sections 2, 4, 6, 8–11, 13, 22, 25, 27–28. Not a full rereading of every foundational file. Sezer et al. is a revalidation of a source already in MOT-03, not a newly discovered foundational theory. Integrate IND-01.3 person × situation × time framework.

## 2. Targeted evidence review

Search date 2026-11-25. Public search terms included goal conflict multiple goals daily life autonomous motivation; self-concordance meta-analysis; interpersonal approach/avoidance goals; goal harmony; cybernetic goal pursuit. Selected primary and synthesis studies relevant to the question, including null and mixed results. This is not a systematic search; no raw data reanalysis. Sources below were checked to the indicated depth; no claim of complete end-to-end reading.

E1. Sezer et al. (online 2024; Motivation Science 11, 259–276, 2025). Goal Motives, Approach/Avoidance Appraisals, Psychological Needs, and Well-Being: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. https://doi.org/10.1037/mot0000366
Author-hosted publisher copy: https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/2024_SezerRiddellEtAl_GoalMotives.pdf
Access: PDF title/abstract, limitations and conclusion directly inspected; abstract page visually checked. 77 studies, 978 effect sizes; model-specific N=10,289 and 39,470 (do not sum as unique participants). Meta-analytic models support associations between self-concordant goals, effort/attainment, need satisfaction and well-being. Separating autonomous and controlled regulation and approach/avoidance appraisals is informative. Predominantly correlational data, limited within-person designs, sparse dark-side pathways and unavailable evidence for the proposed self-concordance × attainment interaction restrict causal and individual interpretation. English-language selection limits transport. Revalidates existing MOT-03 boundary.

E2. Ratchford, Jayawickreme, Jeffrey & Schnitker (2026), A multilevel, longitudinal examination of goal conflict & goal harmony. European Journal of Personality, online March 10. https://doi.org/10.1177/08902070261432959
Access: publisher abstract and plain-language summary; full text restricted. Two studies, total N=849; multilevel random-intercept cross-lagged models. Goal-specific within-person conflict and harmony were negatively related concurrently and across time, while their person-level aggregate association was not significant. Distinguish interference from facilitation, and local goal relation from whole-system characteristic. Non-significance is not proof of exact zero. Cross-lagged association does not identify intervention effects. Effect magnitudes, detailed measurement, missingness and model sensitivity not verified; use at abstract-supported level only.

E3. Szumowska, Jasko, Dukała & Górska (2025), Goal cycle: motivational and emotional antecedents and consequences of goal progress. Motivation and Emotion 49, 384–405. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-025-10130-4
Access: open publisher HTML abstract, both methods portions, selected results, general discussion and conclusion inspected. Three-wave student study N=390; 311 retained at third wave. Unique goal count differs slightly between abstract (3156) and methods (3158); unresolved, not used in inference. Study 2 analyzed 235 of 442 volunteers in a weekly walking challenge; perceived progress experimentally varied, with attrition and an additional non-preregistered comprehension exclusion. Expectancy more consistently predicted progress than importance; importance did not predict later progress in Study 2 and was not consistently predictive across waves in Study 1. Perceived progress positively related to subsequent importance, expectancy and satisfaction; stress result was less consistent. Selected indirect paths are consistent with feedback, but not proof of every mediator or a universal reinforcing cycle. Self-report, measurement overlap (importance index combines absolute and relative items), externally assigned task, short experimental window and exclusions matter. Do not extrapolate experimental procedure to interpersonal manipulation. The claim that importance reliably determines every action is contradicted by these findings.

E4. Woolley, Giurge & Fishbach (2025), Adherence to Personal Resolutions Across Time, Culture, and Goal Domains. Psychological Science. https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976251350960
Access: abstract, introduction, Study 4 results, general discussion and open practices inspected. Studies in US and Chinese adults associate intrinsic motivation with adherence to instrumental personal goals. Study 4 found a small difference in 24-hour app usage between experimental conditions (d=.21, 95% CI [.07,.36] for reported primary contrast); a robustness analysis including zero counts also found a small effect. Probability of initiating any scan did not differ significantly in a nonpreregistered analysis. This distinguishes initiation from extent of activity and short-term behavior from long-term maintenance. Observational year-long evidence is not a year-long randomized intervention. Operational intrinsic/extrinsic contrast concerns activity-as-end versus means; not identical to autonomous/controlled regulation in E1. No proof that enjoyment is necessary for every goal or interpersonal action.

E5. Vowels, Carnelley & Francois-Walcott (2022 issue), Successful negotiation of goal conflict between romantic partners predicts better goal outcomes during COVID-19: A mixed methods study. Journal of Social and Personal Relationships. https://doi.org/10.1177/02654075211033341
Access: publisher abstract, selected theoretical text, results/discussion, limitations and conclusion inspected. Survey N=200, interviews N=48. Repeated diary/weekly reports associate perceived goal conflict with less favorable goal outcomes and perceived successful negotiation with more favorable outcomes. Authors estimate 2–4% outcome variance for conflict, 6–15% for negotiation in this sample. Only one member per couple supplied data, conflict levels were low, pandemic-specific selection and observational design limit inference. No causal or dyadic consensus claim. Mutual voluntary coordination is distinct from pressure or imposed sacrifice.

Evidence synthesis: E3 and E4 supply important limits on using importance, pleasure or action frequency as interchangeable indicators. E1 supports a multicomponent motive model without causal identification of all pathways. E2 is emerging evidence on goal-system structure with restricted access; E5 extends context to relational goal conflict but is not dyadic data. None validates inferring a particular individual's inner motivation from a message or behavior alone. No comprehensive source-status audit or independent replication analysis completed.

## 3. Operational motivational model

For a specified actor, goal and time, distinguish:
- Need: theory-specific condition relevant to functioning, not interchangeable with a desire or preferred outcome.
- Desire: wanting an outcome; can exist without commitment to pursue it.
- Value: subjective desirability/importance of outcome; relative priority is separate from absolute importance.
- Goal content and hierarchy: desired end state, subordinate goal, means/action. Abandoning a means need not mean abandoning the end.
- Regulation quality: intrinsic interest/enjoyment, personally endorsed instrumental importance (identified/integrated), introjected pressure, external regulation; multiple motives can coexist.
- Direction: approach toward desired outcome or avoidance of undesired outcome, independent of endorsement/pressure.
- Expectancy and perceived control: estimated attainability and ability to execute means; may differ from actual opportunity.
- Current allocation: selection among feasible activities under competing goals, time, energy, cost and constraints. A high allocation need not mean high intrinsic interest.
- Intention, plan, execution, actual progress and perceived progress are distinct. Subjective progress may change later value, expectancy and emotion.

This is a conceptual map, not an identified equation or numerical utility model. Goal systems contain facilitating and interfering links, which can vary with context and time; both may coexist at different levels. A momentary priority is not a permanent ranking. Low action does not uniquely identify low motivation; high action does not establish autonomous interest. Neither is proof that a particular hidden motive exists. A statement of desire is evidence of a report, not proof of durable commitment. Repeated voluntary choices under comparable feasible alternatives may improve conditional inference but do not reveal unique causes.

## 4. Analytic protocol

1. Define target outcome and actual question. Distinguish desired state, means, intermediate indicator, and reported or observed behavior.
2. Separate evidence channels and timeframe. Identify what was said, done, possible, constrained and unobserved. Do not confuse external observation with subjective valuation.
3. Map multiple goals, their absolute importance and context-specific relative priority, where evidence supports them. Mark unknown values rather than invent rankings.
4. Compare candidate explanations: genuine value change; reprioritization under resource competition; altered expectancy/control; different means; implementation barrier; pressure versus endorsement; transient state; goal adjustment. Multiple processes may coexist.
5. Compare repeated natural observations under similar opportunities and check changes across contexts. Seek direct voluntary clarification when relevant rather than covert tests. Lack of contrary evidence does not establish a preferred explanation.
6. Examine feedback over time: distinguish progress, perceived progress, emotion, future expectancy and later action. Check whether an immediate response predicts continued autonomous pursuit; neither an immediate improvement nor its absence proves durable motive change.
7. State conditional predictions and discriminating observations, confidence limits and what would cause revision. Do not claim numerical probabilities, prescribe pressure or manufacture situations to test another person's motives. A decision or boundary remains valid without analyst agreement on motive.

## 5. Limited practice checks

Original generic scenarios, not derived from private material. Rubric: identify relevant construct, competing explanation, and evidence/revision condition. Three exercises are self-assessed; external contrast uses published results already seen, not blinded prediction.

A. A potter values improving technique but postpones practice during a studio closure. Value versus available means/opportunity; alternative is shifted priorities. Examine voluntary practice under comparable access and direct account of interest. No conclusion of lost desire. Three rubric elements addressed.
B. A volunteer consistently completes required bookkeeping while finding it tedious and personally important. Intrinsic enjoyment differs from identified regulation; external pressure may coexist. Repeated behavior cannot distinguish the reasons; ask about experienced endorsement without presuming it. Three elements addressed.
C. A musician stops rehearsing with one ensemble but practices the same repertoire elsewhere. Distinguish abandoned means from higher goal. Alternatives include schedule conflict or changed preferences. Compare repertoire, opportunities, reported priorities and subsequent behavior. Three elements addressed.
D. External empirical challenge, E3: if importance alone uniformly predicts action, it should predict subsequent progress in both studies. Observed mixed/null findings contradict that universal proposition. Preserve expectancy, other goals, method and time horizon; the data do not identify a single alternative causal mechanism. E4 initiation null vs usage difference further rejects conflation of start and persistence.

Internal rubric review: A–C address all nine intended elements; not an independent assessment. D is retrospective reasoning from published aggregate results. No external examiner, held-out personal predictions or causal validation of the protocol.
