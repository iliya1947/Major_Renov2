## 3. Homeostasis

### Working definition

Homeostasis describes regulation that keeps key internal variables within viable ranges despite perturbation.

Exercise acutely perturbs:
- energy/substrate state;
- temperature;
- acid-base/ionic conditions;
- cardiovascular demand;
- mechanical tissue state;
- neuroendocrine/immune signaling.

Project rule:

**exercise does not simply “break homeostasis so the body overcompensates.”**

The real system uses multiple interacting feedback and feedforward regulatory processes.

A useful training model must therefore distinguish:
1. acute perturbation;
2. immediate compensation;
3. recovery;
4. longer-term adaptation.

---

## 4. Allostasis

Allostasis is the broader concept of maintaining stability through adaptive change.

It is useful when:
- regulation shifts according to anticipated demand;
- multiple systems coordinate;
- previous exposure, sleep, nutrition, stress and environment modify response.

Project rule:

**homeostasis and allostasis are complementary, not mutually exclusive models.**

### Important boundary

“Allostatic load” is not treated here as:
- one universally validated athlete score;
- a direct prescription threshold;
- proof of overtraining.

Current athlete/military literature still treats allostatic-load application and measurement as evolving.

---

## 5. Stimulus → response → recovery → adaptation

A useful training sequence:

`training exposure → acute perturbation/signaling → transient performance/fatigue response → recovery/remodeling → retained structural/functional change`

Repeated appropriate exposure can accumulate retained changes.

But the arrows are not guaranteed.

An acute stimulus may produce:
- response without meaningful long-term adaptation;
- excessive fatigue;
- damage;
- no detectable change;
- adaptation in one tissue but not another.

Project rule:

**stimulus is necessary for adaptation but acute response magnitude is not automatically a valid surrogate for chronic outcome.**

---

## 6. Acute response vs chronic adaptation

### Acute response
Minutes to days after a session:
- fatigue;
- metabolite changes;
- swelling;
- endocrine response;
- transient signaling;
- soreness;
- temporary strength/power changes.

### Chronic adaptation
Retained change after repeated exposure:
- strength;
- hypertrophy;
- architecture;
- tendon/bone remodeling;
- neural changes;
- metabolic/cardiorespiratory changes.

Current AEL meta-analysis provides a concrete example:
some acute differences such as lactate, GH, RPE or eccentric EMG can differ while pooled chronic strength/architecture outcomes remain similar.

Project rule:

**an acute biomarker is not promoted to a chronic-gain surrogate unless longitudinal evidence supports the link.**

This rule becomes mandatory in PHY-05 and PHY-07.

---

## 7. Overload — correct project definition

Overload means the training exposure must remain sufficiently challenging relative to the organism’s current capacity to continue driving the target adaptation.

It does **not** mean:
- add weight every session forever;
- every workout must exceed the previous workout;
- maximal fatigue is required.

Overload can be changed through:
- external load;
- repetitions;
- sets/volume;
- ROM;
- exercise difficulty/leverage;
- velocity intent;
- density/rest structure;
- frequency;
- task specificity;
- effort/proximity to failure.

Project rule:

**progressive overload = progression of an effective stimulus as capacity changes, not a single mandatory progression variable.**

---

## 8. Overload is target-specific

The dose required for:
- hypertrophy;
- maximal strength;
- power;
- endurance;
- tendon;
- bone

is not identical.

ACSM 2026 synthesis shows different prescription features preferentially influence different outcomes.

Examples:
- heavier loads particularly favor maximal voluntary strength;
- hypertrophy is especially sensitive to sufficient weekly volume;
- power responds to different load/velocity conditions.

Project rule:

**“more overload” has no meaning without naming the target outcome.**

---

## 9. Dose–response

Training dose is multidimensional.

Relevant variables include:
- load/intensity;
- volume;
- frequency;
- effort;
- ROM;
- velocity;
- density/rest;
- exercise selection;
- intervention duration.

There is no single universal scalar dose that fully predicts all adaptations.

Current meta-regression evidence shows:
- volume has positive associations with hypertrophy and strength;
- returns diminish;
- frequency behaves differently for hypertrophy vs strength;
- strength dose-response can be nonlinear.

Project rule:

**dose–response is outcome-specific and commonly nonlinear.**

---

## 10. Diminishing returns

A greater dose can produce additional adaptation while the marginal benefit per additional unit decreases.

This is supported in current resistance-training meta-regression.

Implication:

`more training ≠ proportionally more adaptation`

and at some point additional training can mainly add:
- fatigue;
- time cost;
- recovery demand.

Project rule:

**the practical target is not maximal possible dose; it is sufficient dose with favorable adaptation-to-cost ratio.**

Exact minimum/optimal/maximal volumes remain PRO/PHY-07/08 questions.

---

## 11. Thresholds and “minimum effective dose”

A biological stimulus often needs to exceed a practical threshold to create measurable adaptation.

But the threshold:
- depends on outcome;
- depends on baseline capacity;
- changes with training status;
- can be hard to estimate because measurement noise obscures small adaptations.

Project rule:

**minimum effective dose is not one fixed universal number.**

It is a decision concept requiring:
- outcome definition;
- time horizon;
- measurement precision;
- individual/context.

---

## 12. Specificity

Specificity means adaptation tends to reflect characteristics of the repeated training demand.

Relevant dimensions include:
- movement/task;
- contraction mode;
- force range/load;
- joint angle/ROM;
- velocity;
- muscle length;
- energy-system demand;
- skill/coordination requirements.

Current task-specificity meta-analysis supports stronger improvement in trained/tested strength expressions than in non-trained isometric outcomes.

Project rule:

**training adaptations are specific, but specificity is a gradient, not a binary “transfer / no transfer” rule.**

---

## 13. Transfer

Transfer occurs because tasks can share:
- muscles;
- force capacity;
- neural strategies;
- ROM;
- velocity demands;
- coordination patterns.

Therefore:
- general strength can transfer partly;
- highly task-specific skill/strength can show stronger specificity.

Project rule:

**specificity does not imply that every outcome requires exact rehearsal of the final task.**

Transfer magnitude is an empirical question.

---

## 14. Reversibility

When the training stimulus is reduced or removed:
- some adaptations decay;
- different outcomes decay at different rates;
- not all gains disappear immediately;
- retained adaptation can persist for meaningful periods.

Current detraining evidence shows persistence of some training-derived improvements after exercise withdrawal, with outcome- and time-dependent loss.

Project rule:

**reversibility = decay without sufficient stimulus, not instantaneous reset to baseline.**

---

## 15. Detraining is adaptation-specific

Strength, muscle size, aerobic capacity, metabolic changes and connective-tissue adaptations do not necessarily decay at the same rate.

The decay rate depends on:
- adaptation type;
- training history;
- residual activity;
- duration of cessation;
- age/health;
- measurement method.

Project rule:

**do not use one generic “you lose gains after X weeks” rule across all outcomes.**

Exact time courses belong to later PHY topics/PRO/REC.

---

## 16. Repeated exposure

Adaptation requires repeated exposure because:
- many acute molecular/signaling responses are transient;
- structural remodeling takes repeated cycles;
- skill/neural/tissue changes accumulate across sessions.

Repeated exposure can also modify the response to later exposures.

Example:
- repeated-bout effect reduces damage/soreness/strength loss from subsequent similar eccentric exposures.

Project rule:

**the organism adapts not only in output but also in how it responds to the next bout.**

---

## 17. Repeated-bout effect is not “proof of less adaptation”

A smaller soreness/CK/damage response after familiarization does not mean the training stimulus has become useless.

It can reflect:
- protective structural/neural/cellular adaptation;
- lower damage cost for a similar task.

Project rule:

**reduced damage response with familiarity is compatible with successful adaptation.**

This protects later PHY-11 from “more damage = better training” logic.

---

## 18. Individual variability

Observed response differences arise from multiple sources:

1. true biological differences;
2. baseline status;
3. prior training;
4. age/sex/body size;
5. nutrition/sleep/stress;
6. adherence and actual dose received;
7. measurement error;
8. within-person biological variability;
9. statistical regression/noise.

Project rule:

**observed between-person spread is not automatically evidence of stable “responders” and “nonresponders.”**

---

## 19. Important current nuance — reproducibility of individual response

Current evidence is not uniform across training domains.

### Resistance training
A 2025 repeated-RT study found reproducible individual strength/size responses across repeated exposure and concluded there is a physiological component to response individuality.

### Endurance training
A 2026 repeated-intervention study found limited within-person reproducibility across several physiological adaptations and argued responsiveness is dynamic/context-dependent rather than a fixed trait.

Project synthesis:

**individual variability is real as an observation, but stable personal “trainability” must be demonstrated rather than assumed.**

This is stronger and safer than labeling someone a responder/nonresponder from one block.

---

## 20. Nonresponse

A measured “nonresponse” can occur because:
- true adaptation is small;
- dose is insufficient;
- adherence differs;
- outcome is poorly matched to the adaptation;
- intervention is too short;
- measurement error exceeds the change;
- within-person variability masks it.

Project rule:

**one unchanged measurement after one intervention does not establish a permanent biological inability to adapt.**

This is directly relevant to later IND/TST.

---

## 21. Temporal dynamics

Adaptation is not a single simultaneous event.

Different components can appear at different times:
- neural/skill changes can contribute early;
- muscle size changes accrue over weeks/months;
- tendon/bone remodeling can require different exposure/recovery cycles;
- metabolic/cardiorespiratory changes have their own trajectories.

Project rule:

**the time point of measurement is part of the adaptation definition.**

A “no effect” at one time point may not mean no later effect.

---

## 22. Tissue time courses differ

Current loading review emphasizes that tissues/systems can have different recovery/adaptation timeframes.

Project consequence:

At one moment training can be:
- adequate for muscle;
- excessive for tendon;
- insufficient for bone;
- tolerable systemically.

Project rule:

**one global “recovered/not recovered” state is an oversimplification.**

Detailed tissue-specific time courses remain PHY-06/12/13 and REC.

---

## 23. Adaptation vs fatigue

Training creates at least two practically separable effects:

1. adaptation/fitness-related retained change;
2. fatigue/recovery cost that temporarily reduces performance or readiness.

Observed performance at a given moment reflects both.

Project rule:

**temporary performance decrease after useful training does not mean the underlying adaptation is negative.**

Conversely:
**a session that produces little immediate fatigue is not automatically ineffective.**

---

## 24. Fitness-fatigue models — useful but not literal physiology

Fitness-fatigue models can be useful conceptual/quantitative approximations:
- training generates positive and negative effects with different decay profiles;
- performance reflects their net expression.

But:

- parameters are model-dependent;
- “fitness” and “fatigue” are latent constructs;
- a model fit is not direct measurement of physiological mechanisms.

Project rule:

**use fitness-fatigue as a decision model, not as a literal two-compartment biological law.**

Detailed load-monitoring models → TST/PRO.

---

## 25. Fatigue is task-dependent

Current evidence shows fatigue magnitude depends strongly on:
- set duration;
- proximity to failure;
- volume;
- density/rest;
- load;
- measured outcome.

Project rule:

**fatigue must always be defined relative to a task/outcome/time point.**

“Systemic fatigue” without operational definition is too vague for evidence appraisal.

---

## 26. More fatigue is not more adaptation

High fatigue can coexist with a strong stimulus, but fatigue itself is a cost/response, not the desired chronic adaptation.

Current resistance-training evidence shows:
- going closer to failure and higher volume/density can increase acute fatigue;
- training to momentary failure does not consistently improve long-term outcomes in current ACSM synthesis.

Project rule:

**fatigue is not used as a proxy for stimulus quality.**

This is a major downstream rule for PRO/REC/PHY-05.

---

## 27. Dose, adaptation and fatigue form a trade-off

At low dose:
- stimulus may be too small.

At moderate/effective dose:
- adaptation benefit can rise efficiently.

At higher dose:
- adaptation may continue with diminishing returns;
- fatigue/recovery cost can rise faster.

At excessive/contextually intolerable dose:
- performance/recovery can deteriorate and adherence may fall.

Project rule:

**progression is constrained by recoverability and marginal benefit, not by “more is always better.”**

---

## 28. Adaptation is outcome-specific

A program can simultaneously:
- improve hypertrophy;
- improve strength;
- produce little power improvement;
- create large fatigue;
- have different tendon/bone effects.

Therefore “the program worked” is incomplete.

Project rule:

Always ask:
**which outcome adapted, by how much, over what period, at what cost?**

---

## 29. Adaptation is exposure-specific but organism-modified

The external program is not the same as the internal physiological dose.

Same prescribed session can produce different internal responses because of:
- training status;
- sleep;
- nutrition;
- stress;
- technique;
- body size;
- effort;
- recovery state.

Project rule:

**prescription ≠ received physiological stimulus.**

This supports later monitoring/individualization without requiring a perfect biomarker.

---

## 30. Acute perturbation is not required to be maximal

The body can adapt to repeated submaximal stimuli.

Current resistance-training evidence shows multiple prescriptions improve strength/hypertrophy compared with no training.

Project rule:

**effective adaptation does not require maximal acute disruption, soreness, hormonal response or exhaustion.**

---

## 31. Supercompensation model — restricted use

The classic simple curve:
`stress → fatigue → recovery → supercompensation`

can be useful pedagogically.

But it is too simple to serve as a literal universal biological schedule because:
- different tissues recover/adapt on different time scales;
- repeated training sessions overlap;
- adaptation can continue under accumulated training;
- no single performance marker captures all systems.

Project rule:

**do not schedule training solely from a universal supercompensation clock.**

---

## 32. Homeostasis / allostasis / hormesis boundary

A challenge can be adaptive when:
- dose is tolerable;
- recovery/resources are sufficient;
- repeated exposure produces beneficial retained change.

But the project does not assume a universal inverted-U/hormetic dose curve for every outcome/tissue.

Project rule:

**“stress is good in the right dose” is a broad heuristic, not a quantified prescription law.**

---

## 33. Practical definition of successful adaptation

For this project:

**successful adaptation = a retained improvement in a target outcome or target physiological capacity attributable to repeated training exposure, large enough to matter relative to measurement noise and practical cost.**

This definition requires:
- target outcome;
- baseline;
- repeated exposure;
- time horizon;
- measurement method;
- uncertainty.

It prevents acute feelings/soreness/pump from being treated as success.

---

## 34. Practical definition of maladaptation

Maladaptation is not simply:
- one bad workout;
- transient soreness;
- one poor readiness score.

A maladaptive pattern requires evidence of persistent/unwanted changes such as:
- declining performance;
- impaired recovery;
- symptoms/injury;
- persistent physiological or psychological disturbance;
- failure of expected progress despite adequate exposure.

Detailed overreaching/overtraining/pathology → REC/MED/PRO.

## 35. What PHY-01 can and cannot infer

### PHY-01 can establish

- adaptation requires repeated effective exposure;
- dose–response is outcome-specific and nonlinear;
- specificity is graded;
- reversibility is time/outcome dependent;
- fatigue and adaptation are separable;
- acute responses are not automatic chronic surrogates;
- individual response classification requires repeated reliable data;
- tissues differ in time course.

- exact sets/week;
- exact RIR;
- exact frequency;
- exact deload schedule;

## 37. Established findings

### HIGH

1. Repeated resistance training produces chronic strength/hypertrophy/function adaptations.
2. Acute responses and chronic adaptations are distinct evidence domains.
3. Overload is target-relative and can be progressed through multiple training variables.
4. Dose–response is multidimensional and commonly nonlinear with diminishing returns.
5. Specificity exists across trained/tested task dimensions, but transfer is not zero.
6. Adaptations decay with insufficient continued stimulus, but not instantaneously or uniformly.
7. Fatigue magnitude is task/protocol/time dependent.
8. More acute fatigue does not guarantee greater chronic adaptation.
9. Different tissues/systems can have different recovery/adaptation time courses.
10. One apparent individual response/nonresponse is insufficient to prove stable trainability.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

1. Exact shape/plateau of dose–response for a specific person.
2. Exact detraining time course for each adaptation.
3. Stable reproducibility of individual responsiveness across domains.
4. Quantitative allostatic-load indices for training decisions.
5. Exact fitness-fatigue model parameters.

## 38. Important current evidence updates

1. **ACSM 2026** updates the previous 2009 resistance-training position stand and supports progressive RT while showing adaptation-specific prescription effects rather than one universal method.
2. **2026 volume/frequency meta-regression** supports diminishing returns and different dose-response behavior for strength vs hypertrophy.
3. **2025 task-specificity meta-analysis** reinforces graded transfer rather than complete transfer.
4. **2026 AEL meta-analysis** shows acute physiological differences can coexist with similar chronic outcomes, supporting the acute≠chronic-surrogate rule.
5. **2025 RT fatigue review** shows proximity to failure, set duration, volume and density strongly affect acute fatigue.
6. **2025 repeated RT** and **2026 repeated endurance training** studies produce different conclusions about reproducibility of individual responsiveness; stable “responder type” therefore cannot be assumed generically.
7. **2026 detraining synthesis** supports outcome- and time-dependent persistence rather than immediate loss of all training effects.
