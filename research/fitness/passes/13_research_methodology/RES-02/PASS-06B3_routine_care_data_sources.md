# Sources checked

## 1. Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Prognosis Research and Prediction Models — Chapter 2

Moons KGM, Riley RD, Damen JAA.  
**Chapter 2: Study designs and data sources in prognosis research.**  
Draft version: **March 2026**.

Current official Cochrane chapter checked 2026-11-17.

Key current guidance:

- study design and data source are related but distinct;
- EHR / routine healthcare / administrative registries are increasingly used for all prognosis families;
- such data often were not set up for a specific prognosis question;
- routine data may lack specific inclusion criteria, defined follow-up and prespecified measurement times;
- routine data often have lower or more heterogeneous data quality and more missing relevant prognosis variables;
- harmonization across practices/sites is often necessary;
- linkage can enable more complete longitudinal patient journeys.

https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/cochrane-handbook-systematic-reviews-prognosis-research-and-prediction-models/prognosis-handbook-chapter-2-study-designs-and-data-sources-prognosis-research

The chapter is explicitly a **draft March 2026**, subject to change before finalization.

## 2. Using routinely collected data for research purposes: challenges and mitigation strategies

BMJ. Accepted 25 March 2026; published 2026.

Current BMJ methods article checked 2026-11-17.

Key points used here:

- routinely collected data are defined by origin, not by one study design;
- EHR, claims/billing, registries, wearables/apps can all be routine data;
- "real-world data/evidence" is too ambiguous to substitute for explicit design description;
- prediction requires correct time-point alignment;
- data leakage can arise when information unavailable at prediction time enters predictors;
- measurement quality, coding, documentation and missingness are generated by healthcare workflows;
- aggressive cleaning can even reduce deployment realism if deployment data will be noisier;
- data distributions and documentation can drift across sites and calendar time.

https://www.bmj.com/content/393/bmj-2025-087812

# Part A — Routine-care data are a data source, not a design label

## 1. Core correction

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane explicitly separates:

1. **study design**;
2. **data source**.

EHR, registry or administrative claims identify where records come from.

They do not by themselves tell us whether the prognosis study is:

- cohort;
- nested case-control;
- case-cohort;
- non-nested case-control;
- prediction-model development;
- external validation;
- another structure.

### Project rule

Do not classify a paper as:

> "EHR study"

and stop there.

Required form:

> scientific purpose + longitudinal/sampling design + data source.

Example:

> prognostic-factor study + retrospectively constructed cohort + multi-site EHR data.

---

## 2. "Real-world data" is not a sufficient design description

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Current BMJ methods guidance highlights that "real-world data/evidence" is used for many incompatible study types.

Therefore:

> real-world = provenance/context label, not a scientific design.

Project preferred term:

> routinely collected data

when describing origin of records.

Then explicitly describe the actual design.

---

# Part B — What counts as routinely collected data

## 3. Routine-data families

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

RECORD and current BMJ guidance include sources such as:

1. EHR / EMR;
2. primary-care databases;
3. administrative / insurance / billing claims;
4. disease or procedure registries;
5. public-health reporting systems;
6. mortality/morbidity registries;
7. linked routine-care databases;
8. increasingly, routine wearable/app data.

### Important qualifier

"Routine" means the data were not originally collected specifically for the prognosis question now being asked.

Some registries can contain a mixture of:

- protocol-driven registry-specific collection;
- imported routine-care data;
- linked external data.

Therefore registry status must not be assumed from its name.

---

# Part C — EHR data

## 4. EHR data reflect care workflow, not a research protocol

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

EHR variables are produced through:

- clinical encounters;
- orders;
- tests;
- documentation;
- medications;
- billing;
- referrals;
- local workflows.

Thus the observed data pattern is partly a record of:

> what clinicians chose to measure/document and when,

not simply a neutral measurement of the patient's underlying state.

### Consequence for prognosis

A laboratory value may be missing because:

- it was unnecessary;
- clinician did not order it;
- patient did not attend;
- test occurred outside the captured system;
- technical/system factors prevented capture.

Therefore missingness can carry information about the care process.

Detailed missing-data inference is deferred.

---

## 5. "First EHR entry" is not automatically prognosis T=0

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Current BMJ guidance emphasizes that the first record in a routine dataset may have no clinically meaningful relation to the scientific prediction task.

Examples:

- insurance enrollment date;
- transfer into a health system;
- first digital record after an EHR migration;
- first visit visible to one database.

### Project rule

A prognosis startpoint must be **scientifically defined and reconstructable**, for example:

- first qualifying diagnosis;
- surgery date;
- return-to-training clearance;
- first treatment initiation;
- first eligible assessment.

Do not let database availability define T=0 by convenience.

---

# Part D — Registries

## 6. Registry != automatically cohort study

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

A registry is an organized data resource around:

- a disease;
- procedure;
- treatment;
- population;
- service;
- outcome domain.

It may resemble a longitudinal cohort when:

1. entry criteria are defined;
2. individuals are followed over time;
3. predictors/outcomes are collected in interpretable temporal order.

But the registry itself can also support:

- cross-sectional studies;
- case-control sampling;
- nested studies;
- randomized registry trials;
- secondary linked analyses.

### Project rule

Record:

> registry as data source / infrastructure

separately from:

> analytic study design.

---

## 7. Registry-specific collection may be stronger than ordinary EHR capture

### Established finding
**Confidence: MODERATE-HIGH**

AHRQ guidance notes that registries may actively collect data for registry purposes using explicit procedures.

Therefore:

> registry data quality cannot be inferred from the word "registry" alone.

Need to extract:

1. what was collected specifically for registry purposes;
2. what was imported from EHR/claims;
3. what was linked from external sources;
4. measurement schedules;
5. completeness/validation procedures.

---

# Part E — Administrative / claims data

## 8. Administrative data encode transactions, not necessarily constructs

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Administrative/claims systems primarily support activities such as:

- billing;
- reimbursement;
- coverage;
- utilization tracking;
- health-system administration.

Variables may be represented through:

- diagnosis codes;
- procedure codes;
- prescription/dispensing records;
- encounter records;
- cost/utilization fields.

### Consequence

A code's presence or absence may depend on:

- reimbursement incentives;
- coding rules;
- documentation practices;
- system changes;
- eligibility/coverage;
- healthcare contact.

Therefore a coded variable is not automatically equivalent to:

> research-grade measurement of the underlying clinical/physiological construct.

---

## 9. Administrative data can still provide powerful follow-up

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

When coverage is broad and linkage is reliable, administrative data can support:

- hospitalization outcomes;
- procedures;
- medication dispensing;
- utilization;
- mortality linkage;
- long-term follow-up.

### Project rule

Do not globally rate administrative data as "bad".

Assess fitness-for-purpose variable by variable:

- predictor;
- outcome;
- startpoint;
- follow-up;
- target population.

---

# Part F — Population hierarchy

## 10. RECORD population hierarchy

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

RECORD distinguishes:

1. **source population**  
   population from which the database population arises and about which inference may be intended;

2. **database population**  
   people represented in the actual data resource;

3. **study population**  
   subset selected by researchers using eligibility rules, codes and algorithms.

### Project consequence

A 10-million-person database does not define the prognosis target by itself.

Need to know:

`source population → database capture → study-selection algorithm → final prognosis cohort`

---

## 11. Database population can differ systematically from source population

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Examples:

- one insurer captures only its beneficiaries;
- one hospital network captures people who seek care there;
- specialist registry captures more severe disease;
- fitness app captures users willing to use that app/device.

Therefore:

> database membership itself can be a selection mechanism.

This affects applicability even before the final study inclusion algorithm is applied.

---

# Part G — Building a prognosis cohort inside routine data

## 12. Routine database does not supply a prognosis cohort automatically

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane notes that routine datasets may lack:

- explicit participant inclusion criteria;
- defined follow-up;
- prespecified predictor timing;
- prespecified outcome measurement times.

Therefore the researcher must construct an analytic cohort.

Minimum steps:

1. define target health/training state;
2. define observable eligibility criteria;
3. define T=0;
4. define predictor look-back window;
5. define outcome;
6. define prediction horizon;
7. define end of observable follow-up;
8. define database coverage requirements;
9. define exclusions that prevent coherent follow-up.

---

## 13. Startpoint emulation / reconstruction

### Project synthesis
**Confidence: HIGH**

For routine data, the prognosis startpoint often has to be **emulated from recorded events** rather than observed as a purpose-built research baseline.

Examples:

- first qualifying diagnosis code + confirmatory event;
- first prescription of a drug;
- discharge after a defined event;
- first gym-program registration with baseline test;
- first wearable-defined eligible day after enrollment.

### Requirement

The emulated startpoint must correspond to:

> the real-world moment when the intended prognosis would be made.

If it does not, the study is answering a different question.

# Part H — Predictor timing and availability

## 14. "Recorded before outcome" is too weak

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

For a predictor to belong in a T=0 prediction task, it must be:

1. measured/known before or at T=0;
2. available to the intended user at that moment;
3. defined using data that do not leak future information.

### Example

If T=0 = first training-session date:

- prior 30-day sleep average can be a baseline predictor;
- sleep recorded during weeks 1–4 cannot be used for a pure week-0 prediction without redefining the task.

---

## 15. Event timestamp and information-availability timestamp may differ

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Current BMJ guidance highlights that for prediction the relevant time may be when information becomes available, not merely when the biological/clinical event occurred.

Example:

- blood draw at 09:00;
- result available at 14:00.

A prediction supposedly made at 10:00 cannot legitimately use the 14:00 result.

### Project record

Where relevant store:

1. event/measurement time;
2. result availability time;
3. intended prediction time.

---

# Part I — Outcome ascertainment

## 16. Routine outcomes may be incompletely observable

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

A database may fail to capture outcomes occurring:

- outside the health system;
- after insurance loss;
- in another region;
- in another gym/app/device ecosystem;
- before/after database coverage.

### Consequence

"no recorded outcome" does not always mean:

> no outcome occurred.

It can mean:

> outcome not observed in this source.

This is structurally different from ordinary random missingness.

---

## 17. Linked sources can improve outcome completeness

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane highlights linkage to:

- mortality registries;
- morbidity registries;
- other healthcare sources

as a way to reconstruct more complete longitudinal trajectories.

### Project rule

Record:

1. sources linked;
2. linkage level/method if reported;
3. which variables/outcomes come from each source;
4. whether linkage changes population coverage;
5. whether unlinked participants differ.

Detailed linkage-error appraisal is deferred.

---

# Part J — Measurement heterogeneity

## 18. Same variable name can represent different measurement processes

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Across practices/sites, the same field can differ in:

- instrument;
- laboratory assay;
- unit;
- calibration;
- clinical indication;
- collection protocol;
- operator;
- recording convention;
- update frequency.

Therefore:

> same database column label ≠ guaranteed measurement equivalence.

### Example

"body weight" may come from:

- clinic scale;
- patient self-report;
- imported home scale;
- previous note;
- copied-forward value.

For a prognosis model, these are not necessarily interchangeable.

---

## 19. Harmonization is part of the design reconstruction

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane explicitly notes the need to harmonize:

- definitions;
- data types;
- measurement methods;
- predictor timing;
- outcome timing

across routine-care sites/settings.

### Project rule

If harmonization materially changes variable meaning, record it as part of the operational definition.

Do not describe the predictor only by semantic name.

---

# Part K — Missingness is generated by care and measurement processes

## 20. Absence of measurement can be informative

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

In routine care, measurements are often ordered selectively.

Therefore missingness may depend on:

- symptoms;
- disease severity;
- clinician judgement;
- healthcare access;
- visit frequency;
- insurance;
- site practice;
- calendar time.

### Project consequence

Before any statistical handling, characterize:

> why this variable would or would not be measured in the source system.

Detailed MAR/MNAR and imputation methodology belongs to later statistics topics.

---

## 21. More cleaning is not always more deployable prediction

### Established finding
**Confidence: MODERATE-HIGH**

Current BMJ guidance makes an important prediction-specific point:

If training data are cleaned into a quality level unavailable at deployment, observed model performance may not represent real use.

### Project translation

For prediction-model research:

- remove clear errors;
- document transformations;
- but preserve awareness of the **intended deployment measurement process**.

A model trained on manually adjudicated research-grade predictors may not transport to noisy routine fields.

---

# Part L — Coding and documentation drift

## 22. Codes are historically contingent

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

RECORD highlights that administrative/clinical coding can change due to:

- classification-system revisions;
- billing incentives;
- policy changes;
- stigma/penalties;
- documentation practices.

### Project rule

Calendar time is part of the data-generating system.

A code-based phenotype validated in one era may not have identical meaning later.

---

## 23. Site drift and system drift

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Current BMJ methods guidance notes that:

- documentation;
- diagnoses;
- treatments;
- guidelines;
- software/data systems

can change across sites and time.

Therefore prediction performance can shift even when the mathematical model is unchanged.

### Project rule

For routine-data prognosis, extract:

1. data years;
2. sites/systems;
3. relevant coding versions;
4. known major workflow/system changes;
5. intended deployment period/site.

---

# Part M — Data linkage does not erase source-specific meaning

## 24. Linked database is still a composition of sources

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Linkage can combine:

- EHR;
- claims;
- registries;
- mortality data;
- laboratory systems;
- wearable/app data.

But each variable retains its own provenance and measurement process.

### Project rule

For major predictors/outcomes record **provenance by variable**, not only:

> linked database used.

This becomes particularly important when one source has high-quality outcome ascertainment but another has noisy predictors.

---

# Part N — Routine data and AI / machine learning

## 25. AI/ML does not solve the design problem

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane notes that routine datasets are frequently used for AI/ML prognosis research, but the underlying data limitations remain.

### Project rule

Machine learning cannot repair by itself:

- undefined T=0;
- future leakage;
- nonrepresentative database membership;
- missing outcome capture;
- variable-definition drift;
- inappropriate predictor availability.

Thus:

> algorithm sophistication is downstream of design reconstruction.

# Part O — Fitness/exercise translations

## 26. Gym-management database

Data source:

- membership records;
- attendance scans;
- program enrollment;
- recorded loads/reps;
- bodyweight entries.

Question:

> At the first day of a new 12-week program, what baseline variables predict dropout by week 12?

Valid prognosis reconstruction requires:

1. define program-start T=0;
2. ensure baseline predictors are known by T=0;
3. distinguish true dropout from membership transfer/data loss;
4. define what "dropout" means;
5. account for missing sessions that may reflect unrecorded training elsewhere.

Classification:

> prognostic-factor or prediction-model study + retrospectively constructed cohort + gym-management routine data.

Not merely:

> database study.

---

## 27. Wearable/app data

Question:

> Can prior 14-day sleep/activity patterns predict failure to complete the next 4 weeks of prescribed training?

Need to define:

1. prediction time;
2. required prior wear-time;
3. device/software version;
4. whether missing days mean nonwear vs no activity;
5. adherence outcome;
6. whether participants can switch devices;
7. whether processing algorithms changed.

The app/device ecosystem is part of the measurement environment.

---

## 28. Linked clinic + training data

Potential source:

- injury clinic EHR;
- rehabilitation registry;
- gym training logs.

Question:

> At return-to-training clearance, what predicts reinjury over 6 months?

Need to establish:

1. clearance date = T=0;
2. baseline factors known then;
3. reinjury capture across systems;
4. whether athletes leaving the system remain observable;
5. provenance of each predictor;
6. whether external training exposure is captured.

# Practical rules produced by PASS-06B3

1. EHR, registry and claims are data sources, not complete study-design labels.
2. Routine-data prognosis still requires an explicit longitudinal scientific structure from T=0 to future outcome.
3. "Real-world data" must not substitute for design classification.
4. A database can contain millions of records and still fail to define a coherent prognosis cohort.
5. Source population, database population and study population must be separated.
6. Database membership is itself a potential selection mechanism.
7. Routine-data prognosis commonly requires explicit analytic-cohort construction.
8. The first visible record is not automatically prognosis T=0.
9. T=0 should represent the intended real-world prediction moment.
10. Predictor availability is judged at that moment, not merely by whether a historical record exists somewhere in the database.
11. Event time and information-availability time may differ.
12. Future information in baseline predictors is temporal leakage unless the prediction task is redefined.
13. "No recorded outcome" is not automatically "no outcome".
14. Outcome observability depends on system coverage and linkage.
15. Registry status does not guarantee protocolized or research-grade data; source architecture must be inspected.
16. Administrative codes represent transactions/documentation processes as well as health states.
17. Same variable name across sites may represent different measurement processes.
18. Harmonization of definitions, methods and timing is part of study reconstruction.
19. Missingness in routine data is often generated by care/workflow and can be informative.
20. Cleaning routine data beyond what deployment can reproduce may inflate apparent prediction transportability.
21. Coding systems, documentation, care standards and software change over calendar time.
22. Data linkage expands the longitudinal patient journey but does not erase source-specific measurement meaning.
23. For linked datasets, major variables should retain provenance.
24. AI/ML does not compensate for undefined startpoints, leakage, incomplete outcomes or population-selection problems.
25. Routine-data research must be classified as:
   **scientific purpose + study/sampling design + data source + reconstructed time architecture**.
