# 3. Intellectual lineage

The word **rhythmanalysis** predates Lefebvre.

Historical lineage includes:

- Pinheiro dos Santos;
- Bachelard.

Lefebvre, working with Catherine Régulier, transformed rhythmanalysis into a social/spatial critique of everyday life.

Key moments:

1. Lefebvre & Régulier — **Le projet rythmanalytique** (1985);
2. Lefebvre — **Éléments de rythmanalyse** (1992);
3. English-language uptake through **Rhythmanalysis: Space, Time and Everyday Life** (2004).

### Project lineage representation

earlier philosophical rhythmanalysis  
→  
Lefebvre/Régulier social-spatial rhythmanalysis  
→  
geography / urban studies / sociology / organization studies  
→  
sport / coaching / health / more-than-human applications.

---

# 4. Original status: project/orientation, not fixed method

A critical current 2026 source explicitly states that Lefebvre’s original articulation was:

- conceptual;
- philosophical;
- an orientation toward time, space and body;

rather than a concrete, fully specified research method.

This matters because:

> any project taxonomy that stores “Rhythmanalysis” as one rigid fixed procedure would misrepresent the foundational lineage.

### Current development

The field has subsequently developed rhythmanalysis into:

- analytic lens;
- strategy of inquiry;
- sensitising concept;
- methodology;
- research method;
- multi-method empirical framework.

### Project conclusion

The label must be routed by **enactment**, not terminology alone.

---

# 5. Scientific object: rhythm

The central object is **rhythm**.

A canonical Lefebvrean formulation defines rhythm through interaction among:

- place;
- time;
- expenditure of energy.

### Project representation

**rhythm = patterned relation of time + place/space + embodied/material energy/activity.**

This means rhythm is not reducible to:

- clock time;
- frequency;
- repetition count;
- subjective feeling;
- movement alone.

---

# 6. Rhythm is repetition plus difference

Rhythm requires repetition.

But repetition is never perfectly identical.

Each return contains:

- variation;
- difference;
- modulation;
- interruption;
- change.

### Critical rule

A perfectly static repeated count is not sufficient to capture the full Lefebvrean concept.

Scientific interest lies partly in:

> how repetition reproduces order while simultaneously allowing difference.

---

# 7. Rhythm links stability and change

Current 2026 methodological literature emphasizes that rhythmanalysis can overcome a false opposition between:

- entities vs processes;
- stability vs change.

A routine can be:

- stable enough to be recognizable;
- changing in each enactment.

### Sport example

Weekly training rhythm may reproduce:

- hierarchy;
- athlete expectations;
- recovery patterns;

while every week contains different:

- injuries;
- selections;
- weather;
- fatigue;
- tactical demands.

---

# 8. Linear rhythms

Linear rhythms are commonly associated with:

- social organization;
- production;
- schedules;
- institutional clock-time;
- work routines;
- standardized repetition.

Examples:

- work shift;
- training schedule;
- school timetable;
- clinic appointments;
- competition calendar.

### Critical rule

“Linear” does not simply mean:

> time moves forward.

It refers to socially organized forms of recurrence/order.

---

# 9. Cyclical rhythms

Cyclical rhythms include recurring natural/biological processes such as:

- day/night;
- seasons;
- breathing;
- sleep/wake;
- bodily cycles.

### Important

Lefebvrean analysis is especially interested in interference between:

**linear social rhythms  
↔  
cyclical bodily/natural rhythms.**

### Sport example

A fixed 6:00 training session:

linear institutional schedule

may conflict with:

sleep/circadian/recovery rhythms.

---

# 10. Linear and cyclical rhythms are not isolated domains

Real situations combine them.

Example:

professional football week:

- match schedule;
- training timetable;
- media obligations;
- sleep;
- menstrual cycle;
- fatigue;
- daylight;
- transport.

### Project rule

Store:

- linear rhythms;
- cyclical rhythms;
- their interactions.

Do not classify one study as exclusively “linear” merely because schedules are visible.

---

# 11. Polyrhythmia

**Polyrhythmia** refers to coexistence of multiple rhythms.

It is the normal condition rather than an unusual exception.

Examples in sport:

- athlete body;
- coach schedule;
- team timetable;
- stadium;
- weather;
- opponents;
- media;
- transport;
- crowd.

### Critical rule

Polyrhythmia ≠ harmony.

Multiple rhythms can coexist while:

- supporting;
- competing;
- dominating;
- disrupting

one another.

---

# 12. Eurhythmia

**Eurhythmia** describes rhythms working together in a relatively harmonious/functional relation.

This does not require:

- identical tempo;
- permanent synchronization.

### Surfing example

Temporary coordination among:

- swell;
- wave;
- board;
- body;
- paddling;
- position;
- current.

### Critical rule

Eurhythmia should not be operationalized as:

> perfect synchronization.

It may be temporary and unstable.

---

# 13. Arrhythmia

**Arrhythmia** denotes discordance/conflict among rhythms.

It can reveal:

- suffering;
- interruption;
- breakdown;
- incompatibility;
- political tension.

### Health example

Hospital institutional rhythms can conflict with:

- bodily urgency;
- dementia-related needs;
- sleep/rest;
- individual pace.

### Critical rule

Arrhythmia is not automatically pathological in a biomedical sense.

It can be:

- social;
- institutional;
- embodied;
- political.

---

# 14. Isorhythmia

**Isorhythmia** refers to equivalence/equalization of rhythms.

It appears less often than:

- polyrhythmia;
- eurhythmia;
- arrhythmia.

### Project rule

Store it when explicitly used.

Do not force every rhythmanalytical study to identify all four rhythm categories.

---

# 15. Categories are analytic tools, not compulsory bins

A common failure would be:

1. collect interviews;
2. assign every quote to:
   - polyrhythmia;
   - eurhythmia;
   - arrhythmia;
   - isorhythmia;
3. call it rhythmanalysis.

### Critical rule

Rhythm concepts must help explain:

- temporal-spatial relations;
- embodiment;
- repetition/difference;
- coordination/disruption.

They should not become an arbitrary codebook detached from rhythm.

---

# 16. Dressage

Lefebvre uses **dressage** to describe training/disciplining of bodies into rhythms.

This can include:

- work routines;
- institutional schedules;
- bodily comportment;
- repeated habits;
- temporal discipline.

### Sport relevance

Sport is especially compatible with this concept because bodies are literally trained into:

- technical rhythms;
- tactical rhythms;
- weekly schedules;
- recovery routines;
- competition calendars.

### Critical rule

Dressage is not synonymous with:

> coaching.

It refers to how bodies become synchronized with imposed or normalized rhythms.

---

# 17. Dominating and dominated rhythms

Rhythms are political.

Some rhythms gain priority.

Examples:

- broadcast schedule over athlete recovery;
- ward routine over patient bodily needs;
- coach timetable over individual pace;
- work schedule over sleep.

### Project rule

Extract:

- which rhythm dominates?;
- which is subordinated?;
- how is dominance enforced?;
- what resistance/disruption occurs?

---

# 18. Rhythm and power

Rhythmanalysis is not merely descriptive temporal mapping.

Lefebvre’s critique of everyday life connects rhythm to:

- capitalism;
- production;
- institutional organization;
- domination;
- alienation.

### Critical rule

A study may use rhythmanalysis descriptively.

But critical power analysis is part of the strong Lefebvrean lineage and should be separately stored.

---

# 19. Pacemakers

Later rhythmanalytical literature uses **pacemakers** to identify actors/elements that strongly organize rhythm.

Potential pacemakers:

- timetable;
- clock;
- traffic signal;
- match schedule;
- coach;
- software;
- algorithm;
- transport departure.

### Critical rule

Pacemaker is not automatically equivalent to causal variable.

It is an organizing rhythmic element.

---

# 20. Rhythmic anchors/elements

Current 2026 methodological work develops additional concepts such as:

- rhythmic elements;
- rhythmic anchors.

These help identify relatively stable features around which rhythms organize.

### Project status

Store as:

**later methodological extension**, not foundational Lefebvrean universal vocabulary.

---

# 21. Body of the rhythmanalyst

One of the most distinctive methodological commitments is:

> the rhythmanalyst uses their own body as a reference for rhythms.

The body can sense:

- pace;
- fatigue;
- repetition;
- waiting;
- synchronization;
- tension;
- acceleration;
- interruption.

### Critical rule

Researcher embodiment is not merely reflexive biography.

It is part of the rhythmanalytical instrument.

---

# 22. Body as metronome — with caution

Later scholarship often describes the researcher’s body as a kind of:

> metronome.

This captures embodied comparison with surrounding rhythms.

### Important caveat

The body is not an objective calibrated measuring instrument.

Its rhythms are:

- situated;
- variable;
- socially shaped.

### Project rule

Record:

- researcher bodily position;
- exposure;
- participation;
- bodily sensations;
- reflexive limitations.

---

# 23. Inside and outside

Lefebvrean rhythmanalysis requires a methodological tension:

- be sufficiently **inside** a rhythm to feel/be grasped by it;
- sufficiently **outside** to analyze it.

### Project representation

**engagement ↔ detachment**

not one fixed position.

### Example

A surfing researcher may need:

- actual embodied surf participation;
- later reflective distance.

---

# 24. “To grasp a rhythm one must be grasped by it”

This principle supports:

- immersion;
- duration;
- bodily participation;
- sustained attentiveness.

### Critical rule

It does not require every researcher to physically participate in all practices.

For example:

- hazardous clinical work;
- surgery;
- elite sport

may make full participation impossible.

### Alternative

Researchers can still use:

- observation;
- sound;
- time-lapse;
- interviews;
- environmental measurements.

---

# 25. Listening and sensing

Rhythmanalysis is frequently described as requiring:

- listening;
- watching;
- sensing;
- feeling;
- attunement.

The researcher attends to:

- what repeats;
- what changes;
- pauses;
- noise;
- acceleration;
- synchrony;
- friction.

### Critical rule

This is more than generic “being observant.”

Attention is organized around rhythm.

---

# 26. Methods are plural

No single data-collection method defines rhythmanalysis.

Current literature uses combinations of:

- participant observation;
- non-participant observation;
- ethnography;
- fieldnotes;
- interviews;
- documentary analysis;
- photography;
- video;
- time-lapse;
- audio;
- walking/following;
- diaries;
- maps;
- physiological measurement;
- environmental sensing;
- creative writing;
- vignettes.

### Taxonomy consequence

Rhythmanalysis is better represented by:

**scientific object + conceptual architecture + embodied analytic orientation**

than by one mandatory technique.

# 27. Empirical rhythmanalysis can be highly structured

The 2026 Smíchov station paper proposes an explicit sequential framework:

1. documentary analysis + unstructured observation;
2. time-lapse capture;
3. semi-structured interviews;
4. structured following/observation.

### Important

The authors do not claim this sequence is universal.

They present it as one transparent empirical framework.

### Project rule

Do not make this four-stage sequence mandatory.

---

# 28. Ethnography is common but not mandatory

Many rhythmanalyses are ethnographic or ethnographically informed.

Examples:

- coaching;
- organizational life;
- running;
- public space.

But other studies use:

- reanalysis of existing observational data;
- mixed environmental/physiological measurement;
- memory/vignettes;
- visual methods.

### Critical rule

Rhythmanalysis ≠ ethnography by definition.

---

# 29. Interviews

Interviews can provide:

- participant perception of rhythms;
- schedules;
- disruptions;
- bodily experience;
- institutional expectations.

### Limitation

Interview-only accounts may miss:

- actual timing;
- embodied rhythms;
- nonhuman rhythms;
- tacit synchronization.

### Project rule

Interview-only rhythmanalysis has a higher burden to demonstrate how rhythm is empirically/analytically accessed.

---

# 30. Observation

Observation is especially useful for:

- sequences;
- flows;
- timing;
- interaction;
- routines;
- waiting;
- movement.

### Critical rule

Observation becomes rhythmanalytical only when interpreted through rhythm relations, not merely described chronologically.

---

# 31. Visual/time-lapse methods

Time-lapse can reveal:

- slow rhythms;
- long-duration patterns;
- flows;
- congestion;
- recurrence.

### Limitation

It can suppress:

- lived bodily experience;
- sound;
- subjective meaning;
- subtle interaction.

### Project rule

Use complementary methods where needed.

---

# 32. Audio

Sound can reveal:

- pulses;
- repeated signals;
- silence;

Rhythm may be easier to perceive acoustically than visually.

### Project route

Store:

- audio recording?;

# 33. Walking / following / mobile methods

Rhythmanalysis can use:

- walking;
- following people;
- mobile observation;
- moving interviews.

These can help access:

- tempo;

# 39. Boundary with PQI/posthuman inquiry

D38 route and rhythmanalysis overlap in:

- relationality;
- materiality;
- more-than-human relations;
- becoming/process.

### But

Rhythmanalysis has a distinct:

- Lefebvre/Régulier lineage;
- rhythm-centred scientific object;
- linear/cyclical/polyrhythmia/eurhythmia/arrhythmia vocabulary;
- body-as-rhythmic-reference architecture.

### Rule

A study may be:

`rhythmanalysis + posthuman/new-materialist orientation`

but neither label automatically implies the other.

# 40. Boundary with longitudinal qualitative inquiry

D10 longitudinal qualitative research:

- follows entity/experience over multiple points;
- asks about change/continuity/trajectory.

Rhythmanalysis:

- asks how rhythms organize time-space-body/activity;
- may use one field episode;
- may use long-term immersion;
- may use archival/reanalysis;
- does not require repeated participant waves.

### Critical rule

**trajectory ≠ rhythm.**

---

# 41. Boundary with temporal analysis generally

A paper may discuss:

- timing;
- chronology;
- phases;
- schedules.

This is not enough.

### Minimum rhythmanalytical evidence

At least one of:

- explicit Lefebvrean/Régulier lineage;
- rhythm-centred research object;
- rhythm conceptual architecture;
- embodied/time-space rhythmic analysis.

# 42. Boundary with ethnography

Ethnography:

> understand culture/practice/context.

Rhythmanalysis:

> understand patterned temporal-spatial-embodied relations and their organization/disruption.

### Hybrid route

Many studies will be:

`ethnography + rhythmanalytical analytic/methodological layer`.

### Critical rule

Do not replace ethnography label when authors explicitly retain it.

# 43. Boundary with sensory/carnal ethnography

Sensory/carnal ethnography:

- senses;
- embodied participation;
- somatic knowing;
- apprenticeship.

Rhythmanalysis:

- rhythm;
- repetition/difference;

### Critical rule

Embodied immersion ≠ rhythmanalysis automatically.

---

# 44. Boundary with phenomenology

Phenomenology targets:

- lived meaning;
- experiential structure.

Rhythmanalysis may be phenomenologically influenced but its object is:

- rhythm organization.

### Hybrid

A study can combine:

- phenomenological attention to lived temporality;
- rhythmanalytical concepts.

### Critical rule

Do not collapse one into the other.

---

# 45. Boundary with ordinary thematic analysis

A thematic analysis can produce a theme called:

> “finding a rhythm.”

That is not rhythmanalysis.

### Critical rule

The analytic engine must be rhythm-centred.

Otherwise route:

parent study  
+ thematic analysis  
+ “rhythm” as substantive theme.

---

# 46. Methodology vs method vs orientation

Current literature legitimately uses all three labels.

## Foundational Lefebvre/Régulier

Best represented as:

- philosophical/conceptual project;
- investigative orientation.

## Later empirical scholarship

Uses rhythmanalysis as:

- framework;
- analytic lens;
- strategy of inquiry;
- research method;
- methodology.

### Project decision

Do not force one universal vocabulary.

Classify by enacted function.

# 47. Three project routes

## Route A — FULL_RHYTHMANALYTICAL_INQUIRY

Use when:

- rhythm is the primary scientific object;
- Lefebvre/Régulier or equivalent lineage is central;
- time-space-energy/embodied rhythm relation materially structures design;
- data generation/fieldwork is organized to detect rhythms;
- analysis is rhythm-centred.

## Route B — RHYTHMANALYSIS_INFORMED_HYBRID

Use when:

- another parent methodology remains explicit;
- rhythmanalysis materially alters analysis.

Examples:

- ethnography + rhythmanalysis;
- non-representational ethnography + rhythmanalysis;
- longitudinal ethnography + rhythmanalysis.

## Route C — RHYTHMANALYSIS_ANALYTIC_ORIENTATION

Use when:

- existing data/material are re-read through rhythm concepts;
- no full rhythm-specific design was enacted prospectively.

### Critical rule

Vocabulary-only use is not enough for any route.

---

# 48. Full rhythmanalysis does not require all canonical concepts

A study need not use every concept:

- linear;
- cyclical;
- polyrhythmia;
- eurhythmia;
- arrhythmia;
- isorhythmia;
- dressage.

### Project rule

Store exact conceptual subset.

Do not penalize a study solely because isorhythmia is absent.

---

# 49. Full rhythmanalysis does require rhythm to do real scientific work

A robust study should show that rhythm concepts alter:

- what is observed;
- how observations are organized;
- interpretation;
- conclusions.

### Red flag

If “rhythm” appears only:

- in title;
- introduction;
- metaphorically in Discussion;

→ do not classify full rhythmanalysis.

Empirical materials included:

- fieldnotes;
- semi-structured interviews;
- visual data;
- weekly training/meeting/match schedules.

### Analytic object

They examined how coaching staff:

- shaped;
- manipulated;
- negotiated;

weekly rhythms.

### Key concepts

- eurhythmia;
- polyrhythmia;
- arrhythmia.

### Scientific contribution

Coaching is re-read not as a set of isolated acts but as:

> manipulation/organization of rhythms.

# 51. Coaching exemplar — power

# 77. Project routing decision

Rhythmanalysis is **not** encoded as one rigid atomic procedural design.

It is encoded as a **distinct cross-cutting rhythm-centred methodology/analytic family** with three routes.

## 1. FULL_RHYTHMANALYTICAL_INQUIRY

Use when rhythm structures:

- research question;
- empirical strategy;
- embodied attention;
- analysis;
- claim.

## 2. RHYTHMANALYSIS_INFORMED_HYBRID

Use when another parent remains explicit, for example:

- ethnography;
- longitudinal ethnography;
- non-representational ethnography;
- mixed environmental study.

## 3. RHYTHMANALYSIS_ANALYTIC_ORIENTATION

Use when existing material is retrospectively/reanalytically read through rhythm concepts.

### Why this is preferable

It preserves both:

- foundational non-standardized orientation;
- current 2026 methodological maturation.
