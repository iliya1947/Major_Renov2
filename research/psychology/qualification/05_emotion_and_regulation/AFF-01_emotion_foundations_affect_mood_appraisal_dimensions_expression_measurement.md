# AFF-01 — Emotion foundations: affect, emotion, mood, appraisal, dimensions, expression, and measurement boundaries

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: generic public affective-science evidence and reusable inference rules only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

After LEA and MOT established learning, reward, value, effort, and goal pursuit, AFF must separate **affective experience** from motivation, physiological arousal, facial behavior, appraisal, language, and inferred emotion categories.

High-value reasoning failures to prevent:
- "high arousal = strong emotion";
- "negative affect = one specific emotion";
- "face = direct readout of inner state";
- "physiology pattern = uniquely identified emotion";
- "mood = long emotion" as an exact law;
- "emotion label = complete description of the episode";
- "appraisal = conscious reasoning only";
- "discrete categories and valence/arousal are mutually exclusive at every explanatory level";
- "one questionnaire or algorithm can objectively read emotion";
- "group-level emotion pattern = individual mechanism or diagnosis".

## Core rule

**Emotion is a multicomponent, context-sensitive phenomenon that can involve subjective feeling, appraisal/meaning, action tendencies, physiology, attention, expression, and behavior. These components can covary without being interchangeable or perfectly synchronized.**

Scientific conclusions must specify which component was measured and what theory links it to the inferred emotion.

## 1. Affect as an umbrella term

In affective science, "affect" is often used as a broad term covering feeling-related states and processes, including emotions, moods, and core affect.

Do not assume all authors use the term identically. Always inspect operational definitions.

A study measuring pleasantness/unpleasantness can validly study one affective dimension without measuring a discrete emotion episode.

## 2. Core affect

A common dimensional model organizes core affect primarily along:
- **valence**: pleasant ↔ unpleasant;
- **arousal/activation**: low ↔ high.

This two-dimensional representation is influential and repeatedly recovered in emotion-rating data. However, it does not claim that valence and arousal exhaust every property of emotion.

Current methodological work shows that how many dimensions appear depends strongly on stimuli, tasks, labels, and dimensionality-reduction procedures.

## 3. Emotion versus core affect

A state can have similar valence/arousal but different:
- eliciting situation;
- appraisal;
- action tendency;
- target/object;
- social meaning;
- certainty/control;
- learned history.

For example, two high-arousal unpleasant states could differ in threat probability, blame, controllability, or action preparation.

Thus **same valence/arousal ≠ same emotion**, and distinct emotion labels can still occupy nearby dimensional regions.

## 4. Emotion versus mood

A practical working distinction:
- **emotion**: usually more episodic, often linked to an identifiable object/event/meaning and action-relevant appraisal;
- **mood**: usually more diffuse, longer-lasting, less tightly tied to one object or event, and capable of biasing cognition broadly.

This is a graded conceptual distinction, not an exact temporal threshold. Duration alone cannot classify every affective state.

Do not infer that a diffuse state lacks causes; moods can emerge from accumulated events, physiology, sleep, social context, or other factors.

## 5. Feeling versus emotion

"Feeling" often refers to consciously experienced affective content. Emotion theories differ on whether conscious feeling is necessary for every emotional process.

Therefore:
- self-report primarily samples conscious/communicable experience;
- autonomic or behavioral responses may occur without identical conscious reports;
- a lack of verbal report does not automatically establish absence of all affective processing.

Never equate "emotion" with "what was verbally labeled" without stating that operationalization.

## 6. Arousal is not emotion intensity

NIMH RDoC defines arousal as a continuum of organismic sensitivity/activation and explicitly distinguishes arousal from motivation and valence, although they can covary.

A 2025 review, *Measuring Arousal: Promises and Pitfalls*, argues against treating arousal as a single organism-wide variable. Subjective, autonomic, motor, and neural arousal measures can diverge.

Hence:
- skin conductance ≠ subjective arousal by definition;
- motor activation ≠ emotional intensity;
- pupil dilation ≠ one emotion;
- low physiological activation does not imply absence of emotion.

## 7. Valence is not approach/avoidance in every theory

Pleasantness/unpleasantness often correlates with approach/avoidance tendencies, but approach can occur toward unpleasant goals (e.g., confrontation) and avoidance can protect valued states.

Keep:
- hedonic valence;
- motivational direction;
- action tendency;
- goal congruence

as potentially related but distinct variables.

This preserves the MOT/AFF boundary.

## 8. Dimensional and categorical models address different structures

Major traditions include:
- dimensional/core-affect models;
- basic/discrete emotion models;
- appraisal/component-process models;
- constructionist models;
- semantic-space/high-dimensional category models.

They disagree about what is fundamental, but some apparent conflicts arise because studies measure different phenomena:
- subjective experience;
- semantic concepts;
- facial or vocal expression;
- physiology;
- situation inference;
- action tendencies.

Do not ask "which theory won?" without specifying the target level and discriminating predictions.

## 9. Current methods review: structure depends on method

A 2025/2026 methods review notes:
- core affect is often modeled with two dimensions;
- emotion taxonomies range from a few categories to more than twenty;
- higher-dimensional approaches capture finer conceptual distinctions;
- stimuli, response tasks, analysis algorithms, and validity criteria can materially change the recovered emotion structure.

Canonical rule:

**latent emotion structure is partly an empirical property of the data and partly a property of how the data were elicited, measured, and summarized.**

## 10. 2024 cross-domain emotion-structure study

Han, Adolphs, and colleagues sampled:
- 1,000+ participants;
- about 150 narratives;
- about 1,000 videos;
- more than 10,000 longitudinally sampled everyday experiences.

Across domains, low-dimensional spaces containing valence and arousal recurred, with continuous gradients and no clear discrete clusters for the six classic basic-emotion categories in their analyses.

This is strong evidence for low-dimensional organization of **their measured subjective experience data**, not a universal falsification of all discrete emotion categories at every biological or communicative level.

## 11. Categories can remain useful even without hard clusters

Emotion categories can:
- summarize recurring appraisal/action patterns;
- support communication;
- predict behavior probabilistically;
- organize memories and social expectations.

A category need not have mathematically sharp boundaries to be scientifically useful.

Avoid false dichotomy:
**continuous variation OR meaningful categories**.

Both can describe different levels of the same dataset.

## 12. Appraisal: evaluation of meaning

Appraisal theories propose that emotional differentiation depends on evaluations such as:
- relevance to goals;
- pleasantness/goal congruence;
- certainty;
- agency/blame;
- control/coping potential;
- novelty/expectedness;
- normative significance.

Appraisal can be rapid and need not involve slow, verbally explicit deliberation.

"Appraisal" is a theoretical construct that must be operationalized, not a synonym for any thought occurring before an emotion.

## 13. 2024 appraisal meta-analysis

Yeo & Ong (2024) synthesized:
- 2,634 effect sizes;
- 309 studies across 251 reports;
- 47 appraisal dimensions;
- 63 emotions;
- 853 appraisal–emotion relationships.

They reported that 75% of previously hypothesized relationships were statistically significant, with average moderate-to-large correlation (mean r ≈ .33). Previously unpredicted relationships were also common.

Interpretation:
- appraisal profiles carry substantial information about emotion;
- appraisal-emotion mappings are not one-to-one;
- theory requires refinement rather than simplistic deterministic rules.

## 14. 2026 naturalistic multicomponent evidence

A 2026 Emotion paper using continuous annotations during films found that 13 discrete emotion labels could be predicted from combinations of emotion components, with valence and self-relevance contributing to a hierarchical organization.

This supports componential/appraisal differentiation in a naturalistic stimulus setting while remaining task-specific.

It does not prove a unique causal sequence for every real-life emotional episode.

## 15. Event, appraisal, and emotion label are separate

One event can produce different emotions because people differ in:
- goal relevance;
- prior knowledge;
- perceived agency;
- certainty;
- coping potential;
- norm interpretation;
- current state.

Conversely, different events can receive the same emotion label if their appraisal structures are sufficiently similar.

Do not infer emotion uniquely from the objective event.

## 16. Appraisal and motivation overlap without identity

Goal relevance and goal congruence are important appraisal variables. MOT already studies goal value and control.

Boundary:
- MOT asks why an action/outcome is worth pursuing and how effort is allocated;
- AFF asks how events are affectively interpreted and experienced, including their consequences for attention, physiology and action tendencies.

An appraisal can alter motivation, but the constructs should not be collapsed.

## 17. Interoception

Interoception refers to sensing and representing internal bodily signals.

A 2025 Annual Review emphasizes multiple dimensions:
- afferent bodily signals;
- sensing accuracy;
- neural processing;
- attention;
- interpretation/metacognition.

Interoception can contribute to emotional experience but does not provide a single direct "emotion meter."

Do not equate heartbeat detection accuracy, body awareness questionnaire scores, and emotional awareness.

## 18. Physiology does not provide a unique emotion fingerprint

A major meta-analysis of autonomic responses found substantial within-category variability and no simple one-to-one autonomic signature uniquely distinguishing common emotion categories.

A 2026 psychophysiology study similarly found that similar subjective affective experiences could produce variable skin-conductance and startle-response patterns within individuals.

Conclusion:
**physiology informs emotion science but should not be treated as a deterministic category decoder.**

## 19. Physiology can still be informative

Rejecting one-to-one fingerprints does not mean physiology is random or irrelevant.

Physiological measures can index:
- sympathetic/parasympathetic changes;
- orienting;
- action preparation;
- arousal dimensions;
- threat/reward contexts;
- individual response tendencies.

The correct inference is probabilistic and multivariate, with task and baseline controls.

## 20. Facial expression is not a direct readout

A facial movement can reflect:
- experienced emotion;
- social signaling;
- regulation/display rules;
- politeness;
- masking;
- effort;
- pain;
- speech;
- posed imitation.

Emotion can also occur without a stereotypical facial display.

Therefore:
**smile ≠ happiness by definition; scowl ≠ anger by definition.**

## 21. Face–context integration

A 2025 meta-analysis pooled:
- 37 articles;
- 3,198 participants;
- 1,020 effect sizes.

Faces and visual context both had large effects on emotion labeling; context was not simply subordinate to facial information when signal clarity and congruency were considered.

Emotion perception is therefore context-integrative rather than pure facial decoding.

## 22. 2024 face–context individual variability

A 2024 Nature Communications series across five studies (total N=752) found that integration of facial and situational information varied across emotion categories and individuals.

This further blocks the inference:
**observer labeled a face as X → actor objectively felt X**.

Emotion perception measures observer inference, not direct access to another person's internal state.

## 23. Cross-cultural expression evidence is mixed but structured

A 2024 large-scale study examined 45,231 facial reactions to 2,185 videos across Japan, North America, and Western Europe.

It found substantial cross-cultural similarities in mappings between facial movements and reported experience, alongside culture-specific intensity/display differences and large individual variability.

This evidence is inconsistent with both extreme claims:
- "all facial emotion meaning is culture-free and invariant";
- "there is no cross-cultural convergence whatsoever".

## 24. Culture, language, and concepts

Language influences which distinctions are easy to report and communicate. Cultural norms influence display, interpretation, and socially appropriate expression.

A 2025 perspective argues that emotion meaning in language reflects dynamic patterns of attention, construal, and appraisal.

Language therefore provides evidence about emotional meaning but is not a transparent readout of prelinguistic biology.

## 25. Experience, expression, perception are different targets

Distinguish:
1. **experience** — what the person feels/reports;
2. **expression** — facial, vocal, postural, behavioral output;
3. **perception/inference** — what an observer concludes another person feels;
4. **elicitation** — the situation/stimulus used to generate an episode.

A study of observers labeling expressions does not directly test the structure of first-person emotional experience.

## 26. Posed versus spontaneous expression

Posed expressions are produced under instruction and may exaggerate stereotypical category patterns.

Spontaneous expressions can be more variable and context-sensitive.

Databases of posed faces are useful experimental stimuli but cannot alone establish naturalistic production rules.

Always record whether stimuli are posed, induced, acted, spontaneous, or naturally sampled.

## 27. Emotional intensity is not one raw number

"Intensity" can refer to:
- stronger subjective feeling;
- larger autonomic change;
- stronger expression;
- higher action urgency;
- greater appraisal relevance;
- higher arousal.

These can dissociate.

A 2024 measurement paper shows that even subjective emotion-intensity scaling requires explicit psychometric assumptions; ordinary rating scales should not automatically be treated as interval quantities.

## 28. Time and emotional dynamics

Emotion is temporally dynamic:
- onset;
- rise;
- peak;
- recovery;
- recurrence;
- transition into another state.

Retrospective "How did you feel?" ratings can differ from continuous or momentary measurements because memory, peak/end effects, labeling and temporal averaging intervene.

Choose measurement timescale based on the hypothesis.

## 29. Mood and emotion interact

A background mood can alter:
- attention;
- interpretation/appraisal;
- retrieval accessibility;
- expectations;
- subsequent emotional responses.

A series of brief emotions can also influence longer mood.

Do not infer a unidirectional causal pathway from one cross-sectional correlation.

## 30. Mixed emotions

Positive and negative affective components can coexist or rapidly alternate.

A bipolar single scale from "very bad" to "very good" can obscure simultaneous ambivalence.

If mixed states are theoretically relevant, measure positive and negative components separately or use suitable multidimensional methods.

## 31. Emotion granularity and label differentiation

People differ in how specifically they distinguish emotional experiences in language and self-report.

High differentiation in labels does not prove greater physiological specificity or more "real" emotions. Low differentiation can reflect task/language properties, concept access, reporting strategy, or true experiential similarity.

Granularity claims require validated within-person designs.

## 32. Measurement channels

Common channels:
- self-report;
- behavioral choices/action tendencies;
- facial/body/vocal expression;
- autonomic physiology;
- endocrine measures;
- neural measurement;
- language;
- contextual appraisal ratings;
- ecological momentary assessment.

No channel is a universal gold standard for the entire construct "emotion."

Use convergence when possible while preserving disagreements as data.

## 33. Self-report strengths and limits

Self-report can directly access conscious experience unavailable to external observers.

Limits include:
- language/concept availability;
- demand characteristics;
- scale interpretation;
- retrospective reconstruction;
- social desirability;
- timing;
- memory;
- anchoring and response styles.

Self-report is neither intrinsically invalid nor infallible.

## 34. Behavioral measures

Avoidance, approach, reaction time, freezing, gaze, choice and persistence can reflect emotion but also:
- goals;
- learning history;
- motor constraints;
- executive control;
- task rules;
- social norms.

Behavior should not be assigned a single emotional meaning without experimental controls.

## 35. Neural measurement boundary

Brain activity can correlate with:
- sensory processing;
- salience;
- memory;
- valuation;
- interoception;
- action preparation;
- semantic labeling.

A region's activation does not uniquely identify a discrete emotion or subjective state.

Neural causal/mechanistic depth is deferred to NEU. AFF uses neural data only at the level justified by the measurement and design.

## 36. RDoC boundary

NIMH RDoC does not provide a single "emotion domain." Relevant constructs are distributed across:
- Negative Valence Systems (acute threat/fear, potential threat/anxiety, sustained threat, loss, frustrative nonreward);
- Positive Valence Systems;
- Arousal/Regulatory Systems.

RDoC explicitly states arousal is distinct from valence and motivation, even though they covary.

Therefore RDoC is useful for functional decomposition, not a complete taxonomy of everyday emotion labels.

## 37. Fear versus anxiety in RDoC is operational

RDoC distinguishes:
- acute threat/fear: defensive responses to perceived imminent danger;
- potential threat/anxiety: uncertain, distant, ambiguous, or low-probability harm.

These constructs are research functions, not instructions to diagnose a person from wording like "fear" or "anxiety."

Everyday language and clinical usage may differ.

## 38. Emotion induction validity

An "anger induction" or "fear stimulus" is not automatically successful.

Check:
- manipulation checks;
- baseline;
- target vs non-target emotions;
- demand characteristics;
- appraisal ratings;
- intensity;
- cultural relevance;
- individual variability.

Stimulus category is not ground truth of participant experience.

## 39. Machine learning classification boundary

A classifier can learn:
- stimulus artifacts;
- identity;
- recording context;
- culture;
- labeling conventions;
- posture or speech correlated with the target.

High cross-validation accuracy within one dataset does not prove the model decoded emotion itself.

Require participant-independent testing, external validation, out-of-distribution tests, transparent labels, and comparison against context-only baselines.

## 40. Emotion concept versus episode

Knowledge of what "anger" means is not the same as experiencing anger.

Semantic concept studies, recognition tasks and first-person episode studies can produce different structures because they target different objects.

Do not use a semantic taxonomy as direct proof of biological emotion architecture.

## 41. Emotion Inference Gate — 52 questions

### A. Target construct
1. Is the target affect, core affect, emotion, mood, feeling, or emotion concept?
2. Is it first-person experience or third-person inference?
3. Is the phenomenon episodic or diffuse?
4. Is there a specific object/event?
5. What timescale is measured?
6. Is the state induced, recalled, imagined, or naturally occurring?

### B. Dimensions and categories
7. Are valence and arousal measured separately?
8. Is arousal subjective, autonomic, motor, or neural?
9. Is an emotion category predefined or data-derived?
10. Could method/stimulus selection create the apparent category structure?
11. Is dimensionality supported by cross-validation?
12. Are categories treated as probabilistic rather than necessarily hard clusters?
13. Could mixed emotions be hidden by a bipolar scale?

### C. Appraisal and context
14. Which appraisal dimensions are hypothesized?
15. Is goal relevance measured?
16. Are certainty/expectedness separated?
17. Are agency/blame and control/coping separated?
18. Is normative significance relevant?
19. Is appraisal directly measured or inferred post hoc?
20. Could the same event yield different appraisals?
21. Is objective event content being mistaken for subjective meaning?
22. Could motivation/goal value explain part of the appraisal result?

### D. Subjective and bodily signals
23. Is subjective feeling directly measured?
24. Is self-report prospective, momentary, or retrospective?
25. Could language/scale use alter the report?
26. Is interoception measured separately from emotion labeling?
27. Are physiological measures interpreted as components rather than fingerprints?
28. Is baseline physiology controlled?
29. Are within-person and between-person effects separated?
30. Could arousal reflect non-emotional activation?

### E. Expression and perception
31. Is facial/vocal/body expression posed or spontaneous?
32. Is social display/regulation possible?
33. Is the observer inferring emotion rather than measuring experience?
34. Is situational context available to the observer?
35. Are face and context signal clarity matched?
36. Is culture/language represented adequately?
37. Could the same expression have non-emotional causes?
38. Is absence of stereotypical expression being treated as absence of emotion?

### F. Measurement and validation
39. Are multiple response channels available?
40. Is disagreement across channels preserved rather than averaged away?
41. Does the measure have reliability evidence?
42. Is construct validity demonstrated for the exact target?
43. Is induction success verified?
44. Are classifier labels independent of the inputs?
45. Is external/out-of-sample validation performed?
46. Are individual differences and context tested?
47. Is ecological validity relevant and examined?

### G. Inference limits
48. Is a theory-compatible result being called unique proof?
49. Is one component being substituted for the whole emotion?
50. Is population evidence being converted into an individual diagnosis?
51. What rival emotion model also explains the result?
52. What is the narrowest conclusion surviving all alternatives?

## 42. Failure modes

A. High arousal → strong emotion: **invalid without specifying arousal channel and target.**

B. Negative valence → specific emotion: **invalid category leap.**

C. Emotion label → full episode mechanism: **invalid construct compression.**

D. Face → internal emotion: **invalid direct-readout assumption.**

E. Skin conductance → fear: **invalid physiological specificity inference.**

F. Same emotion category → same physiology: **not supported as a universal rule.**

G. No stereotyped facial display → no emotion: **invalid.**

H. Context ignored because face is "primary": **not supported by current meta-analysis.**

I. Appraisal correlation → appraisal uniquely caused emotion: **causal overreach.**

J. Valence/arousal model → discrete labels meaningless: **false dichotomy.**

K. Category finding → categories have hard biological boundaries: **invalid.**

L. Mood = emotion lasting longer: **oversimplification.**

M. Self-report = biased therefore useless: **invalid methodological dismissal.**

N. Physiology = objective therefore ground truth: **invalid gold-standard assumption.**

O. Classifier accuracy → emotion decoded: **invalid without external construct validity.**

P. Group pattern → individual's stable emotional trait: **invalid transfer.**

## Canonical operating rule

**Define affective target and timescale → distinguish dimensions from categories → identify event/appraisal/context → measure subjective experience separately from arousal/physiology → distinguish expression from observer inference → test culture/context and measurement validity → compare competing emotion models → state only the component-level conclusion supported.**

Never:
**arousal = emotion; face = feeling; physiology = category; label = mechanism; dimensional evidence = categories impossible; category evidence = invariant fingerprint; classifier = mind reader.**
