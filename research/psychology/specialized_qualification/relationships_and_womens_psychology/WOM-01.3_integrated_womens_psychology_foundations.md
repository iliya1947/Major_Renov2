E2. Buck et al. (2026 issue), Gender Differences in Personality Traits and Average Personality States: Using Experience Sampling to Circumvent Bias in Self-Reports. https://doi.org/10.1177/19485506251347722
Access: publisher HTML abstract, theoretical sections, selected methods and results of both studies, general discussion, limitations and conclusion directly inspected. Not full end-to-end; supplements not independently audited. Two German-speaking samples, N=324 older adults and N=1,685 younger adults. Global self-report differences in conscientiousness, agreeableness and emotional stability were larger than corresponding aggregated momentary self-report differences. In study 2 emotional stability and openness state differences were significant; openness reversed direction relative to trait scores. Most equivalence tests were inconclusive (not proof of zero differences); threshold |d|=.10 chosen after first analyses. Trait–state correlations in study 2 .38–.52; state–state intercorrelations .45–.66. Most measurement models achieved partial scalar invariance with exceptions. The findings are consistent with recall/identity-related bias but also with differences in construct coverage, state discriminant validity, sampled situations and time scale. The authors explicitly discuss these alternative interpretations. Do not infer that all gender differences are stereotypes or that momentary reports are a ground truth. This is the main new empirical challenge linking WOM-01 and IND-01.

## 3. Integrated framework

The research question must determine whether a women-only trajectory, distribution within women, between-group contrast, within-person change, or contextual/biological mechanism is the relevant estimand. Do not assume male comparison is necessary to study women's psychology. Preserve individual experience and contextual variation, including findings from the Radcliffe and SWAN longitudinal research streams in WOM-01.2.

1. Define the construct and actual question. Distinguish sex-related characteristics, gender identity and salience, social roles, lived experience, context, and psychological trait/state/motive/behavior. Do not substitute a group label for a measured mechanism.
2. Check evidence provenance, sampling, age, culture, cohort, roles, instruments, reference period, and measurement equivalence. Trait self-description, state reports, informant reports and observed behavior are distinct evidence channels. Do not simply choose the channel giving the preferred answer.
3. Identify the reported statistic and comparison level. Mean difference, variance, facet difference, multivariate discrimination, and longitudinal change do not entail one another. Statistical significance is not practical predictability. Nonsignificance is not equivalence. A group-classification AUC cannot be reversed into a probability for an individual's particular characteristic without additional data.
4. Compare plausible mechanisms: context exposure, norms, role constraints, identity and appraisal, selection/socialization, actual biological variables, developmental trajectories and measurement. Preserve potentially interacting processes. Cross-sectional category comparisons and country-level moderators cannot partition causal contributions. Local experiments may identify effects of their tested manipulation, not mediation of all group differences.
5. Assess the individual using IND-01/02/03 and INT-02: repeated person-specific observations under comparable opportunities, self-reports with explicit provenance, competing hypotheses and temporal change. Population findings constrain hypotheses; they do not establish the motives or preferences of a specific woman. Use actual personal evidence only in authorized private context, not this repository.
6. State supported pattern, uncertainty, transfer boundaries, alternatives and revision condition. Respect explicit choices and boundaries regardless of theoretical inference. Do not invent a universal female trait, an innate causal share, or a numerical probability for an individual.

## 4. Integration checks

External empirical challenge (results known before review): E1 documents nonzero differences in several self-report domains; E2 documents method-sensitive differences with incomplete evidence for equivalence; E3 illustrates the separate cultural measurement problem; E4 suggests regional contextual variation. These findings can coexist because their targets, measures and levels differ. Do not infer from E2 that E1's reported scores are false, or from E1 that the difference must manifest identically in daily life. Prior WOM-01.2 E3 provides a potential social pathway but no mediated causal share, and the women-only studies E4/E5 establish heterogeneous trajectories within their cohorts.

Original synthetic check, unrelated to private materials: a fictional survey of adult women finds high self-rated organization but lower average organization in momentary reports during rotating training tasks. This establishes a discrepancy in measured scores, assuming comparable coding. Alternative accounts include different trait/state content, self-concept, changed opportunity, sampled task difficulty, and measurement error. A person-specific comparison across similar tasks and time windows may discriminate some alternatives; neither lying nor a sex-specific mechanism is identified. An unrelated group's average organization score does not settle the discrepancy. No real-person diagnosis or prediction.

Internal review only. No independently authored held-out task or external examiner available. No prospective individual forecast assessed. No numerical competence score claimed.
