# BIO-16 — Training adaptations

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-20**  
Область: 05 — Bioenergetics / Exercise Metabolism

## 0. Core decision

Training-induced metabolic adaptation is multilevel.

Relevant levels include:

- signaling / epigenetic state;
- gene expression;
- protein/transporter abundance;
- enzyme activity;
- organelle content;
- intrinsic organelle function;
- substrate storage;
- transport/handling;
- local recovery kinetics;
- whole-body physiology;
- task performance.

Project rule:

**change at one biological layer does not guarantee proportional change at another.**

---

## 1. Acute signaling vs chronic adaptation

Acute exercise can change:

- phosphorylation;
- transcription;
- mRNA;
- metabolites.

These do not by themselves establish retained structural/functional adaptation.

Project rule:

**acute signaling ≠ chronic adaptation.**

---

## 2. Mitochondrial content and function

Training can increase mitochondrial content and oxidative capacity.

But keep separate:

- mitochondrial content/density;
- OXPHOS protein abundance;
- citrate synthase;
- mass-specific respiration;
- respiration normalized to mitochondrial content;
- intrinsic function;
- ATP-production capacity.

Project rule:

**mitochondrial content ≠ intrinsic mitochondrial function.**

---

## 3. PGC-1α boundary

PGC-1α is strongly associated with mitochondrial-biogenesis signaling.

Project rule:

**PGC-1α response ≠ completed mitochondrial biogenesis.**

It is a signaling/biogenesis-associated marker rather than the adaptation itself.

---

## 4. Training mode and mitochondria

ET, HIT and SIT can all increase mitochondrial content.

Project rule:

**no single endurance-training intensity owns mitochondrial adaptation.**

Group-level similarity does not imply identical:

- individual response;
- fatigue cost;
- performance transfer;
- intrinsic function.

---

## 5. Concurrent training

Current pooled evidence indicates concurrent RT+ET can retain substantial VO2max and mitochondrial adaptation compared with ET alone.

However:

- reduced endurance dose can attenuate VO2max gains;
- subgroup context matters.

Project rule:

**no pooled difference ≠ universal equivalence under every concurrent-training design.**

Exact sequencing/dose remains PRO.

---

## 6. Enzyme adaptations

Training can alter metabolic-enzyme content/activity.

Examples include:

- glycogen phosphorylase;
- hexokinase;
- phosphofructokinase;
- LDH;
- citrate synthase;
- oxidative enzymes.

Project rule:

**enzyme adaptation is enzyme- and protocol-specific.**

---

## 7. Enzyme activity vs flux

Enzyme assays usually reflect biochemical capacity under assay conditions.

Project rule:

**enzyme activity ≠ in-vivo pathway flux ≠ guaranteed performance gain.**

---

## 8. Substrate transport

Training can remodel substrate-transport machinery.

Human evidence includes adaptation in:

- GLUT4;
- FAT/CD36;
- FABPpm;
- MCT1/MCT4.

Project rule:

**transporter abundance is one adaptation layer, not substrate flux itself.**

---

## 9. GLUT4

Separate:

- total GLUT4 protein;
- intracellular trafficking;
- membrane translocation;
- local glucose uptake;
- whole-body glucose disposal.

Project rule:

**GLUT4 abundance ≠ membrane translocation ≠ whole-body glucose handling.**

---

## 10. Glycogen storage

Training can increase skeletal-muscle glycogen storage/repletion capacity.

But actual glycogen availability depends on:

- prior exercise;
- starting glycogen;
- carbohydrate/energy availability;
- recovery time;
- recruited muscle/fibers.

Project rule:

**glycogen storage capacity ≠ current glycogen status.**

Exact carbohydrate prescription remains NUT.

---

## 11. Glycogen supercompensation boundary

Greater storage capacity does not guarantee supercompensation.

Project rule:

**training adaptation ≠ automatic glycogen supercompensation.**

Diet/recovery conditions still matter.

---

## 12. Buffering capacity

Muscle buffering capacity can increase after some high-intensity/interval protocols.

It does not increase universally after all hard or metabolically stressful training.

Project rule:

**buffering adaptation is protocol specific.**

---

## 13. Buffering ≠ lactate tolerance

Muscle buffering concerns acid-base handling.

It is not equivalent to:

- blood lactate;
- pain tolerance;
- “lactate tolerance”;
- complete fatigue resistance.

Project rule:

**buffering capacity ≠ whole performance/fatigue capacity.**

---

## 14. Lactate transport / MCT1 and MCT4

Human training evidence is more consistent for increased MCT1 protein than MCT4.

Project rule:

**MCT1 response ≠ MCT4 response.**

MCT1/MCT4 remain gradient-dependent transporters rather than irreversible one-way valves.

---

## 15. MCT measurement hierarchy

Keep separate:

- gene expression;
- protein abundance;
- localization;
- transport flux.

Project rule:

**MCT mRNA ≠ MCT protein ≠ lactate flux.**

---

## 16. Lactate handling

Training may alter:

- transport;
- uptake;
- oxidation;
- redistribution;
- disappearance.

Project rule:

**lower blood lactate ≠ lower lactate production and ≠ proof of one “clearance” mechanism.**

When mechanism matters, prefer “lactate handling/disappearance” rather than implying one clearance pathway.

---

## 17. PCr recovery kinetics

Postexercise PCr recovery measured by 31P-MRS is a local functional marker of oxidative energetics.

Training can improve PCr-recovery kinetics in some protocols.

But it depends on:

- end-exercise pH;
- muscle;
- protocol;
- acquisition/modeling;
- age/health;
- training duration.

Project rule:

**PCr-recovery kinetics are trainable but local and method conditioned.**

---

## 18. PCr recovery non-equivalences

Faster PCr recovery does not mean:

- larger resting PCr store;
- higher VO2max;
- complete metabolic recovery;
- complete neural/mechanical readiness.

Project rule:

**PCr-recovery rate ≠ global readiness.**

---

## 19. Aerobic recovery

Training can improve recovery through combinations of:

- local oxidative capacity;
- PCr resynthesis;
- perfusion/O2 utilization;
- lower disturbance at matched work;
- economy;
- other systemic/local adaptations.

Project rule:

**aerobic recovery is multicomponent, not one universal recovery-speed trait.**

---

## 20. Repeated-bout performance

Improved repeated-bout performance may reflect:

- higher first-bout capacity;
- faster PCr recovery;
- oxidative adaptation;
- altered metabolite/ion handling;
- neuromuscular adaptation;
- technical/pacing changes.

Project rule:

**better repeatability does not identify one metabolic adaptation.**

---

## 21. Specificity

Training adaptation is biased toward:

- recruited muscle;
- contraction pattern;
- intensity/duration;
- substrate demand;
- work:rest;
- mode mechanics.

Project rule:

**specificity is graded and biased, not exclusive.**

---

## 22. Convergent adaptation

Different training modes can produce overlapping adaptation.

Examples:

- ET/HIT/SIT → mitochondrial remodeling;
- SIT → glycolytic + oxidative adaptations;
- RT → glucose/metabolic changes in some contexts;
- concurrent training → mitochondrial adaptation.

Project rule:

**overlapping biological adaptation ≠ identical task transfer.**

---

## 23. Time efficiency boundary

A training mode can produce more adaptation per unit exercise time.

That does not prove universal superiority because:

- fatigue/recovery cost;
- adherence;
- injury/mechanical cost;
- total volume;
- task specificity

also matter.

Project rule:

**time efficiency ≠ universal training superiority.**

---

## 24. Detraining

Stopping or greatly reducing training reverses adaptations unevenly.

Potential changes include:

- VO2max;
- threshold-related measures;
- glycogen;
- GLUT4;
- oxidative enzymes;
- cardiovascular/peripheral variables.

Project rule:

**detraining is outcome- and time-specific, not synchronized reversal.**

---

## 25. Adaptation decay rates

Oxidative, glycolytic, transporter and structural traits can decay at different rates.

Project rule:

**there is no universal metabolic detraining half-time.**

Classic study-specific decay numbers are not converted into general rules.

---

## 26. Detraining ≠ instant baseline

Some adaptations can remain above pretraining baseline after training cessation.

Project rule:

**detraining is partial, trait-specific reversal rather than immediate reset.**

---

## 27. Maintenance

Partial training can preserve some adaptations better than complete cessation.

Project rule:

**maintenance may require less stimulus than development for some traits, but exact minimum maintenance dose is outcome/population specific.**

Exact prescription remains PRO.

---

## 28. Retraining

Lost adaptations can be regained after training resumes.

Project rule:

**retraining is effective, but faster/larger physiological response must be demonstrated for the specific trait.**

Prior training does not guarantee a physiological super-response.

---

## 29. Human HIIT epigenetic memory

Human HIIT training–detraining–retraining evidence shows retained:

- DNA-methylation patterns;
- gene-expression signatures.

The corrected current article reports similar physiological adaptation in initial training and retraining.

Project rule:

**molecular/epigenetic memory ≠ demonstrated accelerated physiological retraining.**

---

## 30. Memory hierarchy

Separate:

1. retained molecular signature;
2. retained physiological phenotype;
3. faster regain after re-exposure.

Project rule:

**molecular retention ≠ phenotype retention ≠ accelerated regain.**

---

## 31. Animal memory boundary

Animal studies can support mechanistic plausibility.

Project rule:

**animal metabolic-memory evidence ≠ human retraining prescription.**

---

## 32. Individual responder boundary

A person's response magnitude can vary across repeated nominally similar training blocks.

Project rule:

**one prior response ≠ stable responder phenotype.**

Individualization requires repeated context-specific observations.

---

## 33. Biomarker vs performance

A biomarker can improve while target performance does not.

Likewise, performance can improve without a change in a chosen biomarker.

Project rule:

**biochemical adaptation ≠ task-performance adaptation.**

---

## 34. Measurement context

Adaptation estimates depend on:

- muscle sampled;
- assay;
- normalization;
- test mode;
- time since last exercise;
- nutritional state;
- training status;
- age/sex/population.

Project rule:

**measurement context is part of adaptation interpretation.**

---

## 36. Trainer-level implications

1. Name the adaptation layer actually measured.
2. Do not use acute signaling as proof of chronic adaptation.
3. Keep mitochondrial content and intrinsic function separate.
4. Treat enzyme activity as capacity, not in-vivo flux.
5. Treat GLUT4/MCT protein as transport machinery, not actual whole-body flux.
6. Separate glycogen storage capacity from current glycogen state.
7. Do not assume every high-intensity program increases buffering.
8. Do not diagnose lactate production/clearance from one blood-lactate curve.
9. Use PCr recovery as local recovery information, not global readiness.
10. Expect adaptation overlap across modes while preserving task specificity.
11. Expect trait-specific detraining.
12. Do not promise accelerated physiological retraining from “muscle memory.”
13. Exact programming remains PRO; nutrient manipulation remains NUT; testing remains TST.
