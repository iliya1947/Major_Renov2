E1. Eyal, Steffel & Epley (2018), Perspective mistaking: Accurately understanding the mind of another requires getting perspective, not taking perspective. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000115 ; https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/perspective-mistaking-accurately-understanding-the-mind-of-anothe-2/ . Complete institutional author abstract directly inspected. Across 25 experiments, instructions to imagine another person's viewpoint did not consistently improve measured accuracy over controls. Reduced egocentric bias did not reliably produce more accurate alternative judgments. In the last experiment, acquiring information through conversation improved accuracy whereas imagined perspective taking did not. Some tasks addressed spousal preferences, others expressions and judgments of unfamiliar people. This challenges equating perspective-taking effort with accurate inference; it does not establish that perspective taking is always harmful, direct questions always reveal truth, or anyone is obliged to disclose. Samples, task-specific estimates, uncertainty and replication status not fully audited.

E2. Murphy & Lilienfeld (2019), Are self-report cognitive empathy ratings valid proxies for cognitive empathy ability? Negligible meta-analytic relations with behavioral task performance. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31120296/ . Indexed original author abstract inspected; direct PubMed CAPTCHA. Random-effects meta-analysis of 85 studies (14,327 participants) reported that cognitive empathy self-ratings accounted for about 1% of behavioral-task variance; the association did not significantly differ from the corresponding affective-empathy association. Distinguish self-concept and reported tendencies from measured performance. Heterogeneous tasks do not necessarily represent the same ability; null moderation is not equivalence. Exact confidence intervals, dependency and publication-bias analyses not checked. No individual diagnostic inference.

E3. Zikic et al. (2025), Examining empathic accuracy in a standardized task and in a naturalistic interaction: Associations, differences, and links with empathy. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2024.113016 ; https://scholars.mssm.edu/en/publications/examining-empathic-accuracy-in-a-standardized-task-and-in-a-natur/ . Complete institutional author abstract directly inspected. 137 friend dyads (mean age 19.61 years) rated a friend's affect following a supportive interaction and unfamiliar targets' affect on standardized videos. Accuracy corresponded weakly across tasks and videos; standardized-video scores were higher. Affective empathy self-report, but not cognitive empathy self-report, was associated with accuracy on both tasks. Differences in target, cue availability, setting, measurement and familiarity are bundled in the task comparison; no causal effect of familiarity established. Supports context and target dependence rather than a single generalized perceiver ability. Exact coefficients, reliability and covariate adjustments not checked.

E4. Sened, Lavidor, Lazarus, Bar-Kalifa, Rafaeli & Ickes (2017), Empathic accuracy and relationship satisfaction: A meta-analytic review. https://doi.org/10.1037/fam0000320 ; https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/empathic-accuracy-and-relationship-satisfaction-a-meta-analytic-r/ . Complete institutional author abstract directly inspected. 21 studies, 2,739 participants; inclusion restricted to dyadic interaction paradigm. Pooled correlation with satisfaction r=.134 (p<.05); authors report stronger association for negative (r=.171) than positive emotion accuracy (r=.068), and moderation by relationship length. No detected moderation by gender or actor/partner satisfaction outcome. Subgroup p-values alone are not a test of differences, so retain the authors' reported moderation without inferring additional contrasts. Small average association does not prove accuracy causes satisfaction, or that a particular correct inference reveals affection. Full confidence intervals, heterogeneity and dependency adjustments not checked.

E5. Couples' Perceptions of Each Other's Daily Affect: Empathic Accuracy, Assumed Similarity, and Indirect Accuracy (2018 online). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29473151/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6512343/ . Indexed original abstract and introductory/methodological excerpts inspected; both direct pages returned CAPTCHA. Daily diary and APIM analyses found both target-tracking and assumed-similarity components for positive affect; assumed similarity was reported for hard and soft negative affect. Indirect accuracy can arise when perceived similarity matches actual similarity. Do not infer the absence of all direct accuracy in negative-emotion contexts or automatic cognitive mechanisms from coefficient patterns. Exact sample, coefficient estimates and uncertainty not established in retrieved passages. Observational pathways do not identify causal information-processing sequences.

E6. Directional bias in interpersonal emotion perception (Nature Communications). https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-66879-2 . Selected publisher results passages directly inspected, especially Studies 1, 2, 6A and 6B. In Study 1, ratings of written experiences exceeded targets' own negative-emotion intensity reports (d=.52, 95% CI .36–.67); positive-emotion difference did not reach conventional significance. Study 6A reanalyzed 21-day diaries of 100 heterosexual new-parent couples: negative-emotion overestimation d=.22 [.09,.34]; positive-emotion difference d=.02 [-.11,.14]. In Study 6B (80 couples), negative-emotion overestimation was not significant. Authors report nonlinear associations of bias with selected relationship evaluations, including higher perceived responsiveness at modest negative-emotion overestimation than at substantial error in either direction. This concerns direction and magnitude of ratings relative to target self-report, not tracking accuracy or objective knowledge of intentions. Reanalyzed datasets, observational relationships, model dependence, uncertain transfer and possible overlap with other diary studies limit inference. No recommendation to deliberately misjudge or magnify emotion. Exact full methods, supplements, publication metadata and all results not audited.

E7. Vorauer, Hodges & Hall (2025), Thought-Feeling Accuracy in Person Perception and Metaperception: An Integrative Perspective. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-011624-024416 ; https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-011624-024416 . Complete publisher abstract directly inspected. Review connects judgments of others' thoughts and feelings in live interaction with accuracy of metaperceptions (judgments about others' evaluations of oneself); highlights potential systematic errors in spontaneous metaperceptions. Distinguish first-order inference from belief about what another person thinks of the perceiver. Abstract supports these conceptual distinctions, not a quantified universal effect. ## 3. Construct and measurement map
Empathy is an umbrella, not one scalar. Distinguish perspective taking (attempt to represent another's view), information acquisition, inferred content, target report/criterion, accuracy, affect sharing, contagion, compassion, personal distress, motivation and helpful action. Perceived empathy, confidence and accuracy may diverge. Accurate inference does not imply benevolence, and benevolence does not establish accuracy. Non-helping need not establish indifference if action is infeasible.

Define the exact accuracy target: emotional valence, intensity, specific emotion, thought content, preference or belief about oneself. Specify criterion, timing, scale, context and uncertainty. A voluntary, contemporaneous target report is useful criterion evidence, not direct access to all internal states or an infallible objective measure. An utterance establishes that it was expressed, not necessarily every underlying motive. Later behavior may establish fulfillment of a commitment without uniquely identifying why.

For repeated aligned ratings, distinguish signed mean difference (directional bias), absolute discrepancy (magnitude of error), and tracking of changes. Illustrative model: estimated_target_state(t) = intercept + b_target * reported_target_state(t) + b_self * perceiver_state(t) + residual. Coefficients depend on centering, context, scaling, measurement reliability and model specification. This is a conceptual representation, not a fitted model. The target association can coexist with a self-state association; actual similarity between states may contribute to total accuracy. Even perfect tracking can coexist with a constant level offset, and a zero average signed difference can hide large errors. Do not equate assumed similarity with clinical projection or infer a mechanism from one coefficient.

Separate perceiver, target, specific dyad and context contributions. Familiarity may supply knowledge and also expectations that distort interpretation. Target expressivity, channel cues, shared exposures and criterion limitations alter task difficulty. Agreement between observers can reflect shared stereotypes rather than independent truth. Do not generalize a single task, target or context to a global trait.

## 4. Practical inference and revision
1. Define the exact proposition and its target, context, time and evidence channel. Separate firsthand observations, voluntary reports, inferred emotion, inferred intention and inferred cause.
2. Identify what criterion would establish or challenge the claim. For an unexpressed thought or intention without a relevant independent criterion, accuracy remains unknown even if the explanation feels convincing.
3. Compare target cues, actual shared circumstances, self-state, prior expectations, social norms, ordinary alternatives and measurement error. A shared emotion may arise from a common event; assumed similarity can sometimes be accurate for that reason.
4. Examine level bias, tracking, magnitude of error and confidence separately when relevant. Do not use agreement, confidence or self-reported empathy as substitutes for accuracy. Avoid numerical estimates without appropriate data and calibration.
5. Prefer naturally available new information and voluntary clarification to elaborate imagined reconstruction. Respect a refusal to discuss a topic; absence of clarification is missing information, not proof of hidden intent. Do not covertly monitor, manufacture distress or use mental-state inferences as leverage.
6. Evaluate population, target, task, cue availability, relationship context, criterion validity, reporter differences, temporal alignment and uncertainty. Group-level association with satisfaction does not establish a causal intervention or individual predictive accuracy.
7. Revise atomic propositions rather than protecting a fused narrative. Record what would disconfirm an inference; if rivals predict the same observation, leave them unresolved. Explicit preferences, consent and safety remain distinct from hypothesized inner states.

## 5. Internal synthetic transfer review
Rubric: construct separation, competing explanation, discriminating evidence, inference boundary. Critical errors: unsupported motive/diagnosis, causal inflation, group-to-person determinism, ignoring contrary evidence, coercion or privacy leakage. Six neutral self-authored cases, not independent or held-out.

A. A reader imagines a colleague's frustration, then learns the colleague was worried about an unrelated deadline. Response: perspective-taking process and accuracy differ; shared prior context and self-state may explain the first estimate. Update on voluntary target information; no claim that the initial story revealed hidden motive.
B. An observer consistently estimates a target's mood two scale points higher while tracking every change. Response: tracking and directional level error differ; response anchors or criterion mismatch may contribute. Compare signed and absolute differences in aligned ratings. No perfect accuracy claim.
C. Two participants both report tension after a disrupted group event; one predicts the other's tension accurately. Response: shared exposure, actual similarity and direct cue use differ. Compare naturally divergent contexts and criterion reports. No proof of independent mind reading.
D. A questionnaire suggests strong cognitive empathy but a task score is ordinary. Response: self-report tendency and task performance differ; task validity and target familiarity are alternatives. Compare multiple valid measures without collapsing constructs. No moral or diagnostic label.
E. A study finds a small overall association between accuracy and satisfaction. Response: association, causation and individual outcome differ; prior quality, expressivity and selection may contribute. Compare temporally aligned prospective designs and uncertainty. No prediction of an individual relationship outcome.
F. A participant believes a collaborator regards them as incompetent, although no such evaluation was expressed. Response: metaperception and actual target evaluation differ; prior self-beliefs, ambiguity and real feedback are alternatives. Legitimate explicit feedback can clarify if volunteered. No certainty about private judgment.
