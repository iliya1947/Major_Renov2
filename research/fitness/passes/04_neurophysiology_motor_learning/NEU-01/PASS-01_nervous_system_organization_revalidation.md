## 3. Core architecture: CNS and PNS

### CNS

For current fitness purpose, the central nervous system includes:

- brain;
- spinal cord.

Relevant functions are distributed across:

- cerebral cortex;
- basal ganglia;
- cerebellum;
- brainstem;
- spinal circuits.

The spinal cord is not merely a cable between brain and muscle. It contains:

- interneuronal networks;
- sensory integration;
- reflex circuits;
- motoneuron pools;
- locomotor circuitry.

### PNS

The peripheral nervous system links CNS with:

- sensory receptors;
- skeletal muscle;
- viscera;
- glands;
- peripheral tissues.

It includes peripheral axons/nerves and ganglia outside CNS.

Project rule:

**movement emerges from CNS–PNS interaction; PNS is not passive wiring and spinal cord is not a simple conduit.**

---

## 4. Somatic nervous system

For training-relevant use, the somatic nervous system includes:

- somatic sensory afferents from skin, joints and muscle/tendon receptors;
- somatic motor output to skeletal muscle via lower motor neurons.

Important boundary:

Somatic does **not** mean “everything here is consciously voluntary.”

Somatic circuits include:

- automatic spinal reflexes;
- proprioceptive feedback;
- postural adjustments;
- patterned motor activity.

Voluntary intent can modulate these circuits, but much of movement execution depends on automatic sensorimotor processing.

Project rule:

**somatic ≠ conscious-only.**

---

## 5. Autonomic nervous system

The conventional training-level subdivision remains useful:

- sympathetic;
- parasympathetic.

These systems regulate cardiovascular, respiratory-supportive, thermoregulatory, gastrointestinal and metabolic context around exercise.

However current neurophysiology shows the ANS is more heterogeneous than a binary “fight-or-flight vs rest-and-digest” switch.

The 2025 Nat Rev Neurosci review emphasizes:

- molecularly diverse autonomic neuron populations;
- organ- and circuit-specific functions;
- dynamic brain–body regulation.

The 2025 J Physiol conceptual review argues that even the label “autonomic” and simple sympathetic/parasympathetic partition can obscure the actual organization.

Project rule:

**sympathetic and parasympathetic are useful coarse categories, not two globally opposed master switches.**

For exercise:
- sympathetic activity commonly increases with intensity;
- parasympathetic cardiac influence is withdrawn early and returns during recovery;
- organ-specific patterns can differ.

Detailed exercise autonomic physiology remains PHY/REC/TST-owned where appropriate.

## 6. Afferent vs efferent pathways

Definitions:

- **afferent** = information traveling toward CNS;
- **efferent** = commands/signals traveling from CNS toward peripheral effectors.

Relevant afferent sources include:

- muscle spindles;
- Golgi tendon organs;
- cutaneous receptors;
- joint-related receptors;
- group III/IV muscle afferents;
- vestibular/visual systems upstream of motor control.

Relevant efferent pathways include:

- somatic motor axons to skeletal muscle;
- autonomic efferent pathways to organs/tissues.

Important anatomical boundary:

A named peripheral nerve commonly contains **mixed sensory and motor fibers**.

Therefore:
- afferent and efferent describe direction/function of axons;
- they do not imply that all peripheral nerves are exclusively sensory or exclusively motor.

The 2025 Annual Review confirms that sensory feedback is woven into descending and spinal motor circuits rather than added after movement as a separate correction channel.

Project rule:

**motor control is closed-loop; afferent feedback continuously modifies efferent output.**

## 7. Motor cortex — applied level

Motor cortex includes multiple interacting frontal motor regions.

For current scope:
- primary motor cortex (M1) is important for voluntary movement execution and skilled force control;
- premotor/SMA regions contribute to planning, preparation and sequencing;
- cortical output is distributed across cell populations and interacts with sensory input, basal ganglia, cerebellum, thalamus, brainstem and spinal cord.

M1 should not be modeled as a single “command neuron” that specifies every muscle contraction.

Current strength-neurophysiology review supports roles for:

- cortical excitability/inhibition;
- descending drive;
- sensory feedback;
- corticospinal pathways;
- plasticity with resistance training.

Animal cell-type evidence further shows that different M1 projection neuron classes can contribute differently to execution.

Project rule:

**M1 is a major motor hub, not the sole controller of movement.**

Detailed cortical plasticity belongs NEU-06/NEU-19.

---

## 8. Cerebellum — coordination, prediction and error-based learning

The cerebellum is strongly interconnected with:

- cerebral cortex;
- brainstem;
- spinal/sensory pathways.

Current 2025–2026 reviews support roles in:

- predictive motor control;
- timing;
- coordination;
- sensorimotor calibration;
- error-based adaptation/learning.

The 2025 Nat Rev Neurosci review specifically cautions against reducing cerebellar function to one simple internal-model account; multiple computational formulations remain plausible.

Thus “error correction” is useful at an applied level but incomplete.

The cerebellum does not directly innervate skeletal muscle.

Its influence is expressed through:
- cerebello-thalamo-cortical pathways;
- brainstem pathways;
- interactions with wider sensorimotor networks.

Project rule:

**cerebellum contributes prediction, coordination and adaptation; it is not a direct muscle command center.**

Detailed learning mechanisms belong NEU-11…NEU-19.

---

## 9. Basal ganglia — selection, reinforcement, vigor and automaticity

Basal ganglia participate in cortico-basal-ganglia-thalamo-cortical and brainstem-related loops.

Training-relevant functions include:

- reinforcement-based learning;
- movement initiation/modulation;
- vigor;
- habit/sequence learning;
- context-dependent action policies.

The traditional phrase “action selection” is useful but should not be treated as settled exclusive mechanism.

Current evidence is more nuanced:

- 2024 review supports basal-ganglia contributions to selection/recruitment of actions;
- 2025 striatal perturbation work argues some dynamics fit context-specific reinforcement/modulation better than simple action-selection gating;
- 2025 work on habits supports sensorimotor striatal circuits in learned automatic sequences;
- recurrent cortex–basal-ganglia loops support reward-driven movement adaptation.

Project rule:

**basal ganglia modulate learned action policies, vigor and reinforcement; “action selection switch” is an oversimplification.**

Detailed reward learning/habit formation is downstream NEU/PSY/COA territory.

---

## 10. Corticospinal pathways — basic level

The corticospinal tract (CST) is a major descending pathway for voluntary motor control, especially skilled movements.

Important features:

- arises from multiple cortical areas, not only M1;
- most fibers decussate in the pyramidal system;
- acts through spinal interneurons and motoneurons;
- humans/primates possess specialized direct corticomotoneuronal connections important for fractionated distal movements, especially hand/finger control.

But CST is not the only descending motor route.

Other descending systems from brainstem contribute to:

- posture;
- locomotion;
- proximal/axial control;
- balance;
- orienting and automatic movement components.

The 2026 J Physiol brainstem review reinforces that human movement remains distributed across corticospinal and brainstem systems.

Project rule:

**CST is a major voluntary-control pathway, not the whole motor system.**

---

## 11. Alpha motor neurons

Alpha motor neurons innervate extrafusal skeletal-muscle fibers.

A motor unit is:

`one alpha motor neuron + all muscle fibers it innervates`.

Alpha motor neurons receive convergent input from:

- descending pathways;
- spinal interneurons;
- sensory afferents;
- intrinsic spinal network activity.

Their output determines whether and how recruited motor units generate muscle activation.

Important nuance:

Motoneurons are not passive linear relays.

Human evidence shows intrinsic motoneuron properties can materially shape torque output and control.

Project rule:

**alpha motor neuron is the final common neural output to extrafusal skeletal muscle, but its behavior reflects integrated synaptic + intrinsic properties.**

Detailed motor-unit recruitment/rate coding begins in NEU-02.

---

## 12. Interneurons

Interneurons form local and propriospinal networks within CNS, especially spinal cord.

They integrate:

- descending commands;
- sensory afferents;
- signals from other interneurons.

Functions include:

- excitation/inhibition of motoneuron pools;
- reciprocal coordination;
- reflex modulation;
- locomotor pattern generation;
- gain control;
- interlimb/postural coordination.

Current reviews emphasize substantial interneuron diversity and network organization rather than one generic interneuron class.

Project rule:

**spinal interneurons transform and integrate motor commands; they are not passive relay wires.**

Detailed reflex physiology belongs NEU-04.

---

## 13. Neuromuscular junction — boundary with PHY-02

The NMJ is the cholinergic synapse between:

- alpha-motor-neuron terminal;
- skeletal-muscle endplate.

Basic sequence:

`motor-neuron action potential`
`→ presynaptic Ca2+ entry`
`→ acetylcholine release`
`→ nicotinic receptor activation`
`→ endplate depolarization`
`→ muscle-fiber action potential`.

This closes the neural-output chain and hands off to muscle excitation-contraction physiology.

Current reviews show NMJ is plastic and activity responsive, involving:

- presynaptic terminal;
- postsynaptic endplate;
- perisynaptic Schwann cells;
- extracellular matrix.

However NEU-01 does not repeat:
- detailed neurotransmission;
- muscle action-potential propagation;
- excitation-contraction coupling.

Those are already PHY-02-owned.

Project rule:

**NMJ is the interface between neural command and muscle excitation, not an independent “strength generator.”**

## 14. Integrated signal-flow model

A bounded training-relevant architecture:

`goal / context / learned policy`
`↔ cortical + basal-ganglia + cerebellar networks`
`→ descending cortical/brainstem pathways`
`↔ spinal interneuronal circuits + sensory afferents`
`→ alpha motor neurons`
`→ NMJ`
`→ skeletal-muscle activation`.

At the same time:

`muscle / tendon / skin / joint / vestibular / visual feedback`
`→ afferent pathways`
`→ spinal + brainstem + cortical/cerebellar processing`
`→ updated motor output`.

Autonomic pathways simultaneously adjust:
- cardiovascular state;
- thermoregulation;
- metabolic/visceral support.

Project rule:

**movement is distributed closed-loop control, not a one-way brain-to-muscle command chain.**

## 16. Established project-level model

### Central organization

`cortex + basal ganglia + cerebellum + brainstem + spinal cord`
form interacting control loops.

### Peripheral interface

`afferent sensory input ↔ CNS processing → somatic/autonomic efferent output`.

### Skeletal-muscle output

`descending + afferent + interneuronal inputs`
`→ alpha motor neuron`
`→ NMJ`
`→ muscle fiber activation`.

### Learning/control boundary

No single structure owns:
- movement generation;
- error correction;
- action selection;
- force;
- automaticity.

These functions emerge from distributed circuits with task-dependent weighting.
