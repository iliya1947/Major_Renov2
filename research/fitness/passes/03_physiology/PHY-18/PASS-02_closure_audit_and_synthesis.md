## 3. Ordinary detraining vs disuse audit

PASS.

Ordinary training cessation in an otherwise active person is not equivalent to:

- limb immobilization;
- bed rest;
- denervation;
- severe inactivity.

Disuse studies can demonstrate biological direction and mechanisms, but their quantitative loss rates cannot be transferred directly to ordinary breaks.

Canonical rule:

**detraining and severe disuse are related but not interchangeable exposures.**

No blocker.

## 4. Strength-loss audit

PASS.

Strength can regress during detraining through combinations of:

- neural drive/coordination;
- task familiarity;
- rate of force development;
- antagonist coactivation;
- muscle size;
- architecture;
- tendon/mechanical properties.

The 14-day powerlifter study showed measurable neuromuscular/performance changes without statistically significant muscle thickness or CSA loss.

The periodic-RT trial showed strength declined over 10 weeks detraining and was rapidly regained.

Canonical rule:

**strength loss does not quantify muscle loss.**

No blocker.

## 5. Muscle-size audit

PASS.

Muscle size loss depends on:

- detraining duration;
- muscle/population;
- prior training;
- ordinary activity;
- measurement method.

Short breaks can show little measurable atrophy, while longer cessation can reduce CSA/lean-mass outcomes.

The broad 2026 RCT meta-analysis confirms that residual post-training effects vary by outcome and detraining duration rather than following one universal trajectory.

Canonical rule:

**atrophy during detraining is graded and context dependent.**

No blocker.

## 6. Neural-detraining audit

PASS.

Human TMS/EMG evidence supports partial regression/change in neural responses after cessation.

Important boundary:

- TMS excitability/inhibition;
- EMG amplitude/coactivation;
- strength;
- rate of force development

are related but non-identical constructs.

No single neural signal is retained as a universal detraining biomarker.

Canonical rule:

**early performance decline can include neural/skill components without proving structural loss.**

No blocker.

## 7. Architecture audit

PASS.

Human hamstring studies show different detraining trajectories for:

- fascicle length;
- pennation angle;
- serial sarcomere number;
- muscle volume/ACSA.

Examples:

- after 3 weeks detraining following Nordic training, fascicle length and serial sarcomere number decreased while muscle volume and eccentric strength remained above pre-training values;
- after 2 weeks detraining in another eccentric-training study, fascicle length and pennation angle returned toward baseline while ACSA remained elevated.

Canonical rule:

**muscle architecture has multiple clocks; CSA alone does not define retention.**

No blocker.

## 8. Cardiorespiratory-detraining audit

PASS.

Meta-analysis in trained athletes shows VO2max decreases after both short- and long-term training cessation, with larger pooled effect after longer cessation.

The broader 2026 RCT detraining meta-analysis shows an important second boundary:

- loss from the trained peak does not necessarily mean loss of all training benefit;
- some VO2/body-composition/metabolic outcomes can remain improved relative to pre-training baseline.

Canonical rule:

**detraining can lower VO2max while residual cardiorespiratory benefit persists.**

No blocker.

## 9. Residual-adaptation audit

PASS.

Retention is graded and outcome specific.

A 2024 reduced-frequency trial in young women found:

- weekly concurrent training largely maintained leg-press 1RM, quadriceps CSA and maximal aerobic power for 12 weeks;
- one session every 14 days preserved approximately 90–95% of those measured adaptations in that protocol.

This is useful proof that maintenance can require less dose than development.

It is not a universal maintenance-frequency prescription.

Canonical rule:

**maintenance dose can be substantially lower than development dose, but exact dose is population/protocol specific.**

No blocker.

## 10. Retraining audit

PASS.

The periodic-RT trial supports rapid reacquisition after a 10-week break:

- strength and CSA fell during detraining;
- the first 5 weeks of retraining produced larger gains in several outcomes than a comparable later 5-week continuous-training interval;
- final overall adaptations were similar between periodic and continuous RT.

This supports functional regain.

It does not establish:

- a universal retraining multiplier;
- indefinite memory;
- equal regain after injury/illness or very long cessation.

Canonical rule:

**recently lost adaptations can return rapidly, but retraining speed is context dependent.**

No blocker.

## 11. Myonuclear-memory audit

PASS WITH EVIDENCE CONFLICT RETAINED.

The human evidence is not reducible to “myonuclei are permanent.”

### Supporting context-specific retention

The 2024 within-person human strength-training study found:

- training increased myonuclear number;
- fibre CSA decreased during 16 weeks detraining;
- training-acquired myonuclei were maintained;
- previously trained type-II fibres retained more myonuclei.

### Limiting evidence

The 2022 systematic review/meta-analysis across human and animal atrophy evidence did **not** support universal human myonuclear permanence and reported lower human myonuclear content with atrophy.

The 2025 review likewise states that indefinite persistence/removal mechanisms remain unresolved.

### Causal benefit boundary

In the 2024 human study:

- previously trained muscle had larger type-II fCSA after retraining;
- but the delta increase was not significantly greater;
- authors concluded physiological benefit remained uncertain.

Canonical rule:

**human myonuclear retention can occur in specific training/detraining contexts; universal or indefinite permanence and causal faster hypertrophy are not established.**

No blocker.

## 12. Epigenetic-memory audit

PASS.

The 2025 human HIIT study found:

- retained hypomethylation after training;
- retained methylation/gene-expression memory through 3 months cessation and retraining;
- several genes with retained memory profiles.

However physiological adaptation during training and retraining was similar, and no clear superior physiological memory effect was demonstrated.

Canonical rule:

**epigenetic memory is molecular history, not proof of superior functional retraining.**

No blocker.

## 13. Proteomic-memory audit

PASS.

The 2025 repeated-RT study found:

- many training-responsive proteins returned toward baseline after 10 weeks cessation;
- some remained elevated;
- retained proteins included contraction/cytoskeletal/calcium-related factors;
- many adaptations reappeared during retraining.

This establishes retained proteomic signatures.

It does not identify one master causal protein or prove that retained proteins drive faster regrowth.

Canonical rule:

**proteomic memory is real as a retained signature; causal practical benefit remains unresolved.**

No blocker.

## 14. Returning-trainee audit

PASS.

A previously trained person can retain some combination of:

- strength;
- muscle size/architecture;
- motor skill;
- neural coordination;
- cardiorespiratory/metabolic adaptation;
- myonuclear state;
- epigenetic/transcriptional state;
- proteomic state.

Therefore a returner is not biologically identical to an absolute novice.

But current state still depends on:

- break duration;
- activity during the break;
- injury/illness;
- age;
- nutrition;
- previous training duration;
- current tolerance.

Canonical rule:

**prior training history matters, but does not justify skipping progressive re-exposure.**

No blocker.

Result: **PASS.**

Canonical synthesis:

`trained state`
`→ reduced/absent stimulus`
`→ outcome-specific decay on different clocks`.

Possible retained layers:

`strength/skill + muscle structure + cardiorespiratory/metabolic state + cellular/molecular history`.

Then:

`residual state + renewed training`
`→ reacquisition`
which can be faster for some recent losses.

But:

`functional regain`
does not identify
`one causal muscle-memory mechanism`.
