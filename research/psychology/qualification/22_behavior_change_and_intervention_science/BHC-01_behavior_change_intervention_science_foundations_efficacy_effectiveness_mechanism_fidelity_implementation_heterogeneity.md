# BHC-01 — Behavior change and intervention-science foundations: efficacy/effectiveness, package/mechanism, mediation/moderation, fidelity/adherence, maintenance, implementation/context, heterogeneity, and causal limits

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Establish the general intervention-inference architecture required before studying therapy families or specific behavior-change packages.

The core problem is not merely:

> Does an intervention appear to work?

Reliable reasoning requires separating:

1. the intervention package;
2. its intended components/techniques;
3. actual delivery;
4. participant receipt and enactment;
5. clinical/behavioral outcome;
6. hypothesized mediator;
7. causal mechanism;
8. effect moderator;
9. reach and selection;
10. implementation/context;
11. maintenance/sustainment;
12. harms and unintended effects.

Canonical rule:

`intervention specification -> estimand/comparator -> delivery/fidelity -> receipt/enactment -> outcome -> heterogeneity -> mechanism evidence -> maintenance -> implementation/context -> narrow conclusion`.

Never:

`treatment works -> theory is true`

or:

`mediator changed -> mechanism proven`.

# I. Question Card

## 1. Concrete dependency

Phase B established learning, motivation, emotion, stress, social, developmental and measurement mechanisms.

What remains unresolved is how to reason from an intervention study to claims about:

- efficacy;
- real-world effectiveness;
- active ingredients;
- mechanism;
- who benefits;
- adherence/engagement;
- durability;
- implementation.

Without this layer, predictable errors include:

- package efficacy treated as proof of a named mechanism;
- post-treatment mediator treated as causal pathway;
- treatment completion treated as adherence;
- intervention attendance treated as skill enactment;
- efficacy trial results generalized to routine practice;
- dropout interpreted as treatment inefficacy;
- fidelity deviations interpreted as failure without considering adaptive tailoring;

- short-term outcome treated as durable behavior change;
- implementation failure confused with intervention failure;
- theory-labelled components treated as active ingredients;
- more sessions assumed to be better dose;

# II. Intervention development is staged but not linear

## 12. NIH Stage Model

The current NIH Stage Model distinguishes:

- Stage 0 — relevant basic science;
- Stage I — intervention generation/refinement/adaptation and pilot testing;
- Stage II — efficacy in research settings;
- Stage III — real-world efficacy with community providers while retaining experimental control;
- Stage IV — effectiveness in community settings;
- Stage V — implementation/dissemination.

## 13. The stages are not a mandatory waterfall

NIH explicitly describes the model as:

- iterative;
- recursive;
- multidirectional;
- non-prescriptive.

A later stage can expose a problem requiring return to refinement.

Therefore:

`Stage II success -> automatic Stage IV/V success`

is invalid.

## 14. Mechanism research belongs across stages

NIH encourages mechanism-of-change research throughout intervention development.

Mechanism is not something that appears only after efficacy is established.

## 15. Implementability should be considered early

NIH recommends attention to implementability during intervention development rather than treating implementation as a late afterthought.

This is consistent with the MRC complex-intervention framework.

# III. Efficacy versus effectiveness

## 16. Efficacy

Efficacy asks whether an intervention can produce benefit under relatively controlled/optimized conditions.

Typical features:

- selected participants;
- trained/supervised providers;
- standardized delivery;
- high fidelity monitoring;
- intensive follow-up;
- controlled comparator.

## 17. Effectiveness

Effectiveness asks how the intervention performs under more ordinary real-world conditions.

Typical concerns:

- representative participants;
- routine providers;
- variable settings;
- ordinary adherence;
- service constraints;
- competing demands.

## 18. Efficacy/effectiveness is better treated as a continuum than a binary

The NIH Stage Model explicitly describes variation from internal-validity emphasis toward generalizability.

Trials can contain both explanatory and pragmatic elements.

## 19. CONSORT 2025 warns about adherence/fidelity optimization

CONSORT 2025 notes that intensive efforts to maximize fidelity/adherence in explanatory trials can produce benefit estimates that are not reproduced under real-world conditions.

Therefore:

`highly controlled efficacy effect -> routine-practice effect of same magnitude`

is unsafe.

## 20. Pragmatic does not mean methodologically weak

A pragmatic trial still requires:

- valid randomization/analysis where relevant;
- outcome measurement;
- clear intervention description;
- bias control;
- explicit estimand.

It simply targets a different practical question.

# IV. Intervention package versus active ingredient

## 21. An intervention label is not an intervention specification

Labels such as:

- CBT;
- motivational intervention;
- mindfulness;
- behavioral activation;
- coaching;
- psychoeducation

can hide substantial differences in:

- procedures;
- dose;
- delivery mode;
- provider;
- tailoring;
- materials;
- setting.

## 22. TIDieR makes intervention specification explicit

TIDieR emphasizes describing:

- name;
- rationale;
- materials;
- procedures;
- provider;
- mode;
- setting;
- timing/dose;
- tailoring;
- modifications;
- planned/actual fidelity.

Therefore:

`same therapy label -> same intervention`

is invalid.

## 23. Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy is a content language

BCTTv1 identified 93 techniques grouped into 16 clusters.

Its purpose is to specify intervention content more precisely.

## 24. The Behavior Change Technique Ontology extends specification

The later BCT Ontology expanded/reorganized behavior-change content terms.

This reinforces that intervention content can be formally specified.

## 25. Technique classification is not mechanism identification

A BCT label says what technique is delivered.

It does not establish:

- that the technique was received;
- that it changed its hypothesized target;
- that the target caused outcome change.

Therefore:

`BCT present -> active ingredient`

is invalid.

# V. Package efficacy does not identify active components

## 26. A multi-component package can work for many reasons

Possible explanations:

- one component drives most benefit;
- several components add;
- components interact;
- common/contextual factors matter;
- expectancy/contact matters;
- package improves adherence rather than target mechanism;
- different participants benefit from different components.

## 27. Dismantling/component designs are needed for component claims

Component-specific effects are better addressed by:

- factorial trials;
- dismantling studies;
- sequential multiple assignment designs where appropriate;
- component network meta-analysis;
- mechanistic perturbation.

A standard package-versus-control RCT does not identify active ingredients.

## 28. Current component evidence shows why decomposition matters

A 2026 Bayesian component network meta-analysis of task-shared psychosocial interventions included:

- 34 RCTs;
- 30 trials contributing IPD;
- 10,612 participants.

It estimated different incremental effects for components, with:

- strengthening social support;
- behavioral activation;
- problem management

among the more beneficial components in that specific intervention context.

## 29. Component effects are context-bound

That 2026 result concerns:

- task-shared psychosocial interventions;
- adults with common mental disorders;
- the included component taxonomy;
- available trials.

It does **not** prove universal superiority of those components.

## 30. Component effects can interact

The same analysis found evidence that component effects varied with baseline severity and other characteristics.

Thus:

`component average effect -> same effect for every person`

is invalid.

# VI. Theory, target, mediator, and mechanism

## 31. Intervention theory is a hypothesis

A program theory explains:

- what is expected to change;
- how;
- under what conditions;
- why this should alter outcomes.

MRC recommends testing/refining programme theory across phases.

## 32. Theory-consistent outcome does not validate theory

If intervention I is based on theory T and outcome improves:

`I works -> T is true`

is invalid.

Alternative pathways may generate the same outcome.

## 33. Target

A target is a modifiable construct/process an intervention intends to change.

Examples:

- threat appraisal;
- avoidance;
- self-efficacy;
- reinforcement contingency;
- social support;
- action planning.

## 34. Target engagement

NIH SOBC's experimental-medicine approach requires showing that the intervention actually changes the hypothesized target.

Without target engagement:

- the mechanism hypothesis is weakened;
- treatment outcome alone cannot rescue it.

## 35. Target engagement is still not full mechanism proof

Need evidence that:

1. intervention changes target;
2. target change temporally precedes relevant outcome change;
3. target change causally contributes to outcome;
4. alternatives/confounding are addressed.

## 36. Mediator

A mediator is a variable on a hypothesized causal pathway.

Statistically:

`intervention -> mediator -> outcome`.

But an indirect effect estimate is not automatically a causal mechanism.

## 37. Mechanism

Mechanism is the process through which intervention causes outcome change.

It is stronger than:

- correlate;
- predictor;
- mediator variable;
- theory-consistent change.

## 38. Randomization of intervention does not randomize mediator

Even in an RCT:

- treatment assignment may be randomized;
- mediator values usually are not.

Mediator–outcome confounding can therefore remain.

## 39. Temporal ordering matters

If mediator and outcome are measured at the same time, direction is ambiguous.

If outcome improves before the mediator, mediator-as-cause becomes less plausible.

## 40. Reciprocal effects are possible

A 2026 routine psychotherapy RCT analysis found:

- alliance/self-efficacy/agency related to outcomes;
- some process variables predicted later outcomes;
- outcomes also predicted later process changes.

This is a concrete reminder:

`process correlated with improvement -> one-way mechanism`

is unsafe.

## 41. Construct overlap can create apparent mediation

A 2026 psychotherapy reanalysis showed an apparent mediation effect disappeared when overlapping distress/depression content was handled differently.

Therefore measure independence matters.

# VII. Mechanism evidence ladder

## 42. Level 0 — theory

A hypothesized process exists.

No empirical target evidence.

## 43. Level 1 — intervention changes outcome

Package effect established.

Mechanism unknown.

## 44. Level 2 — intervention changes target

Target engagement established.

Outcome mechanism still not established.

## 45. Level 3 — target change predicts outcome change

Temporal/process association exists.

Confounding/reverse causality may remain.

## 46. Level 4 — causal target perturbation

Manipulating target produces expected downstream behavior/outcome.

Strong causal evidence.

## 47. Level 5 — causal mediation / convergent mechanism

Evidence converges across:

- perturbation;
- temporal sequence;
- mediation;
- alternative mechanisms;
- replication;
- contexts.

Strong mechanism language becomes defensible.

---

# VIII. Moderator and treatment-effect heterogeneity

## 48. Moderator/effect modifier

A moderator concerns whether treatment effect differs across values of a baseline/context characteristic.

It does not explain the causal pathway.

Therefore:

`moderator -> mediator`

is invalid.

## 49. Subgroup response is not automatically personalized treatment

A credible effect modifier requires:

- prespecification or strong rationale;
- adequate power;
- interaction testing;
- replication;
- transport to target setting.

## 50. Within-arm significance is not an interaction

Incorrect:

`treatment significant in subgroup A but not B -> subgroup effect differs`.

Need direct interaction/effect-modification test.

## 51. Meta-regression is vulnerable to ecological confounding

Study-level associations do not automatically identify person-level moderators.

IPD improves this problem but does not eliminate all bias.

## 52. Current psychotherapy modifier evidence is mixed

A 2026 IPD meta-analysis of combined psychotherapy + pharmacotherapy versus pharmacotherapy alone in depression found average benefit but no identified patient-level effect modifiers in the available IPD.

## 53. Other evidence finds severity-related heterogeneity

A 2026 meta-analysis of 387 randomized psychotherapy trials for depression found average effects tended to increase with baseline severity, though sensitivity analyses were not universally consistent.

Therefore:

**effect heterogeneity exists, but the specific moderators are not automatically stable or clinically actionable.**

## 54. Personalized-effect models need calibration/validation

Predicted individual treatment benefit requires:

- model validation;
- calibration;
- transport;
- adequate sample size;
- clinically meaningful decision benefit.

A treatment-effect model is not trustworthy because it outputs personalized numbers.

# IX. Fidelity

## 55. Fidelity is multidimensional

The NIH Behavior Change Consortium model distinguishes:

1. study design;
2. provider training;
3. intervention delivery;
4. treatment receipt;
5. enactment of skills.

## 56. Delivery

Did the provider/system deliver the intended content?

## 57. Receipt

Did the participant:

- understand;
- acquire;
- demonstrate

the relevant skills/content during treatment?

## 58. Enactment

Did the participant use the intervention skills/behaviors in daily life?

## 59. Delivery != receipt != enactment

A session can be delivered perfectly while:

- participant misunderstands it;
- participant understands but does not use it;
- participant uses it only transiently.

## 60. Fidelity measurement remains inconsistent

A 2026 scoping review identified 20 fidelity frameworks/models and substantial terminology/construct disagreement beyond delivery.

Therefore fidelity is important but not one universally standardized number.

## 61. Current FIPP work improves delivery guidance

The 2025/2026 Fidelity of Intervention delivery in Psychosocial and behavioral Programs guideline provides a consensus-based delivery-focused reporting/assessment approach.

It does not replace receipt/enactment or all other fidelity domains.

---

# X. Fidelity versus adaptation

## 62. Fidelity does not mean mechanical rigidity

Real-world interventions may need adaptation for:

- language;
- culture;
- setting;
- accessibility;
- delivery mode;
- resources.

## 63. Adaptation can preserve or destroy intervention function

The key question is not simply:

`Was the manual changed?`

but:

- what was changed;
- why;
- which core function/component was preserved;
- whether outcomes/target engagement changed.

## 64. Rigid adherence can itself become counterproductive

Context-sensitive implementation may require form changes while retaining core function.

But this should be documented and evaluated, not retroactively labeled "flexibility."

## 65. Unmeasured adaptation creates causal ambiguity

If providers adapt interventions idiosyncratically and effects vary, it becomes difficult to know:

- what package was actually tested;
- what component produced outcome.

---

# XI. Adherence, engagement, initiation, completion, attrition

## 66. Intervention initiation

Did the participant start the intervention?

## 67. Attendance/exposure

How much scheduled intervention was received?

## 68. Adherence

Did the participant perform required/recommended intervention behaviors?

Adherence is intervention-specific.

## 69. Engagement

Engagement can include:

- behavioral use;
- attention;
- cognitive involvement;
- emotional involvement.

It should not be treated as a single universal metric.

## 70. Completion

Finishing a predefined program does not prove:

- receipt;
- enactment;
- mechanism engagement;
- clinical benefit.

## 71. Dropout

Dropout means premature discontinuation under a study/treatment definition.

It is not automatically:

- rejection;
- nonresponse;
- worsening;
- low motivation.

## 72. Study attrition and treatment dropout differ

A person can:

- stop treatment but complete follow-up;
- complete treatment but not research assessment.

These create different biases.

## 73. Missing outcomes can bias efficacy estimates

If dropout/attrition relates to:

- outcome;
- treatment burden;
- adverse effects;
- prognosis,

complete-case analyses can mislead.

## 74. Current evidence shows initiation/dropout depend on modality and context

A 2026 meta-analysis comparing internet-based with face-to-face CBT found:

- different non-initiation rates;
- broadly comparable dropout/attrition in included trials.

This does not establish one modality as universally more acceptable.

---

# XII. Dose

## 75. Dose is not simply number of sessions

Possible dose dimensions:

- sessions;
- minutes;
- intensity;
- contact frequency;
- homework;
- actual exposure;
- skills practiced;
- enactment.

## 76. More dose is not universally better

A 2026 GAD psychotherapy meta-analysis found:

- number of sessions was not associated with effectiveness;
- treatment duration was not associated with effectiveness;
- session length showed one association.

This is domain-specific evidence, not a universal dose law.

## 77. Good-enough-level selection can confound dose–outcome relations

People who improve faster may stop earlier.

Then:

`fewer sessions -> better outcome`

or:

`more sessions -> worse outcome`

can reflect selection rather than causal dose effects.

## 78. Randomized dose comparisons are stronger

Dose optimization ideally requires randomized/sequential designs rather than observational session-count associations.

---

# XIII. Maintenance of change

## 79. End-of-treatment benefit != maintained benefit

Measure:

- post-treatment;
- short follow-up;
- longer follow-up.

## 80. Maintenance is a separate target

Behavior change may decay because:

- environmental contingencies return;
- support ends;
- competing habits remain;
- stress/context changes;
- skills are not enacted;
- new barriers emerge.

## 81. Maintenance mechanisms may differ from initiation mechanisms

What helps a person begin behavior can differ from what sustains it.

This aligns with the 2026 Annual Review three-phase framing:

- build motivation;
- follow through;
- develop durable patterns/habits.

## 82. Relapse is not proof the intervention never worked

Acute benefit and durable maintenance are separate outcomes.

## 83. Booster/support effects require testing

A booster is not automatically beneficial merely because maintenance is difficult.

---

# XIV. Individual maintenance versus implementation sustainment

## 84. RE-AIM separates maintenance at multiple levels

Maintenance concerns:

- durability of individual outcomes;
- sustainment of the program/practice in settings.

## 85. Clinical maintenance != organizational sustainability

A participant can maintain gains after a program disappears from a service.

A service can sustain a program that has weak individual effects.

Do not collapse them.

---

# XV. Implementation outcomes

## 86. Implementation success is not clinical effectiveness

Proctor's framework separates implementation outcomes from service and client outcomes.

## 87. Eight classic implementation outcomes

1. acceptability;
2. adoption;
3. appropriateness;
4. feasibility;
5. fidelity;
6. implementation cost;
7. penetration;
8. sustainability.

## 88. Acceptability

Stakeholders find the intervention agreeable/satisfactory.

Acceptability does not prove effectiveness.

## 89. Adoption

Providers/settings choose to use it.

Adoption does not prove fidelity or benefit.

## 90. Appropriateness

Perceived fit/relevance for context/problem.

Appropriateness does not prove efficacy.

## 91. Feasibility

Can the intervention be carried out successfully in the setting?

## 92. Fidelity

Was it implemented as intended?

## 93. Cost

What resources are required for implementation?

## 94. Penetration

How extensively is the intervention integrated/reached within the service system?

## 95. Sustainability

Does implementation persist over time?

## 96. Implementation outcomes need their own measurement validity

Proctor's framework explicitly highlighted measurement problems.

A home-grown "acceptability score" is not automatically valid.

---

# XVI. Reach and selection

## 97. Reach

RE-AIM defines reach through:

- number;
- proportion;
- representativeness

of intended individuals who participate.

## 98. Trial participants can differ from target population

Selection can occur through:

- eligibility criteria;
- recruitment channel;
- burden;
- digital access;
- willingness;
- language;
- comorbidity exclusions.

## 99. High efficacy with low reach can produce low population impact

Likewise:

- high reach with negligible effect has low impact.

Population value requires more than within-trial effect size.

## 100. Nonparticipation is evidence about implementation/transport

It should not be treated as irrelevant missingness.

---

# XVII. Context

## 101. MRC treats interventions as events in systems

Complex interventions interact with:

- social;
- organizational;
- cultural;
- economic;
- political;
- physical

context.

## 102. Context can modify intervention effect

The same package can differ because:

- provider capacity differs;
- institutional incentives differ;
- norms differ;
- support/resources differ;
- baseline risk differs.

## 103. Context can also be changed by intervention

Interventions can alter:

- norms;
- relationships;
- workflows;
- feedback loops.

Thus context is not always an external fixed moderator.

## 104. Contextual transport requires evidence

`worked in setting A -> works in setting B`

requires more than similarity of intervention label.

---

# XVIII. Intervention failure versus implementation failure

## 105. Intervention failure

The intervention was delivered adequately but did not generate meaningful benefit.

## 106. Implementation failure

An efficacious intervention failed to reach/deliver/engage/sustain adequately in the new context.

## 107. Poor outcome alone cannot distinguish them

Need:

- fidelity;
- reach;
- receipt;
- enactment;
- context;
- implementation outcomes.

## 108. Proctor framework makes this distinction explicit

Clinical outcome alone is insufficient to evaluate implementation success.

# XIX. Harms and unintended consequences

## 109. Psychological/behavioral interventions can have harms

Potential outcomes include:

- symptom worsening;
- burden;
- opportunity cost;
- stigma;
- conflict;
- dependency;
- increased distress;
- unintended behavior change.

## 110. Absence of reported harm != evidence of no harm

Harms may be:

- unmeasured;
- poorly reported;
- selectively collected.

## 111. CONSORT 2025 requires transparent outcome reporting

Benefits and harms require clear prespecification/reporting.

## 112. Net benefit is broader than symptom improvement

Consider:

- function;
- quality of life;
- burden;
- adverse effects;
- access;
- cost;
- durability.

# XX. Control-group effects

## 113. Waitlist is not inert

Waitlist can change:

- expectations;
- help-seeking;
- disappointment;
- monitoring.

## 114. Attention control is not inert

Human contact, monitoring, psychoeducation and expectation may have effects.

## 115. Usual care varies

"Usual care" can differ across:

- countries;
- services;
- time.

Comparator content must be described.

## 116. Active comparator answers a narrower question

Two effective packages can show small differences even when both outperform no treatment.

Thus:

`small between-active-treatment difference -> both ineffective`

is invalid.

---

# XXI. Outcome measurement

## 117. Statistical significance != useful change

Need:

- effect size;
- precision;
- meaningfulness;
- functional outcome;
- individual variation.

## 118. Symptom score != behavior

Behavior-change interventions should measure the behavior when behavior is the target.

## 119. Self-report != objective behavior

Both can be useful.

Neither is universally superior.

## 120. Measurement reactivity can alter behavior

Monitoring itself can become an intervention component.

## 121. Composite outcomes can hide tradeoffs

If one component improves and another worsens, a composite may obscure it.

## 122. Reliable change != meaningful change

PSYCHOMETRICS controls person-level change interpretation.

---

# XXII. Mediation and measurement

## 123. Mediator measurement must be valid

A poorly measured target can generate:

- attenuation;
- false null;
- unstable mediation;
- construct overlap.

## 124. Outcome overlap can inflate mechanism claims

If mediator and outcome share items/content, indirect associations can become partly tautological.

## 125. Repeated measures strengthen temporal inference

But repeated measures alone do not identify causality.

## 126. Within-person and between-person mediation differ

A person who generally has high self-efficacy and does well is different from:

`this person's increase in self-efficacy caused later improvement`.

CLN-02 controls this distinction.

---

# XXIII. Fidelity and mechanism interact

## 127. No target engagement with low delivery fidelity is ambiguous

Possibilities:

- intervention was not delivered;
- participant did not receive it;
- target theory is wrong;
- measure failed.

## 128. Good fidelity + no target engagement is more damaging to target theory

If intended manipulation was delivered well but target does not change, the proposed intervention-to-target link weakens.

## 129. Target engagement + no outcome challenges target relevance

Possibilities:

- target not causally relevant;
- effect too small;
- downstream context blocks translation;
- outcome measure insensitive.

## 130. Outcome improvement + no target engagement weakens proposed mechanism

Alternative pathways should be investigated.

---

# XXIV. Component and mechanism evidence are not identical

## 131. A component can be effective through multiple mechanisms

Example:

social-support intervention could affect:

- stress appraisal;
- practical resources;
- reinforcement;
- emotion regulation;
- belonging.

## 132. A mechanism can be engaged by multiple components

Self-efficacy could be changed by:

- mastery;
- feedback;
- modeling;
- goal progress.

## 133. Therefore

`component = mechanism`

is invalid.

Use:

`component -> target/mechanism hypothesis -> test`.

---

# XXV. Treatment-effect heterogeneity

## 134. Average treatment effect is a population quantity

It does not imply:

- everyone benefits;
- same magnitude;
- no harms;
- one mechanism.

## 135. Variation in observed individual change != treatment-effect heterogeneity

Observed change combines:

- treatment effect;
- natural history;
- measurement error;
- regression;
- context;
- other events.

Individual causal effects are not directly observed in parallel-group RCTs.

## 136. Responder analyses can mislead

"Responder" status depends on:

- threshold;
- measurement error;
- baseline;
- regression;
- comparator response.

## 137. Treatment-effect heterogeneity requires interaction evidence

Not just outcome variability.

## 138. Personalized treatment rules need external validation

Without validation they are exploratory models.

---

# XXVI. Common intervention-analysis errors

## Failure A — efficacy = effectiveness

Incorrect.

Controlled benefit may not transport to routine settings.

## Failure B — effectiveness = implementation success

Incorrect.

Clinical outcomes and implementation outcomes differ.

## Failure C — package works = component works

Incorrect.

Package RCT does not isolate components.

## Failure D — component works = proposed mechanism true

Incorrect.

Component can operate via multiple pathways.

## Failure E — mediator = mechanism

Incorrect.

Causal mediation assumptions and target perturbation matter.

## Failure F — target engagement = mechanism

Incomplete.

Target must causally affect outcome.

## Failure G — theory-consistent result = theory confirmed

Incorrect.

Alternative mechanisms remain.

## Failure H — moderator = mechanism

Incorrect.

Effect modification and mediation differ.

## Failure I — subgroup significant here, not there = interaction

Incorrect.

Direct interaction test is required.

## Failure J — personalized estimate = personalized evidence

Incorrect.

Calibration/validation/transport are required.

## Failure K — fidelity = provider adherence only

Incorrect.

Design, training, delivery, receipt and enactment can differ.

## Failure L — attendance = adherence

Incorrect.

Attendance does not establish enactment.

## Failure M — completion = success

Incorrect.

Completion and outcome are separate.

## Failure N — dropout = treatment failure

Incorrect.

Reasons and missingness differ.

## Failure O — more sessions = stronger treatment

Incorrect.

Dose dimensions and selection effects matter.

## Failure P — short-term benefit = maintenance

Incorrect.

Durability needs follow-up.

## Failure Q — individual maintenance = organizational sustainability

Incorrect.

Different levels.

## Failure R — acceptability = effectiveness

Incorrect.

People can like ineffective interventions.

## Failure S — adoption = effectiveness

Incorrect.

Popular interventions can be ineffective.

## Failure T — fidelity = no adaptation

Incorrect.

Adaptation can be necessary, but must be documented/evaluated.

## Failure U — usual care = inert control

Incorrect.

Usual care contains active care.

## Failure V — waitlist = inert control

Incorrect.

Expectancy/timing effects can differ.

## Failure W — no reported harms = safe

Incorrect.

Harms may be unmeasured.

## Failure X — effect-size heterogeneity = person-level moderator

Incorrect.

Study-level heterogeneity does not identify individual effect modification.

1. efficacy, effectiveness and implementation are distinct;
2. intervention label is not sufficient specification;
3. package efficacy does not identify active component;
4. component does not equal mechanism;

15. short-term response is not maintenance;

18. context can change intervention impact;
19. average treatment effect does not specify individual causal effect;

# XXXI. Canonical working rule

**An intervention result is interpretable only after separating what was offered, what was delivered, what was received/enacted, what changed, for whom, for how long, through what evidence-supported process, and under what context.**

Use:

`package -> delivery -> receipt/enactment -> outcome -> heterogeneity -> target engagement -> causal mechanism evidence -> maintenance -> implementation/context`.

Never:

`works -> mechanism proven`

`mediator -> mechanism`

`BCT -> active ingredient`

`completion -> adherence`

`attendance -> enactment`

`efficacy -> effectiveness`

`effectiveness -> implementation success`

`short-term response -> maintenance`

`average effect -> individual effect`

`subgroup p-value -> personalized treatment`

`popular/acceptable -> effective`.

## Source-boundary note

The NIH Stage Model, NIMH experimental-therapeutics framework, MRC framework, CONSORT 2025, TIDieR, RE-AIM and Proctor implementation outcomes are used as methodological frameworks, not as competing universal ontologies.

BCT taxonomies/ontology specify intervention content. They do not prove active ingredients or mechanisms.

Recent 2026 component/IPD/meta-analytic findings are used as examples of component and heterogeneity inference. Their numerical results are not generalized beyond the studied populations/interventions.

The 2026 GAD dose-response meta-analysis does not establish a universal psychotherapy dose rule.

Mechanism/process studies are treated as evidence of temporal/process relations under their specific designs; they do not override Phase A causal-identification requirements.

No source is used to prescribe an individualized intervention.
