# PSY-RES-09 — Evidence-to-individual reasoning and uncertainty calibration

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Establish an explicit bridge from population-level psychological evidence and private observations to person-specific hypotheses without overclaiming traits, mechanisms, diagnoses, causal explanations, or future outcomes.

This block converts the methodology from PSY-RES-01 through PSY-RES-08 into an individual-inference protocol.

The target is not "certainty about a person."

The target is:

**the best currently supported model, with explicit alternatives, uncertainty, disconfirming evidence, and revision rules.**

## Core rule

Use:

`population evidence -> applicability check -> individual observation -> candidate hypotheses -> rival hypotheses -> discriminating predictions -> new evidence -> update -> confidence -> revision`

Never:

`familiar psychological pattern -> label -> confident explanation of the person`.

## 1. Population evidence is a prior constraint, not a person-specific conclusion

Population research can establish that:

- a construct exists;
- a relation is common;
- a mechanism is plausible;
- a risk factor changes probability;
- a treatment helps on average;
- a trait tends to predict an outcome.

It does not automatically establish that the same relation or mechanism is present in a particular person.

Fisher, Medaglia, and Jeronimus showed empirically that between-person and within-person relations can differ substantially and argued that group-to-individual generalization should be tested rather than assumed.

Therefore:

`population pattern -> candidate expectation for an individual`

is defensible.

`population pattern -> this individual works this way`

is not.

## 2. Individual evidence does not erase population evidence

The reverse error is also invalid:

`one personal observation -> population evidence irrelevant`.

Population evidence provides:

- base rates;
- plausible mechanisms;
- expected ranges;
- known moderators;
- measurement knowledge;
- alternative explanations.

Individual evidence should **update** population-informed expectations rather than replace scientific knowledge wholesale.

Canonical rule:

**Nomothetic evidence constrains the hypothesis space; idiographic evidence determines how well a hypothesis fits the person.**

## 3. Evidence-based individual reasoning is probabilistic

APA's evidence-based practice framework states that applying research evidence to an individual involves probabilistic inference and requires attention to the person's characteristics, context, and ongoing data.

Individual conclusions should therefore be expressed as:

- supported;
- weakened;
- unresolved;
- contradicted;
- more or less plausible than alternatives;

rather than as absolute truths unless the proposition is directly observable.

## 4. Start from the exact proposition being inferred

Before evaluating evidence, write the proposition explicitly.

Examples of proposition types:

- **descriptive:** pattern X occurs repeatedly;
- **trait:** X is relatively stable across relevant contexts/time;
- **state:** X is transient/context-linked;
- **functional:** A tends to precede or alter B within the person;
- **causal:** manipulating A would change B;
- **mechanistic:** A changes B through M;
- **predictive:** condition A predicts later B;
- **clinical:** symptom/syndrome/diagnosis claim;
- **capacity:** person can perform behavior/strategy X under specified conditions;
- **preference:** person tends to choose X over Y under specified conditions.

Different propositions require different evidence.

Do not let evidence for one proposition silently support another.

## 5. Observation, self-report, interpretation, and inference are separate layers

Record individual data at four levels.

### Level A — Direct observation / raw report

What was actually observed, measured, or stated?

### Level B — Context

When, where, under what conditions, with what antecedents/consequences?

### Level C — Interpretation

What psychological meaning could the observation have?

### Level D — Inference

What broader proposition about the person is being supported?

The transition from A to D is not automatic.

## 6. Source quality matters at the individual level

Individual evidence can come from:

- direct behavior;
- self-report;
- informant report;
- standardized measurement;
- structured interview;
- records;
- repeated observations;
- performance tasks;
- physiological/digital measures;
- experimental/behavioral perturbation.

Each source has different error modes.

Self-report can be the strongest evidence for subjective experience.

It may be weaker for:

- exact causal attribution;
- unobserved behavior;
- retrospective chronology;
- processes outside awareness.

Behavioral observation may be strong for what happened but not automatically for motive.

## 7. Repetition strengthens pattern claims only when evidence is informative

Repeated observations strengthen an inference when they:

- occur across independent occasions;
- cover relevant contexts;
- use sufficiently reliable observation;
- are not all generated by the same biasing process.

Ten retellings of one event are not ten independent observations.

Ten questionnaire items with near-identical wording are not ten independent sources.

Canonical rule:

**count information sources, not merely information instances.**

## 8. Cross-context consistency supports trait-like inference

A stable trait hypothesis gains support when a pattern:

- appears repeatedly;
- persists across relevant settings;
- survives changes in immediate state;
- predicts behavior prospectively;
- is supported by different evidence sources.

A context-bound pattern should not be upgraded to a general trait without such evidence.

Conversely, context-specific variability can itself be systematic and psychologically meaningful.

## 9. Within-person evidence is required for within-person mechanisms

If the claim concerns:

> what tends to happen inside this person over time,

then the strongest relevant evidence comes from repeated within-person observations or experimental perturbations.

Between-person evidence can generate hypotheses but cannot by itself establish an intraindividual functional relation.

Person-specific assessment research explicitly recommends repeated individualized measurement to test hypothesized functional relations.

## 10. A case formulation is a hypothesis model, not a fact inventory

A person-specific formulation can be understood as an **idiographic theory** containing:

1. a measurement model — what constructs/behaviors are being represented and how;
2. a functional/causal model — what variables are hypothesized to trigger, maintain, modify, or predict other variables.

Mumma emphasizes that both levels can and should be tested with person-specific data.

Therefore a formulation must be revisable.

If it cannot in principle be wrong, it is not functioning as a scientific model.

## 11. Rival hypotheses are mandatory

For every nontrivial psychological inference, maintain at least one plausible alternative until the evidence discriminates among them.

Useful alternative classes include:

- different psychological construct;
- situational explanation;
- transient state;
- measurement/reporting artifact;
- social/contextual cause;
- developmental factor;
- physiological/medical factor where relevant;
- interaction of several mechanisms;
- no stable underlying mechanism.

The preferred hypothesis should win because it explains evidence **better**, not because it was generated first.

## 12. Contradictions have high information value

Evidence inconsistent with the current model should be recorded explicitly.

Do not:

- reinterpret every contradiction until the model survives;
- dismiss exceptions automatically;
- change the model's definition after seeing contrary evidence.

A contradiction can indicate:

- wrong hypothesis;
- overly broad hypothesis;
- missing moderator;
- state/context dependence;
- measurement error;
- temporal change;
- multiple mechanisms.

Canonical rule:

**A model that explains every possible outcome explains nothing discriminating.**

## 13. Generate discriminating predictions

For each candidate hypothesis ask:

1. What should be observed if it is true?
2. What should be absent?
3. Under what context should the effect strengthen/weaken?
4. What would the rival hypothesis predict differently?
5. What future observation would change confidence most?

Predictions should preferably be specified **before** examining the next relevant evidence.

This reduces retrospective story-fitting.

## 14. Prospective prediction is stronger than post-hoc fit for model testing

A hypothesis created after an event can often fit that event.

Its credibility increases if it successfully predicts:

- a new behavior;
- a new context;
- a future state;
- a response to a perturbation;
- an exception/boundary condition.

Repeated prospective success is especially useful for individualized formulation.

Prediction alone still does not prove causality.

## 15. Perturbation can test functional hypotheses

When safe, ethical, and appropriate, a deliberate change in one variable can provide stronger evidence about a functional relation than passive observation.

Generic structure:

`baseline -> controlled change in A -> observe B -> repeat / reverse / compare`.

Potential threats remain:

- expectancy;
- time trends;
- regression to the mean;
- concurrent changes;
- learning;
- carryover;
- nonstationarity.

A single before-after change is not sufficient causal proof.

## 16. Bayesian discipline without fake precision

The logic of individual assessment is compatible with Bayesian updating:

`prior plausibility -> diagnosticity of new evidence -> updated plausibility`.

But numerical probabilities should be used only when there is a defensible basis for them.

Do not fabricate:

- "80% likely";
- exact posterior odds;
- pseudo-Bayesian confidence values;

when no calibrated likelihood/base-rate information exists.

Qualitative Bayesian reasoning is acceptable:

- initially plausible;
- strengthened;
- substantially strengthened;
- weakened;
- substantially weakened;
- unresolved.

## 17. Base rates matter

Rare explanations should not become the default merely because they fit an observation vividly.

Assessment literature treats base rates as relevant to probabilistic interpretation.

However:

`low base rate -> impossible`

is also invalid.

Base rates provide prior constraints; sufficiently diagnostic individual evidence can update them.

## 18. Incremental validity: new data must add information

Before seeking another test, anecdote, or source, ask:

> What uncertainty will this new datum reduce beyond what is already known?

Incremental validity depends on:

- existing information;
- assessment goal;
- criterion;
- population;
- cost/burden;
- redundancy with current evidence.

More data is not automatically better if it is redundant or low quality.

## 19. Independent convergence increases confidence

Confidence increases more when different evidence channels converge than when one channel repeats itself.

Example generic structure:

- self-report;
- behavior;
- prospective prediction;
- standardized measure;
- informant;
- repeated time-series pattern.

Independence is relative, not absolute.

Shared sources and shared methods reduce the incremental value of convergence.

## 20. Source disagreement is evidence, not noise

If sources diverge, do not automatically choose the preferred one.

Ask whether disagreement reflects:

- different contexts;
- different time periods;
- different construct facets;
- self-presentation;
- observer access;
- measurement error;
- true state variability.

Discordance can reveal the architecture of the phenomenon.

## 21. Time must be part of the individual model

A person-specific model should distinguish:

- stable baseline;
- state fluctuation;
- developmental change;
- learning/adaptation;
- episodic change;
- context-triggered change.

Evidence from different periods should not be treated as simultaneous without justification.

A historical observation may establish capability/history without proving current baseline frequency.

## 22. Ability, preference, frequency, and state are separate claims

Observed use of a strategy can support:

- **ability:** the strategy can be executed;
- **frequency:** it occurs often;
- **preference:** it tends to be selected when alternatives exist;
- **state:** it occurred under a specific condition.

Do not infer preference or stable trait merely from demonstrated ability.

Do not infer inability merely from non-use.

## 23. Negative evidence must be interpreted carefully

Failure to observe a behavior can mean:

- the behavior is absent;
- the opportunity never occurred;
- the context suppressed it;
- measurement missed it;
- the person chose another option;
- the behavior is rare.

Therefore absence of evidence is informative only when there was a meaningful opportunity to observe the predicted event.

## 24. Confidence should track evidence quality, not narrative coherence

A psychologically elegant explanation can feel convincing because it is coherent.

Coherence is useful but insufficient.

Confidence should depend on:

- observation quality;
- measurement validity;
- independence of evidence;
- replication/repetition;
- cross-context consistency;
- temporal fit;
- discriminating predictions;
- contradiction handling;
- population applicability;
- rival-hypothesis performance.

## 25. Confidence tags

Use confidence tags as **ordinal epistemic labels**, not numerical probabilities.

### OPEN

Hypothesis generated; evidence insufficient for preference.

### LOW

Some compatible evidence exists, but alternatives remain equally or more plausible, evidence is sparse, or major contradictions/missing data remain.

### MODERATE

Multiple relevant observations support the hypothesis; it explains more than alternatives, but important uncertainty or untested predictions remain.

### HIGH

Converging, reasonably independent evidence supports the hypothesis across relevant contexts/time; competing explanations are substantially weakened; prospective/discriminating predictions have succeeded.

### VERY HIGH

Reserved for propositions with repeated, high-quality, multi-source or directly testable evidence and few viable alternatives.

For psychological mechanisms, **VERY HIGH should be rare** unless person-specific functional evidence is strong.

These labels are internal reasoning aids, not validated probability scales.

## 26. Calibration is a property that can be measured

Calibration asks whether stated confidence corresponds to actual correctness over repeated judgments.

For resolvable claims, predictions can be tracked prospectively.

A well-calibrated set of 70% predictions should be correct approximately 70% of the time.

Clinical-psychology methodology literature emphasizes calibration because experts and models can discriminate cases while still systematically over- or underestimating probabilities.

Where numeric predictions are justified, track:

- calibration;
- discrimination;
- Brier score or another proper scoring rule where appropriate.

Where numeric probabilities are not justified, track whether HIGH-confidence claims are later revised disproportionately often.

## 27. Revision rate is diagnostic of confidence discipline

If HIGH/VERY HIGH claims are frequently overturned, confidence is miscalibrated even if individual explanations sounded reasonable.

Maintain an error log with:

- original proposition;
- original confidence;
- evidence basis;
- later contradictory evidence;
- reason for error;
- corrected model;
- preventable reasoning failure.

Recurring error types should alter future confidence thresholds.

## 28. Correction handling rule

When the person corrects a factual statement:

- update the observation layer.

When the correction changes what a behavior **meant**:

- update interpretation and affected hypotheses.

When the correction changes the causal/functional mechanism:

- treat it as a substantive model error;
- identify which inference step failed;
- lower confidence in related untested claims generated by the same reasoning pattern.

Do not relabel a mechanism-level correction as a wording issue.

## 29. Evidence dependency map

For important conclusions, record which observations support which hypotheses.

Generic structure:

`O1, O2 -> H1`

`O2, O3 -> H2`

`O4 contradicts H1`

This makes hidden dependence visible.

If five conclusions all depend on one uncertain observation, they are not five independent conclusions.

## 30. Model complexity penalty

Prefer the simplest model that explains the evidence **without discarding important contradictions**.

Do not add hidden mechanisms solely to rescue a preferred interpretation.

A more complex model is warranted when it:

- predicts additional data;
- explains previously unresolved variation;
- survives independent tests;
- distinguishes itself from simpler alternatives.

## 31. Action and intervention can be both behavior and evidence

An individual's action can:

1. change the situation;
2. reveal capability/preference;
3. generate feedback;
4. test a prediction.

But the interpretation must distinguish these roles.

A response to an intervention can update the model.

It does not automatically prove why the response occurred.

## 32. Ongoing monitoring is part of individual inference

APA evidence-based practice explicitly notes that applying research to a particular patient involves probabilistic inference and that ongoing monitoring and adjustment are essential.

The generic methodological lesson extends beyond treatment:

**an individual model should update when new observations arrive.**

A model frozen after initial formulation becomes historical description rather than active inference.

## 33. Individual Evidence Ladder

This ladder describes increasing person-specific support for a proposition.

It is not a universal quality hierarchy; different claims may require different evidence.

### Level 0 — Population plausibility

General research makes the hypothesis scientifically plausible.

No person-specific support yet.

### Level 1 — Single compatible observation

One observation/report fits the hypothesis.

Alternative explanations remain broad.

### Level 2 — Repeated person-specific pattern

The pattern recurs across relevant occasions.

### Level 3 — Cross-context / multisource convergence

Different contexts or reasonably independent sources converge.

### Level 4 — Discriminating prospective prediction

The hypothesis correctly predicts new evidence that plausible rivals did not predict as well.

### Level 5 — Person-specific functional test

Repeated within-person data or safe perturbation supports the hypothesized functional relation.

### Level 6 — Replicated person-specific model

The functional/predictive model survives repeated tests across relevant time/context and known boundary conditions are specified.

## 34. Individual Inference Record

For every important person-specific conclusion, store privately:

1. **Proposition**
2. **Claim type**
3. **Raw observations**
4. **Sources**
5. **Dates/time relevance**
6. **Relevant contexts**
7. **Population evidence**
8. **Applicability/transport concerns**
9. **Candidate hypothesis**
10. **Rival hypotheses**
11. **Supporting evidence**
12. **Contradicting evidence**
13. **Evidence dependencies**
14. **Predictions**
15. **Discriminating test / next useful observation**
16. **Current confidence tag**
17. **Reasons for confidence**
18. **Known uncertainty**
19. **Revision triggers**
20. **Current status: open / provisional / stable / contradicted / retired**

This structure belongs in the private project, not the public repository.

## 35. Evidence-to-Individual Gate

Before adding a substantive person-specific inference, ask:

### A. Proposition

1. What exact proposition am I making?
2. Is it observation, trait, state, capacity, preference, prediction, causal relation, mechanism, or clinical claim?
3. Does my wording exceed that proposition?

### B. Population evidence

4. What population-level evidence makes the hypothesis plausible?
5. How strong is that evidence?
6. Is it transferable to the target person/context?
7. Am I confusing between-person and within-person evidence?

### C. Individual data

8. What direct person-specific observations support the claim?
9. How many genuinely independent observations exist?
10. Across how many relevant contexts?
11. Across what time period?
12. Are measurements/reports reliable enough for this inference?

### D. Source structure

13. Is evidence all from one source/method?
14. Is subjective report the appropriate evidence type for the proposition?
15. Are informant/behavior/test data available?
16. Do sources converge or conflict?

### E. Alternatives

17. What are at least two plausible explanations if available?
18. What situational/state explanation exists?
19. What measurement/reporting explanation exists?
20. What simpler explanation exists?
21. Have I privileged the first hypothesis merely because it is familiar?

### F. Contradictions

22. What evidence contradicts the hypothesis?
23. Does the model explain the contradiction prospectively, or am I rescuing it post hoc?
24. Would I have predicted this contradictory observation before seeing it?

### G. Discrimination

25. What does the preferred hypothesis predict next?
26. What do rivals predict differently?
27. What observation would most efficiently discriminate among them?
28. Has any prospective prediction succeeded?

### H. Functional/causal claims

29. Is the claim merely correlational?
30. Is temporal order known?
31. Is there repeated within-person evidence?
32. Has any safe perturbation/intervention tested the proposed relation?
33. Could a third variable explain it?

### I. Base rates and incremental value

34. Am I ignoring the base rate because the explanation is vivid?
35. Does the new evidence actually add information beyond what is already known?
36. Am I double-counting correlated evidence?

### J. Confidence

37. Which confidence tag is justified?
38. What evidence would make confidence rise one level?
39. What evidence would make it fall one level?
40. Is confidence based on evidence or narrative coherence?

### K. Error control

41. Has a previous correction already weakened this reasoning route?
42. If this inference is wrong, what was the most likely failure point?

### L. Final statement

43. What is the strongest defensible conclusion now?
44. What remains unresolved?
45. What wording would constitute overclaiming?

## 36. High-value failure modes

### Failure A — population stereotype substitution

Incorrect:

`people with pattern X often do Y -> this person does Y for the same reason`.

Correct:

Population evidence generates a candidate hypothesis; person-specific evidence must establish fit.

### Failure B — anecdote overfit

Incorrect:

`one vivid event -> stable trait/mechanism`.

Correct:

Single observations usually support capability/state/event-level claims more strongly than stable trait claims.

### Failure C — repetition illusion

Incorrect:

`same story repeated many times -> many independent confirmations`.

Correct:

Repeated reference to one source/event remains one dependent evidence stream.

### Failure D — coherence inflation

Incorrect:

`the explanation fits everything -> high confidence`.

Correct:

High confidence requires discriminating evidence and successful predictions, not only narrative fit.

### Failure E — correction minimization

Incorrect:

`user corrected mechanism -> wording tweak`.

Correct:

Mechanism-changing correction is a model error and should propagate to dependent inferences.

### Failure F — contradiction absorption

Incorrect:

`any contrary evidence can be explained within the model`.

Correct:

A useful model exposes conditions that would weaken or falsify it.

### Failure G — base-rate neglect

Incorrect:

`rare explanation feels specific -> therefore likely`.

Correct:

Prior plausibility matters; diagnostic evidence must be strong enough to overcome low prior probability.

### Failure H — false Bayesian precision

Incorrect:

`I estimate 87% confidence` without calibrated evidence.

Correct:

Use ordinal confidence unless valid probabilistic inputs or a resolvable forecasting framework justify numbers.

### Failure I — between-person to within-person jump

Incorrect:

`people high in X also high in Y -> when this person becomes more X, Y rises`.

Correct:

That is a separate intraindividual hypothesis requiring within-person evidence.

### Failure J — ability = preference

Incorrect:

`person demonstrated tool X -> person prefers/regularly uses X`.

Correct:

Capability, preference, frequency, and state are different propositions.

### Failure K — historical = current

Incorrect:

`behavior occurred years ago -> current baseline style`.

Correct:

Historical data establish history/capability unless continuity is separately supported.

### Failure L — more data = more certainty

Incorrect.

Correct:

New data only deserve more confidence if they have sufficient reliability, relevance, independence, and incremental validity.

## Canonical working rule

**General science constrains what is plausible. Individual evidence determines what is supported. Rival hypotheses prevent pattern-matching. Prospective tests increase confidence. Contradictions force revision. Confidence must track evidence, not explanatory elegance.**

## Current-standard note

As of 2026-11-19:

- APA's 2020 *Guidelines for Psychological Assessment and Evaluation* remain listed by APA as current science guidelines and are scheduled for review around 2030.
- APA's evidence-based psychological-practice guidance remains current and explicitly treats application of research to a particular patient as probabilistic and subject to ongoing monitoring.
- The person-specific/idiographic literature is methodologically active; formal techniques continue to evolve. Therefore this block uses stable principles—level-of-analysis separation, repeated measurement, hypothesis testing, and calibration—rather than treating any one statistical model as canonical.

## Scope note

Much of the formal person-specific literature comes from clinical psychology and psychopathology. The present block extracts generic inference principles that also apply to nonclinical individualized psychological modeling.

This repository contains only the generic protocol. Any actual Individual Inference Record belongs in the private project and must not be committed here.
