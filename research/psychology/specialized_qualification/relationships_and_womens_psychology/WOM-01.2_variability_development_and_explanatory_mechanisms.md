# WOM-01.2 — Psychological variation among women and competing explanations of sex/gender differences

Date: 2026-11-25. Status: bounded research note complete; WOM-01 remains IN_PROGRESS.
Scope: personality distributions, developmental and cultural variation, and limits of biological/social causal attribution. Not a comprehensive account of women's psychology.

## 1. Question and continuity

How do differences between women and men vary by psychological construct, facet and context? What does longitudinal research among women themselves reveal about individual variation and development? Which designs can distinguish social and biological contributions, and which cannot?
WOM-01.1 was retrieved and read through its final handoff at head 8fdf3d90f73420ac6899972987910b4acaa3a544. Retain its measurement-invariance limitations and IND-03 individual transfer rules. No new full reread of foundational SOC/PER/DEV files.
Competing accounts: biological characteristics, social roles and norms, resource access, selection and socialization, developmental and cohort influences, measurement and sampling, interacting pathways. Not mutually exclusive.

## 2. Evidence ledger

Targeted public searches 2026-11-25: Rammstedt Roth Roemer Lechner 2026 revisiting gender differences personality 27 nations; women longitudinal personality development Mills/Radcliffe; sex differences personality social role theory cross-cultural gender equality; women longitudinal midlife perceived stress role quality SWAN. Selected primary and synthesis evidence plus contrary findings. No systematic review or raw-data reanalysis.

E1. Rammstedt, Roth, Roemer & Lechner (2026). Revisiting gender differences in personality: New evidence on big five domains and facets with large-scale samples from 27 nations. Personality and Individual Differences 257, 113806. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2026.113806
Published article repeatedly returned 403. Earlier preprint abstract (2025-03-20, author list differs from final article) directly read at https://sciety.org/articles/activity/10.31219/osf.io/ysd3p_v1 ; author-institution 2025 conference abstract checked at https://www.gesis.org/en/data-on-adult-education/conferences/piaac-conference-2025/piaac-conference-presentations-on-video . Both describe N=143,313 in 27 nationally representative adult samples, consistent translation methods, no overall Extraversion difference, larger reported effects for Neuroticism (.40) and Agreeableness (.35), particularly anxiety and compassion respectively in facet analyses. These numerical findings are verified for the earlier author abstracts, NOT the final 2026 results. Published metadata corroborates sample and topic; exact final estimates, invariance and sensitivity analyses remain unverified. Do not count preprint, conference and journal versions as independent studies. Consistent translation does not establish scalar invariance. No inference of clinical anxiety or individual disposition from group membership.

E2. Herlitz, Hönig, Hedebrant & Asperholm (2025 issue). A Systematic Review and New Analyses of the Gender-Equality Paradox. https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916231202685
Publisher HTML abstract, selection methods, personal-characteristics results and selected discussion directly inspected. Review of 54 articles and new analyses of 27 meta-analyses/large-scale studies. Associations between better national living conditions and magnitude of differences vary by domain; some personality differences increase, others show less systematic patterns, and several other psychological domains show decreases. Economic indicators were particularly predictive. Authors explicitly disclaim causal evidence. Country-level associations cannot identify person-level mechanisms or biological versus social contributions; wealth, education and equality are correlated. Different constructs and datasets, some overlap, historical and measurement heterogeneity, national-level aggregation. No universal equality-to-difference rule.

E3. Are many sex/gender differences really power differences? (2024). PNAS Nexus 3(2), pgae025. https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae025
Publisher abstract, analytic setup, discussion and limitations directly inspected. Directional comparison of 59 experimental power effects with 102 meta-analytic sex/gender differences: 72 concordant, 8 opposite. The authors selected significant experimental power effects, matched conceptually related rather than necessarily identical outcomes, and fewer than 5% of the power experiments tested a power-by-sex/gender interaction. This is suggestive evidence for a social-resource contribution to selected outcomes, NOT a mediation estimate, percentage of all differences caused by power, or evidence against every biological explanation. Experimental causal evidence concerns manipulation of power under tested conditions; mapping onto group differences remains correlational. Power here means actual or perceived resource control, not a personality label.

E4. Newton, Stewart & Vandewater (2019). Age is opportunity: Women's personality trajectories from mid- to later-life. Journal of Research in Personality 80, 43–54. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2019.04.005
Published abstract and author accepted manuscript as HTML transcription https://www.academia.edu/87899187/_Age_is_opportunity_Women_s_personality_trajectories_from_mid_to_later_life ; selected model results, discussion and limitations directly inspected. Four waves at ages 43, 53, 62, 72; N=166 Radcliffe alumnae. Growth models support distinct trajectories of generativity, stagnation, ego integrity and despair; positive constructs showed average linear increases, other constructs nonlinear trajectories. Unconditional model ICCs .27, .22, .20, .38 respectively; variability within women across time comprises a substantial share of observed variance, which includes measurement variability. One measure did not meet the authors' desired factorial-invariance standard. Specific educated historical cohort, four widely separated waves, missingness and measurement constraints; no universal stages, age-triggered psychological mechanism or prediction for an individual woman.

E5. Hedgeman et al. (2018). Perceived stress across the midlife: longitudinal changes among a diverse sample of women, the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN). https://doi.org/10.1186/s40695-018-0032-3
Open publisher HTML abstract, design, regression results, discussion, limitations and conclusion excerpts inspected. 3,044 women aged 42–52 at baseline, approximately annual assessment over 13 visits. Self-reported perceived stress generally declined, but increased in certain groups at the New Jersey site, where follow-up was shorter. Baseline hardship and education were associated with stress; adjusted menopausal stage was not significant (reported p=.5203). Stage is not a direct hormonal measurement; this is about perceived stress, not all emotions, health or reproductive transitions. Site-specific follow-up, small subgroups, baseline-only financial hardship, measurement windows and observational model choices limit interpretation. Do not translate a nonsignificant stage association into proof of no biological effects or rank causal contributions. Women's trajectories vary by exposure, resources and setting.

E6. Social role and birth cohort influences on gender-linked personality traits in women: a 20-year longitudinal analysis (2006). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17059312/
Indexed primary abstract only; direct PubMed opening blocked. Four assessments of 758 mothers across two decades, growth models of historical gender-linked trait measures. Both positively framed trait sets increased over mean ages 39–59. Indicates that gender-linked dimensions need not be opposite ends of one scale and change can occur within women. Historical construct definitions, cohort selection and detailed role/cohort estimates not verified; no causal claim.

E7. The CEO of women's work lives: how Big Five Conscientiousness, Extraversion, and Openness predict 50 years of work experiences in a changing sociocultural context (2011). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21859225/
Indexed primary abstract only; direct PubMed opening blocked. Mills longitudinal cohort, trait–work associations varied across historical and occupational stages. This suggests context can moderate personality expression and predictive relations; no verified numerical effects or causal disentanglement of age, period and cohort. Not an independent test of every Mills study.

Evidence boundaries: E2–E5 directly inspected relevant sections, not full article end-to-end. E1 prior author abstracts verified, final numerical results inaccessible; E6/E7 indexed abstracts only. No exhaustive literature or source-status audit. Within-women evidence comes from independent historical-cohort and SWAN research streams, not just women–men comparisons. Detailed motherhood, reproductive psychophysiology, emotion and social strategy mechanisms remain assigned to subsequent units.

## 3. Integration and competing explanations

A descriptive group difference is not a mechanism. Test different claims with different designs:
- Distributional: estimate means, dispersion, overlap, facet patterns and uncertainty with comparable measures. A small overall difference can conceal divergent facet results; a null broad domain does not prove equality of every facet.
- Social/contextual: examine role demands, norms, opportunities, resources and historical conditions directly. Cross-national moderators are ecological; experiments may identify local effects of a specific exposure but not how much it mediates a population gap.
- Biological: specify the actual biological variable, timing and target outcome, and consider reciprocal influences, confounding and context. Sex/gender category or age alone is not a biological measure. Reserve detailed physiological evidence for WOM-06.
- Developmental: distinguish age, cohort, period, role transitions and within-person change. Repeated women-only measurements can reveal trajectories without identifying a sex-specific mechanism or claiming that all women change alike.
- Individual: use person-specific evidence under INT-02/IND-03. A woman's particular values, priorities, role experiences, appraisals, and observed patterns are not determined by group averages.

The central synthesis is not "all differences are social" or "all differences are biological." Multiple paths may contribute, including reciprocal role selection and experience; a group-level association or cross-cultural pattern is insufficient to estimate their causal shares. Recognize empirically supported differences while retaining distributional overlap, context, measurement and individual variation.

## 4. Practice and counterexample checks

Original neutral scenarios, not derived from any private data. Internal assessment only.
A. Two countries show different magnitudes of a questionnaire gap. This is a country-level moderator; sample composition, translation, measurement invariance, norms and resource differences are candidate explanations. It does not establish either individual socialization effects or innate difference.
B. An average trait-domain gap is absent while narrower facets differ. These observations are compatible because aggregation may cancel or dilute differences. Compare actual facet measures and uncertainty rather than claim universal equality or categorical difference.
C. A hypothetical longitudinal women's cohort has decreasing average stress with some increasing individual trajectories. Preserve direction and variability at both levels; examine context, exposure, assessment windows and selection. An average decline does not forecast every member.
D. A selected comparison finds many power effects directionally consistent with gender differences. The result supports investigation of a social pathway; it is not the fraction of variance caused by power. Test comparable outcomes, direct exposures and competing causal explanations.

External challenge: E2 blocks a universal cultural-equality convergence prediction; E3 supplies a potential contextual mechanism without identifying mediation; E4/E5 block uniform developmental narratives and automatic biological attribution. E1's final-version access gap prevents upgrading earlier numerical results to peer-reviewed final estimates. These checks use already-visible evidence, not independent or prospective assessment.
