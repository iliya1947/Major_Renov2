# PHY-13 — Bone physiology

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-18**  
Область: 03 — Physiology

## 0. Core decision

Bone is a mechanosensitive, continuously remodeling tissue.

Canonical model:

`site-specific mechanical loading`
`→ osteocyte mechanosensing`
`→ altered modeling/remodeling`
`→ density + geometry + microarchitecture + material adaptation`
`→ altered skeletal strength`.

No single marker captures the whole process.

## 1. Bone remodeling

Bone remodeling follows a coupled sequence of:

- activation;
- osteoclast-mediated resorption;
- reversal/coupling;
- osteoblast-mediated formation;
- mineralization.

Osteocytes help coordinate local remodeling and mechanical adaptation.

Project rule:

**remodeling is a coupled renewal process, not simply “building bone.”**

## 2. Osteoblasts / osteoclasts / osteocytes

### Osteoclasts
Resorb mineralized bone.

### Osteoblasts
Produce osteoid and support mineralization.

### Osteocytes
Act as central mechanosensory/coordinating cells in mature bone.

Project rule:

**cellular roles are related but distinct.**

## 3. Bone-turnover markers

Markers such as P1NP, osteocalcin/BALP and CTX are turnover proxies.

They do not directly quantify:

- local osteoblast/osteoclast numbers;
- local net balance;
- whole-bone strength.

Project rule:

**bone-turnover marker ≠ direct structural adaptation.**

## 4. Mechanotransduction

Bone responds to mechanical loading through osteocyte-centered mechanotransduction.

Relevant pathways include:

- fluid shear/matrix deformation;
- integrin/cytoskeletal signaling;
- Piezo1;
- Wnt/β-catenin;
- sclerostin/SOST;
- RANKL/OPG;
- YAP/TAZ-related pathways.

Project rule:

**molecular mechanotransduction supports mechanism but does not specify an optimal human exercise dose.**

## 5. Bone mineral density

DXA BMD is an important skeletal outcome.

Exercise can produce small-to-moderate, site-specific improvements or attenuate decline.

Project rule:

**BMD is important but is not total bone strength.**

## 6. Bone geometry / microarchitecture / strength

Mechanical competence also depends on:

- cortical thickness/area/porosity;
- periosteal/endosteal geometry;
- trabecular architecture;
- material quality;
- mineralization;
- matrix properties.

Project rule:

**bone strength is a systems property of density + structure + material quality.**

## 7. Site specificity

Exercise loading affects skeletal sites differently.

The same program can produce different responses at:

- lumbar spine;
- femoral neck;
- total hip;
- tibia;
- other sites.

Project rule:

**there is no single whole-body “bone adaptation” value.**

## 8. Loading magnitude

Higher local mechanical strain can be osteogenic.

But:

- external load is not local bone strain;
- muscle force is a major contributor to skeletal loading;
- geometry/technique alter local strain.

Project rule:

**more external load does not guarantee more bone adaptation.**

## 9. Loading rate

Higher loading rate can contribute to osteogenic signaling, especially with impact/ballistic exercise.

Project rule:

**rate matters, but no universal human threshold is established.**

## 10. Novelty / distribution / cycles

Bone response can depend on:

- novel strain distribution;
- directionality;
- cycle number;
- frequency;
- rest insertion;
- waveform.

Project rule:

**loading pattern matters, but exact animal-derived rules must not be copied directly into human programming.**

## 11. Resistance training

Resistance exercise can be osteogenic through external and muscle-generated forces.

Current evidence supports site-specific BMD maintenance/improvement.

High-intensity RT can improve muscle strength more than lower-intensity RT without showing a clearly greater lumbar-spine/femoral-neck BMD response.

Project rule:

**heavier RT ≠ automatically greater BMD adaptation.**

## 12. Impact / high-rate loading

Impact exercise can provide high-rate and multi-directional loading.

But:

- it is not universally required;
- more jump height is not automatically more osteogenic;
- safety depends on skeletal/clinical status.

Project rule:

**impact is one tool, not a universal “more is better” prescription.**

## 13. Acute biomarkers vs chronic adaptation

Exercise can acutely change:

- P1NP;
- CTX;
- osteocalcin/BALP;
- sclerostin-related signaling.

Project rule:

**acute marker response ≠ chronic BMD/geometry/strength adaptation.**

## 14. Adaptation time course

Bone adaptation follows multiple clocks.

### Fast
- signaling;
- turnover markers.

### Slower
- density;
- geometry;
- microarchitecture;
- estimated mechanical strength.

Structural changes generally require months to measure reliably.

Project rule:

**there is no universal bone-adaptation onset week.**

## 15. Aging

Aging affects:

- remodeling balance;
- osteoblast/osteocyte biology;
- matrix crosslinking;
- mineral properties;
- microdamage;
- cortical porosity;
- trabecular structure;
- mechanosensitivity.

Project rule:

**aging is more than lower BMD; it changes the response machinery itself.**

## 16. Energy availability

Low energy availability can suppress bone-formation markers and disturb resorption/inflammatory responses.

Project rule:

**mechanical loading cannot be assumed to fully offset sustained under-fuelling.**

Short-term biomarkers do not quantify long-term BMD loss.

Exact nutrition → NUT/IND.

## 17. Canonical project rules

1. Bone remodeling is coupled formation/resorption.
2. Turnover markers ≠ direct cellular balance.
3. Turnover markers ≠ whole-bone strength.
4. Osteocytes are central mechanosensors.
5. Mechanistic pathways ≠ human dose prescription.
6. BMD ≠ total bone strength.
7. Geometry/microarchitecture/material properties matter.
8. Bone adaptation is site specific.
9. External load ≠ local bone strain.
10. Magnitude, rate and loading pattern can all matter.
11. More impact height ≠ guaranteed greater adaptation.
12. Heavier RT ≠ automatically greater BMD response.
13. Acute biomarker response ≠ chronic adaptation.
14. Bone adaptation follows multiple clocks.
15. Aging modifies mechanosensitivity/remodeling.
16. Low energy availability can impair bone remodeling.
17. Clinical low-bone-mass/fracture-risk management belongs to MED.

## 18. Training-relevant implications

1. **Use repeated site-specific mechanical loading rather than relying on one global exercise label.**
2. **Resistance and impact loading can both contribute.**
3. **Do not chase maximal external load solely for bone.**
4. **Do not infer bone adaptation from muscle-strength improvement alone.**
5. **Do not infer whole-bone strength from BMD alone.**
6. **Do not rank chronic programs by one acute bone marker.**
7. **Expect structural bone adaptation to require months rather than days.**
8. **Respect age and energy-availability context.**
9. **Route abnormal low-bone-mass/fracture-risk questions to MED.**

## 19. What PHY-13 does not prescribe

Not owned here:

- exact impact count/height;
- exact RT %1RM/frequency for bone;
- DXA screening rules;
- T/Z-score interpretation;
- osteoporosis treatment;
- fracture-risk pharmacology;
- personal bone-stress-injury threshold;
- exact energy-availability target.

These remain MEC, MED, TEC, PRO, TST, NUT and IND.
