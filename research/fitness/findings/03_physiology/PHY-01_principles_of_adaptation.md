# PHY-01 — Principles of adaptation

Статус: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v2.0 — revalidated 2026-11-18**  
Область: 03 — Physiology

## 0. Decision

Создать базовую физиологическую модель training adaptation для downstream hypertrophy, strength, fatigue, recovery and programming без:

- acute-response → chronic-gain substitution;
- overload = only more weight;
- linear “more is always better” thinking;
- absolute specificity/no-transfer thinking;
- instant detraining assumptions;
- one-block responder/nonresponder labels;
- one universal recovery clock;
- fatigue-as-effectiveness proxy.

## 1. Homeostasis

Homeostasis describes regulation that keeps key internal variables within viable ranges despite perturbation.

Exercise acutely perturbs:
- energy/substrate state;
- temperature;
- ionic/acid-base conditions;
- cardiovascular demand;
- mechanical tissue state;
- neuroendocrine/immune signaling.

Project rule:

**exercise does not simply “break homeostasis so the body overcompensates.”**

## 2. Allostasis

Allostasis describes stability through adaptive change and coordinated regulation.

It is useful for understanding how:
- prior exposure;
- sleep;
- nutrition;
- psychosocial stress;
- environment

can modify response.

Project rule:

**homeostasis and allostasis are complementary concepts, not competing prescription systems.**

Allostatic load is not treated as one universally validated training score.

## 3. Stimulus → response → recovery → adaptation

Useful project sequence:

`training exposure → acute perturbation/signaling → transient response/fatigue → recovery/remodeling → retained adaptation`

The sequence is conditional, not guaranteed.

An acute exposure can produce:
- response without meaningful adaptation;
- excessive fatigue;
- damage;
- no detectable change;
- adaptation in one tissue but not another.

## 4. Acute response vs chronic adaptation

### Acute response
Minutes to days:
- fatigue;
- metabolites;
- swelling;
- hormonal response;
- transient signaling;
- soreness;
- temporary performance change.

### Chronic adaptation
Retained changes after repeated exposure:
- strength;
- hypertrophy;
- architecture;
- connective-tissue remodeling;
- metabolic/cardiorespiratory adaptation.

Project rule:

**acute magnitude is not automatically a valid chronic-gain surrogate.**

## 5. Overload

Overload means the stimulus remains sufficiently challenging relative to current capacity to drive the target adaptation.

It can be progressed through:
- external load;
- repetitions;
- sets/volume;
- ROM;
- leverage/exercise difficulty;
- velocity intent;
- density/rest;
- frequency;
- effort/proximity to failure.

Project rule:

**progressive overload ≠ mandatory load increase every session.**

## 6. Target-specific overload

The effective overload for:
- hypertrophy;
- maximal strength;
- power;
- endurance;
- tendon;
- bone

is not identical.

Project rule:

**“more overload” is meaningless without naming the target adaptation.**

## 7. Dose–response

Training dose is multidimensional.

Relevant variables include:
- load/intensity;
- volume;
- frequency;
- effort;
- ROM;
- velocity;
- density/rest;
- exercise selection;
- duration.

Project rule:

**dose–response is outcome-specific and often nonlinear.**

## 8. Diminishing returns

Additional training can still add benefit while marginal return decreases.

Therefore:

`more training ≠ proportionally more adaptation`

Additional dose can increasingly add:
- fatigue;
- recovery cost;
- time cost.

Project rule:

**the practical target is sufficient effective dose with favorable adaptation-to-cost ratio, not maximal possible dose.**

## 9. Minimum effective dose

A practical minimum effective dose depends on:
- target outcome;
- baseline capacity;
- training status;
- time horizon;
- measurement sensitivity.

Project rule:

**there is no single universal minimum-effective-dose number.**

## 10. Specificity

Adaptation tends to reflect repeated task demands.

Relevant dimensions:
- task/movement;
- contraction mode;
- joint angle/ROM;
- force/load range;
- velocity;
- muscle length;
- skill/coordination;
- energy-system demand.

Project rule:

**specificity is graded, not binary.**

## 11. Transfer

Transfer can occur when tasks share:
- muscles;
- force capacities;
- ROM;
- velocity;
- coordination demands;
- physiological systems.

Project rule:

**specificity does not imply zero transfer.**

## 12. Reversibility

When training stimulus is reduced or removed:
- some adaptations decay;
- different adaptations decay at different rates;
- gains do not disappear instantly.

Project rule:

**reversibility = time/outcome-dependent decay, not immediate reset to baseline.**

## 13. Repeated exposure

Adaptation requires repeated exposure because:
- many acute responses are transient;
- structural remodeling needs repeated cycles;
- neural/skill/tissue changes accumulate over sessions.

Repeated exposure can also change the response to later bouts.

## 14. Repeated-bout effect

Subsequent familiar exposures can produce less:
- soreness;
- damage;
- strength loss.

Project rule:

**a smaller damage response after familiarization does not mean the training stimulus became ineffective.**

## 15. Individual variability

Observed response differences can reflect:
- true biology;
- baseline status;
- prior training;
- actual dose received;
- adherence;
- sleep/nutrition/stress;
- age/sex/body size;
- measurement error;
- within-person variability;
- statistical noise.

Project rule:

**between-person spread does not automatically establish stable responder/nonresponder types.**

## 16. Responder / nonresponder boundary

Stable trainability requires repeated reliable evidence.

A single training block can be misleading because:
- measurement noise may exceed change;
- dose may be insufficient;
- adherence may differ;
- the outcome may be poorly matched;
- the intervention may be too short.

Project rule:

**one unchanged result does not prove a permanent inability to adapt.**

## 17. Temporal dynamics

Different adaptations can appear on different time scales.

Examples:
- early neural/skill contributions;
- muscle-size changes over longer periods;
- tendon/bone remodeling on different schedules;
- metabolic/cardiorespiratory changes with their own trajectories.

Project rule:

**measurement time point is part of the adaptation definition.**

## 18. Different tissue time courses

Muscle, tendon, bone, neural and metabolic systems do not necessarily recover/adapt on the same schedule.

Project rule:

**one global “recovered/not recovered” state is an oversimplification.**

## 19. Adaptation vs fatigue

Training produces at least two practically separable effects:

1. retained adaptation/fitness-related change;
2. fatigue/recovery cost that can temporarily suppress performance.

Project rules:

**temporary performance decrease after useful training does not prove negative adaptation.**

**low immediate fatigue does not prove the session was ineffective.**

## 20. Fatigue is task-dependent

Fatigue depends on:
- task;
- set duration;
- proximity to failure;
- volume;
- density/rest;
- load;
- measured outcome;
- time point.

Project rule:

**fatigue must be operationally defined.**

## 21. More fatigue ≠ more adaptation

Higher acute fatigue can occur with:
- closer-to-failure training;
- more volume;
- denser work.

But:

**fatigue is a cost/state, not the target chronic adaptation.**

It is not used as a proxy for stimulus quality.

## 22. Dose–adaptation–fatigue trade-off

At low dose:
- stimulus may be insufficient.

At effective dose:
- adaptation can rise efficiently.

At higher dose:
- adaptation may continue with diminishing returns;
- fatigue/recovery cost can rise faster.

At excessive dose:
- performance/recovery/adherence can deteriorate.

Project rule:

**progression is constrained by marginal benefit and recoverability.**

## 23. Fitness-fatigue model

Useful as a conceptual/quantitative approximation:
- training generates positive and negative latent effects with different decay profiles;
- observed performance reflects their net expression.

Project rule:

**fitness-fatigue is a decision model, not a literal two-compartment biological law.**

## 24. Supercompensation model

The classic:
`stress → fatigue → recovery → supercompensation`

can be pedagogically useful.

But it is too simple as a universal schedule because:
- tissues differ in time course;
- sessions overlap;
- no single marker captures all systems.

Project rule:

**do not schedule training solely from a universal supercompensation clock.**

## 25. Homeostasis / allostasis / hormesis boundary

A tolerable challenge can be adaptive when repeated with sufficient recovery/resources.

But:

**“stress is good in the right dose” is a heuristic, not a quantified universal law.**

## 26. Successful adaptation

Project definition:

**successful adaptation = a retained improvement in a target outcome or physiological capacity attributable to repeated training exposure, large enough to matter relative to measurement noise and practical cost.**

This requires:
- defined target;
- baseline;
- repeated exposure;
- time horizon;
- measurement method;
- uncertainty.

## 27. Maladaptation

Maladaptation is not:
- one poor session;
- transient soreness;
- one bad readiness score.

It implies a persistent unwanted pattern such as:
- declining performance;
- impaired recovery;
- symptoms/injury;
- persistent physiological/psychological disturbance;
- failure of expected progress despite adequate exposure.

Detailed overreaching/overtraining/pathology → REC/MED/PRO.

## 28. What PHY-01 can establish

PHY-01 can establish:

1. repeated effective exposure is needed;
2. acute response ≠ chronic adaptation;
3. overload is multidimensional;
4. dose–response is outcome-specific/nonlinear;
5. specificity is graded;
6. reversibility is time/outcome dependent;
7. individual-response labels require repeated reliable evidence;
8. tissues differ in time course;
9. fatigue is separable from retained adaptation.

## 29. What PHY-01 cannot prescribe

Not yet owned here:
- exact sets/week;
- exact RIR;
- exact frequency;
- exact deload timing;
- exact progression algorithm;
- exact personal “optimal dose”.

Those remain for PHY-07/08, PRO, TST, REC and IND.

## 30. Canonical project rules

1. Acute response ≠ chronic adaptation.
2. Overload ≠ only more weight.
3. Dose is multidimensional.
4. Dose–response is outcome-specific and often nonlinear.
5. More is not proportionally better indefinitely.
6. Specificity is graded; transfer can occur.
7. Reversibility is not instantaneous.
8. Individual variability is not automatically a stable trait.
9. Different tissues adapt on different time scales.
10. Fatigue is a cost/state, not a gain metric.
11. Fitness-fatigue/allostasis/supercompensation are bounded models, not literal universal laws.
12. Successful adaptation must be defined relative to target, time, measurement and practical cost.

## 31. Evidence / confidence

### HIGH
- acute vs chronic distinction;
- multidimensional overload;
- nonlinear/outcome-specific dose-response;
- graded specificity;
- reversibility;
- task-dependent fatigue;
- fatigue ≠ adaptation;
- different tissue time courses;
- caution against one-block responder classification.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT
- exact personal dose-response curve;
- exact detraining rate by tissue/outcome;
- stability of individual responsiveness across domains;
- quantitative allostatic-load indices;
- exact fitness-fatigue parameters.
