# 3. Lineage

VRE developed within health/health-services research but draws on multiple traditions.

The 2019 book and later methodology papers locate its intellectual/practical roots across:

- ethnography;
- visual anthropology;
- applied anthropology;
- medical sociology;
- health-services research;
- patient-safety research;
- practice theory;
- science and technology studies;
- adult education;
- critical pedagogy;
- participatory/action research;
- qualitative research ethics.

### Project lineage representation

**ethnographic contextual inquiry  
+ visual recording of situated practice  
+ participant/practitioner collaborative reflexivity  
+ practice theory / complexity attention  
+ participatory improvement  
→ Video-Reflexive Ethnography.**

### Critical rule

VRE is not:

> ethnography where a camera happens to be used.

The video-reflexive loop changes the epistemic and practical architecture.

---

# 4. Canonical scientific target

VRE seeks to:

> capture, illuminate and potentially optimise in-situ practice by enabling participants/practitioners to see, interrogate and reimagine their own everyday work through selected video footage.

The scientific object is often not:

- stable attitudes;
- isolated individual behaviour;
- decontextualized error;
- a one-time self-report.

It is:

- situated practice;
- interaction;
- tacit coordination;
- local complexity;
- taken-as-given routines;
- unnoticed competence;
- relational work;
- possibilities for change.

### Canonical project representation

ethnographic familiarisation  
+  
situated video capture  
+  
participant-sensitive clip selection  
+  
collaborative reflexive viewing  
+  
exnovation  
+  
practice re-imagination  
+  
possible iterative return to field/practice  
+  
care / collaboration / reflexivity / exnovation governance  
+  
contextual/practice claims.

---

# 5. Three-phase core architecture

Current methodology literature repeatedly describes VRE through three interrelated phases:

1. **ethnographic familiarisation / contextualisation**;
2. **video-recording of practice and selection/editing of footage**;
3. **reflexive sessions with participants/practitioners and researchers**.

### Important

These phases are:

- interrelated;
- iterative;
- not necessarily one-way.

A real VRE project can cycle:

familiarisation  
→ recording  
→ reflexive session  
→ changed question  
→ more observation  
→ more recording  
→ new reflexive session.

### Critical rule

A project with only:

video recording  
→ interview

is not automatically VRE.

---

# 6. Phase 1 — ethnographic familiarisation

Phase 1 commonly includes:

- observation;
- shadowing;
- fieldnotes;
- informal conversation;
- interviews;
- relationship-building;
- learning local work rhythms;
- identifying meaningful practices;
- mapping hierarchy/access;
- negotiating consent and filming boundaries.

### Scientific purpose

Familiarisation helps answer:

- What actually matters here?
- Which practices are routine but consequential?
- When and where is filming appropriate?
- Which interactions should not be filmed?
- Which tensions are visible only with contextual knowledge?
- Who may be vulnerable in later playback?

### Critical rule

The ethnographic phase is not just logistics before “real data collection.”

It creates the contextual knowledge needed to interpret video responsibly.

---

# 7. Focused ethnography overlap

D4's focused ethnography route overlaps strongly with VRE because VRE often uses:

- bounded clinical settings;
- concentrated observation;
- high recording intensity;
- team-based analysis.

But focused ethnography does not require:

- playback to practitioners;
- collective reflexive sessions;
- exnovation;
- practice redesign.

### Taxonomy rule

**VRE ≠ focused ethnography + camera.**

A VRE study can use a focused-ethnographic field strategy, but the reflexive architecture remains separately constitutive.

---

# 8. Phase 2 — in-situ video capture

VRE typically records:

- actual workplace practice;
- interaction;
- workflow;
- coordination;
- space/material use;
- body positioning;
- equipment interaction;
- interruptions;
- communication.

The camera is useful because practice includes details difficult to retain fully through fieldnotes.

### Critical rule

The aim is not cinematic completeness.

The aim is to create reflexive access to practice.

---

# 9. “Naturalistic” does not mean unaffected by filming

Participants may alter behaviour because:

- researcher is present;
- camera is visible;
- consent has been discussed.

VRE literature does not require pretending the camera is invisible.

### Project rule

Extract:

- camera placement;
- researcher position;
- participant adaptation;
- discussion of reactivity;
- how filming became part of the social setting.

### Critical interpretation

Reactivity can itself become part of reflexive inquiry.

---

# 10. The researcher is not a fly on the wall

VRE fieldwork recognizes the researcher as:

- socially present;
- negotiating access;
- selecting footage;
- facilitating discussion;
- affecting psychological safety;
- helping shape what becomes visible.

### Project rule

Researcher positionality must be explicit.

Especially relevant:

- insider/outsider professional identity;
- clinical background;
- authority;
- relationship to managers;
- relationship to trainees/students;
- ability to facilitate non-punitive discussion.

---

# 11. What gets filmed is negotiated

Current VRE guidance and protocols show that filming targets can be influenced by:

- participant priorities;
- researcher observation;
- research question;
- local feasibility;
- ethics;
- clinical risk;
- site champions.

### Strong architecture

Participants can help identify:

- important routines;
- puzzling processes;
- potential strengths;
- improvement opportunities.

### Critical rule

Researcher-selected filming alone does not necessarily invalidate VRE, but stronger collaboration requires transparency about:

> who decided what deserved to be filmed?

---

# 12. Phase 2.5 — clip selection/editing is methodological

Raw footage is usually reduced to short clips before reflexive sessions.

Selection can depend on:

- relevance to research question;
- everyday/typical practice;
- complexity;
- participant-identified interest;
- positive competence;
- unexpected moments;
- technical visibility;
- time limits;
- ethical safety.

### Critical rule

Editing is not neutral preprocessing.

It determines what participants later see and therefore what can become an object of collective reflexivity.

---

# 13. Clip selection is a power node

Researchers often have substantial control over:

- which footage is kept;
- which moments are excluded;
- sequence;
- clip duration;
- framing;
- which practitioners are shown.

### Strong practice

Current VRE protocols include:

- participant consultation;
- fast feedback;
- clip approval;
- senior/on-site contextual consultation;
- excluding footage that could damage relationships or expose vulnerabilities.

### Project extraction

Record:

1. who selected footage?;
2. who edited?;
3. participant input?;
4. participant veto?;
5. why clips were selected?;
6. whether “problem” clips were balanced with competence/ordinary practice?;
7. who approved sharing?

---

# 14. Phase 3 — reflexive sessions

The defining reflexive move is:

participants/practitioners  
→ watch footage of their own/local practice  
→ discuss what they see  
→ question assumptions  
→ reinterpret actions/context  
→ possibly identify new possibilities.

### Reflexive sessions can involve

- small groups;
- whole teams;
- one-to-one meetings;
- online sessions;
- interprofessional groups.

### Critical rule

Playback is not simply member checking.

Participants are not being asked only:

> “Is the researcher’s interpretation correct?”

They are being invited to generate new interpretation and possibilities through the footage.

---

# 15. Reflexivity ≠ reflection only

VRE literature distinguishes:

## Reflection

Thinking back on what happened.

## Reflexivity

Monitoring/questioning how:

- practice;
- context;
- relations;
- assumptions;
- one's own position

are being produced and can be altered.

### Project rule

A participant saying:

> “I should have done X”

may be reflective.

A VRE reflexive process asks more broadly:

- Why did this practice unfold this way?
- What relationships/material constraints shaped it?
- What does the video make visible that we normally miss?
- What else could be possible?

---

# 16. Four core principles

The current VRE methodology is organized around:

1. **exnovation**;
2. **collaboration**;
3. **reflexivity**;
4. **care**.

These principles guide:

- fieldwork;
- filming;
- clip selection;
- facilitation;
- interpretation;
- change.

### Critical rule

They are not just values listed in the Discussion.

A coherent VRE study should show how they altered decisions.

---

# 17. Exnovation

Exnovation is one of VRE's most distinctive concepts.

It means foregrounding:

- what is already happening;
- taken-for-granted accomplishment;
- hidden competence;
- local creativity;
- how teams make complex practice work.

It contrasts with improvement models that begin only from:

- error;
- deficit;
- non-compliance;
- external expert solution.

### Project shorthand

**exnovation = innovation-from-within via making existing practice visible.**

---

# 18. Exnovation is not positivity bias

Exnovation does not mean:

> only show good practice.

A VRE session can examine:

- breakdown;
- risk;
- contradiction;
- unsafe routine.

But it does so within the local ecology of practice and asks:

- what is already working?;
- how is complexity currently being managed?;
- what capacities exist here?;
- how could those capacities be redirected?

### Critical rule

Strengths focus should not erase harm or error.

---

# 19. Collaboration

VRE is explicitly participatory.

Collaboration can include participants in:

- choosing what to film;
- filming decisions;
- clip selection;
- co-analysis;
- reflexive sessions;
- redesign;
- dissemination;
- co-authorship.

### Critical rule

Collaboration is a gradient.

Do not infer full co-researcher status because participants attended a reflexive session.

Extract actual decision rights.

---

# 20. Reflexivity

Reflexivity applies to:

- practitioners;
- patients;
- researchers;
- facilitators.

Participants revisit practice.

Researchers must also inspect:

- what they selected;
- what they emphasized;
- how facilitation shaped discussion;
- how hierarchy changed what could be said.

### Critical rule

VRE researcher reflexivity is not optional background.

The methodology itself exposes research practice to scrutiny.

---

# 21. Care

Care is a methodological principle, not only generic research ethics.

Being:

- filmed;
- replayed;
- discussed by colleagues

can be personally and professionally exposing.

Care requires active attention to:

- psychological safety;
- blame;
- hierarchy;
- embarrassment;
- professional reputation;
- patient vulnerability;
- relational consequences.

### Critical rule

A technically valid clip can be methodologically inappropriate if showing it damages psychological safety enough to prevent genuine reflexivity.

---

# 22. Care can conflict with analytic richness

Example:

A trainee may prefer an individual reflexive interview rather than viewing footage with a supervisor.

This may improve:

- safety;
- willingness to speak.

But it may reduce:

- multiple perspectives;
- team-level dialogue;
- collective co-analysis.

### Project rule

Store these trade-offs rather than pretending one format is universally superior.

---

# 23. Potentiation

Iedema describes VRE as a **potentiation technology**.

The goal is not only to document practice.

The video-reflexive process can increase participants' potential to:

- see practice differently;
- loosen attachment to taken-for-granted arrangements;
- consider alternatives;
- act differently.

### Critical rule

Potentiation ≠ proven effectiveness.

It is a methodological theory about expanding action potential.

---

# 24. Liminalisation

Visual feedback can function as a kind of self-irritant.

Participants see themselves/practice from a slightly unfamiliar position.

This can create **liminalisation**:

- temporary distance from settled identity/practice;
- uncertainty about what once felt obvious;
- openness to alternative ways of acting.

### Project route

VRE  
+ visual feedback  
→ liminalisation  
→ possible movement  
→ possible potentiation.

---

# 25. “Situated distance”

VRE creates a useful tension:

- the footage is familiar because it is participants' own practice;
- it is strange because it is seen from an externalized recorded perspective.

This can create **situated distance**.

### Scientific value

The familiar/unfamiliar combination helps make tacit practice visible without fully decontextualizing it.

---

# 26. Engagement and movement as quality concepts

Iedema's quality paper proposes two relational indicators:

1. **engagement**;
2. **movement**.

## Engagement

How substantially researchers/participants invest in and participate in the VRE process.

## Movement

Whether the inquiry produces movement in:

- understanding;
- positioning;
- relationships;
- possibilities;
- action potential.

### Critical caveat

These are VRE-specific relational quality concepts.

They are not universal substitutes for all qualitative quality standards.

---

# 27. Engagement ≠ attendance

A clinician present in a reflexive session may still be:

- silent due to hierarchy;
- defensive;
- disengaged;
- afraid of blame.

### Project rule

Extract:

- who spoke?;
- whose interpretations shaped discussion?;
- who was absent?;
- who declined footage sharing?;
- how facilitator handled silence/disagreement?

---

# 28. Movement ≠ measured practice improvement automatically

Movement can be:

- conceptual;
- relational;
- affective;
- practical.

A team may rethink a routine without yet demonstrating:

- lower error rates;
- improved patient outcomes;
- sustained behaviour change.

### Critical rule

Separate:

`reflexive/potentiating movement`

from:

`verified implementation or clinical outcome improvement`.

---

# 29. VRE as research and improvement methodology

Current literature explicitly treats VRE as:

- research methodology;
- practice-improvement methodology;
- collaborative visual methodology.

It can produce academic knowledge while also enabling local practice change.

### Project rule

Store separately:

1. research question;
2. improvement aim;
3. reflexive learning outcome;
4. implemented practice change;
5. measured patient/service outcome.

Do not collapse all into one “impact” field.

---

# 30. Practice optimisation is local and emergent

VRE does not generally begin by importing a predefined “best practice” solution.

Instead it can support:

- local noticing;
- local redesign;
- adaptation;
- practice-specific experimentation.

### Critical rule

This does not mean external evidence/guidelines are irrelevant.

A VRE project can later compare local insight with:

- safety guidance;
- professional standards;
- clinical evidence.

But its distinctive move is to start from situated practice.

---

# 31. VRE vs conventional ethnography

## Conventional ethnography

Typically aims to understand:

- culture;
- social world;
- practices;
- meanings;
- relationships.

Participants need not become co-analysts.

## VRE

Adds:

- recorded practice shown back;
- practitioner co-analysis;
- reflexive intervention into practice;
- exnovation;
- possible optimisation/change.

### Critical rule

**ethnography + video ≠ VRE.**

---

# 32. VRE vs video ethnography

Video ethnography can use video to:

- document interaction;
- analyze embodied practice;
- preserve multimodal detail.

Researcher may analyze footage without showing it back.

### VRE

Requires video to function reflexively:

footage  
→ participants/practitioners  
→ collaborative interpretation  
→ possible re-imagination/change.

### Critical rule

The difference is not the camera.

The difference is the **reflexive participant-practice loop**.

---

# 33. VRE vs video elicitation

Video elicitation:

researcher shows video  
→ participant comments.

This can be done with:

- researcher-recorded material;
- external video;
- past events.

### VRE

Usually adds:

- ethnographic familiarisation;
- in-situ local practice;
- iterative collaboration;
- exnovation;
- collective practice relation;
- possible improvement.

### Rule

One video-elicitation interview ≠ VRE automatically.

---

# 34. VRE vs stimulated recall / interpersonal process recall

Stimulated recall often asks a participant to revisit a recorded episode to reconstruct:

- thoughts;
- intentions;
- decision-making;
- perceptions.

VRE can include similar replay but has a different target.

### VRE target

Not primarily:

> “What were you thinking at second 42?”

Rather:

> “What does this footage allow us to see about how practice is organized, accomplished and potentially changed?”

### Critical rule

Individual cognitive reconstruction ≠ collaborative practice reflexivity.

---

# 35. VRE vs reflective practice

Reflective practice may use:

- journals;
- supervision;
- video feedback;
- debrief.

### VRE adds

- ethnographic contextualisation;
- research process;
- systematic video selection;
- collective co-analysis;
- exnovation;
- practice-theory orientation;
- methodological principles.

### Rule

Self-review of training footage is not VRE unless broader architecture is present.

---

# 36. VRE vs Participatory Video

D36 Participatory Video and VRE share:

- visual media;
- participation;
- reflexivity;
- collaborative discussion;
- potential action.

But the canonical production architecture differs.

## Participatory Video

Participants often become:

- camera operators;
- directors;
- editors;
- producers;
- message owners.

Aim often includes:

- self-representation;
- community dialogue;
- advocacy.

## VRE

Researchers may retain more technical filming/editing work.

Core target is:

- existing practice;
- local complexity;
- replay to practitioners;
- exnovation;
- reflexive improvement.

### Critical rule

Participant media ownership is constitutive for strong PV.

It is **not** a mandatory defining criterion of VRE.

---

# 37. VRE vs quality-improvement meeting

A quality-improvement meeting may:

- identify problems;
- review incidents;
- plan interventions.

VRE differs because:

- practice is ethnographically contextualized;
- actual practice is videoed;
- participants confront situated evidence of their own work;
- exnovation/reflexivity guide interpretation.

### Critical rule

Showing a training video in a QI meeting does not create VRE.

---

# 38. VRE vs audit / compliance observation

Audit often asks:

> Did staff follow standard X?

VRE asks more open questions:

- What is actually happening here?
- How is work being accomplished under real constraints?
- What local competence is invisible?
- What makes the protocol difficult or workable?
- What alternatives emerge from practitioners' own analysis?

### Critical rule

VRE can inform compliance improvement but should not be reduced to surveillance.

---

# 39. Surveillance risk

Video in workplaces can easily feel like:

- monitoring;
- performance assessment;
- disciplinary evidence.

This can destroy:

- trust;
- care;
- collaboration;
- reflexivity.

### Project extraction

Record:

- who can access footage?;
- can managers use it punitively?;
- is footage excluded from HR/disciplinary process?;
- are supervisors present?;
- was confidentiality within reflexive sessions addressed?;
- could participation affect evaluation?

---

# 40. Hierarchy

VRE is especially sensitive to:

- consultant/junior doctor;
- supervisor/student;
- manager/staff;
- clinician/patient;
- educator/trainee.

### Current guidance

If hierarchy inhibits discussion, reflexive sessions may be:

- separated;
- individually conducted;
- differently facilitated.

### Critical rule

A mixed-hierarchy group is not automatically more “collaborative.”

---

# 41. Facilitation

The facilitator can shape whether a reflexive session becomes:

- blame;
- self-defence;
- learning;
- team inquiry;
- process analysis.

Strong facilitation helps keep attention on:

- practice;
- system;
- interaction;
- context;

rather than personal fault.

### Project fields

- facilitator identity;
- relationship to participants;
- facilitation training;
- prompts;
- conflict handling;
- hierarchy management;
- process vs individual framing.

---

# 42. Consent is ongoing

Current VRE protocols repeatedly use:

- initial written consent;
- verbal consent before observation/filming;
- post-filming permission;
- consent before reflexive-session use;
- sometimes separate dissemination release.

### Critical rule

Consent to participate at baseline ≠ permanent permission for every future use of identifiable video.

---

# 43. Consent after filming matters

A participant may agree to be filmed but later object to:

- a specific clip;
- group viewing;
- teaching use;
- conference presentation;
- publication.

### Project rule

Extract clip-level or use-level approval where reported.

Especially in clinical settings, this is stronger than one blanket research-consent field.

---

# 44. Non-consenting bystanders

Clinical video can capture:

- staff passing through;
- patients;
- visitors;
- names;
- screens;
- documents;
- distinctive voices.

Current protocols may:

- blur faces;
- delete voices;
- exclude footage;
- stop recording;
- reframe camera.

### Critical rule

Video introduces third-party exposure risk beyond ordinary observation.

---

# 45. Identifiability

Video often reveals identity through:

- face;
- voice;
- gait;
- uniform;
- ward;
- role;
- environment.

Pseudonyms do not solve this.

### Project rule

Store:

- image masking?;
- voice masking?;
- clip restrictions?;
- dissemination audience?;
- participant preference to be identified?;
- future reuse restrictions?

---

# 46. Clinical video is especially sensitive

Footage can reveal:

- patient condition;
- staff action;
- medication;
- room/location;
- errors;
- protected health information.

### Critical rule

Raw footage governance should be treated as a separate methodological/ethical object.

Fields:

- storage;
- access;
- encryption;
- retention;
- deletion;
- secondary use;
- teaching use;
- publication use.

---

# 47. Reflexive-session data are new data

A VRE project may produce:

1. ethnographic observations;
2. fieldnotes;
3. interviews;
4. raw video;
5. edited clips;
6. reflexive-session discussion;
7. researcher memos;
8. later practice-change observations.

### Critical rule

Reflexive-session interpretation is not merely annotation of the original footage.

It is a new layer of co-generated empirical material.

# 48. Analysis can occur at multiple levels

Possible analytic levels include:

- researcher analysis of fieldnotes/video;
- participant interpretation of clips;
- analysis of reflexive-session transcripts;
- comparison across sessions;
- analysis of practice change.

Current dementia VRE work explicitly used multi-level analysis.

### Project rule

Extract each level separately.

Do not store only:

`analysis = thematic analysis`

if participant reflexive co-analysis is also central.

# 49. Formal thematic analysis is optional, not constitutive

Some VRE studies use:

- thematic analysis;
- discourse/interaction analysis;
- multimodal analysis;
- practice-theory interpretation.

VRE is not defined by one final analytic technique.

### Constitutive architecture

What defines it more strongly is:

- situated video;
- reflexive participant viewing;
- collaborative sense-making;
- exnovation/care/reflexivity/collaboration.

---

# 50. Practice-change loop

A strong VRE project can feed reflexive insights into:

- altered workflow;
- redesigned handover;
- changed communication;
- new team agreement;
- altered training;
- changed environment.

### Critical rule

Proposed change ≠ implemented change.

Implemented change ≠ sustained change.

Sustained change ≠ improved patient outcome.

Store separately.

- prevalence across all physiotherapy students;

# 57. VRE can be research without a successful practice change

A project may generate strong knowledge but fail to produce:

- implementation;
- redesign;
- sustained change.

That does not automatically invalidate it as VRE.

### Why

VRE also has a research function:

- illuminating practice;
- revealing complexity;
- generating participant-practitioner interpretation.

### But

If a paper claims improvement impact, improvement must be demonstrated separately.

---

# 58. VRE can be improvement without causal evaluation

A team may change practice after reflexive sessions.

But without an appropriate causal/evaluation design, one cannot conclude:

> VRE caused the improved clinical outcome.

### Rule

Store:

`VRE process / local improvement`

separately from:

`causal effect of VRE`.

# 59. What VRE can establish

Depending on implementation, VRE can support contextual claims about:

- situated practice;
- tacit routines;
- interactional complexity;
- unnoticed strengths;
- participant/practitioner interpretations;
- local constraints;
- reflexive learning;
- professional/team re-positioning;
- locally generated improvement possibilities;
- changes in how participants understand practice;
- documented local practice redesign.

---

# 60. What VRE cannot establish by itself

VRE alone does not establish:

- population prevalence;
- incidence;
- average treatment effect;
- comparative efficacy;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism;
- causal mediation;
- general causal effectiveness of VRE as an intervention.

### Critical rule

Contextual practice knowledge ≠ epidemiologic/causal proof.

# 61. Transferability

VRE findings are usually:

- local;
- practice-situated;
- organization-specific.

Useful transfer may concern:

- mechanisms of noticing;
- recurring practice tensions;
- facilitation lessons;
- design principles;
- questions to ask elsewhere.

### Critical rule

A VRE insight from one ICU/ward/programme should not be represented as universal workflow truth without further evidence.

2. Calling any video feedback session “VRE.”

4. Using decontextualized training footage only.
5. Researcher-only interpretation of footage.
6. Playback used only for member checking.
7. Playback used only for performance scoring.

9. Exnovation reduced to “positive feedback.”

11. Collaboration claimed from attendance alone.
