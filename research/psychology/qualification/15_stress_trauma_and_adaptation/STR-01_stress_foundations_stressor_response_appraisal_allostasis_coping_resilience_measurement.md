# STR-01 — Stress foundations: stressor, stress response, appraisal, acute/chronic stress, allostasis, coping, resilience, and measurement boundaries

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public stress-science evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

AFF established emotion generation, regulation, and temporal dynamics. STR begins with a different dependency:

**What exactly is "stress," what produces it, how is it measured, when is a stress response adaptive versus costly, and what can be inferred about coping or resilience?**

High-risk errors to prevent:
- "stressor = stress";
- "feeling stressed = high cortisol";
- "high cortisol = harmful stress";
- "low cortisol = resilience";
- "acute stress = mild stress; chronic stress = severe stress";
- "stress response = pathology";
- "negative emotion = stress";
- "high arousal = stress";
- "allostatic load = one objective stress score";
- "coping = emotion regulation";
- "avoidance = always bad coping";
- "resilience = a fixed trait";
- "absence of symptoms = proven resilience without measuring adversity";
- "quick recovery = no meaningful stress response";
- "one laboratory stress task identifies real-world chronic stress";
- "one biomarker or questionnaire reveals a person's stress mechanism".

## Core rule

**Stress is a transactional, multilevel process involving environmental or internal demands (stressors), appraisal and context, multiple psychological and biological response systems, coping/adaptation, and recovery over time. Stressor exposure, subjective stress, physiological response, and downstream health effects are related but noninterchangeable.**

No single measure is the ground truth of "stress."

## 1. Stressor versus stress response

A **stressor** is an event, condition, demand, or anticipated challenge capable of requiring adaptation.

A **stress response** is the set of psychological, behavioral, autonomic, endocrine, immune, cognitive, or motivational changes occurring in relation to the stressor.

These are not the same object.

Examples of stressor properties:
- intensity;
- duration;
- predictability;
- controllability;
- novelty;
- social-evaluative threat;
- physical danger;
- resource demand;
- recurrence.

Examples of response properties:
- subjective distress;
- vigilance;
- heart-rate change;
- blood-pressure change;
- cortisol response;
- behavior;
- inflammatory changes;
- recovery trajectory.

## 2. Stress is not simply negative emotion

Stress can involve:
- fear/anxiety;
- anger/frustration;
- sadness;
- uncertainty;
- concentration;
- action readiness;
- mixed affect.

But affective valence is not a sufficient stress definition.

A person may experience strong negative emotion without a stressor-demand process, and a stress response can include physiological activation with modest reported negative affect.

Cross-reference AFF: emotion, arousal, and stress are overlapping but distinct targets.

## 3. Stress is not simply arousal

NIMH RDoC defines arousal as a continuum of sensitivity/activation that is distinct from motivation and valence.

Stress can alter arousal, but:
- arousal occurs during exercise, excitement, novelty, wakefulness, and other states;
- stress can include fatigue or blunted activation;
- different stress-response systems can move on different trajectories.

Thus:
**arousal ≠ stress**.

## 4. RDoC does not contain one unitary "stress" construct

Current NIMH RDoC distributes relevant functions across:
- Acute Threat ("Fear");
- Potential Threat ("Anxiety");
- Sustained Threat;
- Loss;
- Frustrative Nonreward;
- Arousal/Regulatory Systems;
- cognitive and social domains.

This distribution is useful because "stress" can involve different threats, time horizons, meanings, and systems.

Do not treat RDoC as claiming all stress belongs to one Negative Valence mechanism.

## 5. Acute versus chronic stress

**Acute stress** refers to responses to a relatively time-limited challenge or episode.

**Chronic stress** refers to sustained, recurrent, or persistent stressor exposure and/or stress-response burden over longer periods.

The distinction concerns temporal pattern and exposure history, not merely intensity.

A brief severe event can be acute.
A low-intensity recurring demand can become chronic.

A single cross-sectional distress rating cannot establish chronic stress exposure.

## 6. Repeated acute stress versus chronic stress

Repeated acute events can create cumulative burden, but "many acute events" and "one continuous chronic stressor" need not have identical consequences.

Relevant features include:
- frequency;
- recovery between episodes;
- habituation or sensitization;
- predictability;
- cumulative exposure;
- changes in baseline state;
- social/environmental resources.

Researchers should report the exposure pattern rather than using "chronic stress" as a vague severity label.

## 7. Stress appraisal

Transactional stress models emphasize that the meaning of a demand depends partly on appraisal.

Common appraisal dimensions include:
- relevance/importance;
- threat or harm;
- challenge/opportunity;
- controllability;
- predictability;
- coping resources;
- available support;
- uncertainty.

Appraisal is not necessarily slow conscious verbal reasoning.

AFF-01's appraisal boundary applies: appraisal is a theoretical/operational construct, not any thought occurring before distress.

## 8. Demands and resources

In challenge/threat frameworks:
- **challenge** generally refers to perceived coping resources meeting or exceeding perceived demands;
- **threat** generally refers to demands exceeding perceived resources.

A 2025 updated meta-analysis of 62 studies (N = 7,418) found challenge states were associated with better performance than threat states on average, with small effects across multiple domains.

This supports the usefulness of demand/resource appraisal.

It does not prove:
- challenge is always healthy;
- threat appraisal uniquely causes poor performance;
- every stress response can be classified with one demand/resource score.

## 9. Controllability

Perceived and objective control can alter appraisal, coping selection, learning, and stress-response patterns.

But:
- perceived control can be inaccurate;
- objective control can exist without being recognized;
- high perceived control may be unhelpful if the outcome is genuinely uncontrollable;
- control can differ across outcome, timing, and action.

A 2026 preregistered study found the affective benefits of perceived control varied with acute-stress condition and feedback context.

Therefore **control is a moderator/process variable, not an unconditional resilience resource**.

## 10. Stress response systems

Major systems commonly measured include:
- autonomic nervous system (ANS);
- hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis;
- immune/inflammatory responses;
- metabolic/cardiovascular changes;
- subjective experience;
- cognition/attention;
- behavior.

These systems interact but do not move in perfect synchrony.

A "stress response" should identify which system and timescale are meant.

## 11. Sympathetic and parasympathetic responses

Acute challenge can involve:
- sympathetic activation;
- parasympathetic withdrawal/re-engagement;
- cardiovascular changes;
- peripheral vascular responses.

Heart rate and HRV are not interchangeable measures.

HRV depends on:
- respiration;
- posture;
- physical activity;
- measurement duration;
- circadian context;
- medication and health variables.

A lower HRV value is not by itself proof of psychological stress.

## 12. HPA-axis response

The HPA axis can produce changes in ACTH and cortisol in relation to stressors.

Cortisol:
- has strong circadian regulation;
- varies with awakening;
- changes with food, exercise, illness, medication, sleep, and other factors;
- often peaks later than rapid autonomic changes after acute stress.

Thus cortisol sampling time is part of the construct.

A single cortisol value cannot establish acute or chronic psychological stress.

## 13. Cortisol awakening response is not generic stress reactivity

The cortisol awakening response (CAR) is a regulated rise in cortisol during roughly the first 30–45 minutes after awakening.

A 2025 Endocrine Reviews synthesis treats CAR as a functionally regulated morning process involving circadian/environmental/neurocognitive influences.

CAR is therefore **not interchangeable with laboratory cortisol reactivity**.

A 2024 meta-analysis found no significant overall associations of CAR, diurnal slope, or total output with TSST cortisol reactivity.

This directly blocks a one-dimensional "high cortisol reactor" trait inference.

## 14. TSST: standardized acute psychosocial stress

The Trier Social Stress Test (TSST) is a widely used laboratory paradigm involving social-evaluative and uncontrollable performance elements.

A 2025 meta-analysis synthesized:
- 171 studies;
- 8,452 healthy adults.

Average physiological and psychological responses were both substantial, but response channels differed:
- physiological effects were larger overall than psychological effects;
- ANS effects exceeded HPA effects on average;
- correlations across physiological and psychological channels were generally weak/non-significant.

Therefore:
**TSST response is multichannel, not one latent stress intensity score.**

## 15. Laboratory stress versus real-world stress

Laboratory paradigms provide:
- standardized timing;
- controlled exposure;
- repeated biomarkers;
- stronger causal manipulation.

They usually provide limited:
- duration;
- ecological complexity;
- chronic exposure;
- social history;
- real stakes.

Daily-life designs provide ecological context but weaker experimental control.

Neither should automatically replace the other.

## 16. Stress recovery is system-specific

Objective stressor offset does not imply all systems begin or complete recovery simultaneously.

A 2026 review emphasizes:
- cardiovascular activation may already decline while cortisol is still rising;
- threat-related cognition may persist after the task ends;
- vagal, HPA, cognitive, and immune processes have distinct timing.

Therefore:
**stressor ended ≠ organism fully recovered**.

## 17. Reactivity versus recovery

Separate:
- baseline;
- response amplitude/reactivity;
- peak timing;
- recovery slope/time;
- post-event undershoot/overshoot;
- later recurrence.

Two people can show the same peak but different recovery.

A high peak followed by efficient recovery can have a different interpretation than moderate activation that remains elevated.

## 18. Adaptiveness is context- and state-dependent

The stress response evolved to support adaptation under challenge.

Acute stress can:
- mobilize energy;
- prioritize information;
- alter memory;
- increase vigilance;
- change action selection.

A 2024 review argues that acute stress responses can drive adaptive neuroplasticity and resilience mechanisms.

A 2025 review emphasizes that whether stress responses are adaptive or maladaptive depends on context, task, organism state, and stress history.

Thus:
**stress response ≠ damage by definition**.

## 19. Recovery is not identical to adaptation

**Recovery**: movement toward a prior/reference state after perturbation.

**Adaptation**: functional adjustment to current or future conditions.

An organism can adapt by changing its baseline, strategy, or behavior rather than returning exactly to the old state.

Therefore "did not return to baseline" is not automatically maladaptive.

## 20. Homeostasis

Homeostasis refers to regulation that maintains important physiological variables within viable ranges.

It does not imply every measured variable stays constant.

Homeostatic regulation often requires active responses to environmental change.

## 21. Allostasis

Allostasis is commonly summarized as **stability through change**: adaptive adjustments in mediators and behavior that meet predicted or actual demands.

Allostasis complements rather than simply replaces homeostasis.

McEwen's classic framework describes stress mediators as beneficial when activated appropriately and efficiently terminated, but potentially costly under repeated, prolonged, dysregulated, or inefficient activation.

## 22. Allostatic load

Allostatic load (AL) is a conceptual framework for cumulative multisystem physiological burden associated with repeated/chronic adaptation demands.

Common AL indices combine biomarkers across systems such as:
- cardiovascular;
- metabolic;
- inflammatory;
- neuroendocrine.

But there is no universally standardized AL index.

## 23. Allostatic load measurement heterogeneity

A 2026 integrative review of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic research found:
- AL indices ranging from 5 to 14 biomarkers;
- cardiometabolic markers universally represented;
- neuroendocrine mediators present in only 5 of 15 included studies;
- incompatible scoring approaches;
- inconsistent medication adjustment.

The authors warn many indices can function more like cardiometabolic risk scores than full stress-response measures.

**AL score ≠ direct objective reading of cumulative psychological stress.**

## 24. Allostatic load and multimorbidity overlap

A 2026 systematic review of 428 studies plus analyses across four population datasets found substantial heterogeneity in AL operationalization and moderate-to-high overlap with multimorbidity, especially cardiometabolic phenotypes.

Interpretation:
- AL can summarize multisystem dysregulation;
- many current implementations blur subclinical and clinical disease;
- longitudinal/theoretically coherent measurement is needed.

Do not infer mechanism from a high composite alone.

## 25. Chronic stress measurement is conceptually inconsistent

A 2025/2026 integrative scoping review of 45 reviews covering more than 2,000 studies found chronic stress definitions varied between:
- stressors/exposures;
- subjective stress responses;
- biomarkers;
- downstream outcomes such as burnout.

This is a major construct-validity problem.

Canonical rule:
**Define whether "chronic stress" means exposure, perceived appraisal, biological dysregulation, or downstream consequence.**

## 26. Stress biomarkers are channel-specific

A 2026 conceptual review covers:
- cortisol/DHEA;
- HRV/alpha-amylase;
- cytokines/CRP;
- telomeres;
- DNA methylation;
- multi-omics.

Each has different:
- temporal resolution;
- specificity;
- confounds;
- sample handling;
- biological meaning.

There is no universal biomarker of stress.

## 27. Biomarker abnormality is not unique stress causality

Inflammation, cortisol changes, HRV changes, or metabolic changes can have causes unrelated to psychological stress.

Required alternatives may include:
- infection;
- sleep disruption;
- physical activity;
- medication;
- endocrine/metabolic disease;
- diet;
- age;
- circadian timing.

Stress can contribute without being the unique cause.

## 28. Subjective stress is not inferior by definition

Self-report directly measures perceived demand/distress unavailable to a biomarker.

Its limitations include:
- memory;
- language;
- response style;
- context;
- social desirability;
- scale interpretation.

Biomarkers are not automatically more objective in the sense of being more valid for the psychological construct.

Use measurement matched to the question.

## 29. Perceived stress versus stressor exposure

Two people can report different perceived stress under similar externally coded demands because of:
- appraisal;
- control;
- resources;
- prior exposure;
- goals;
- uncertainty;
- social support.

Conversely, low perceived stress does not prove low exposure or low biological response.

Measure exposure and perception separately when possible.

## 30. Daily-life stress measurement

A 2026 scoping review of smartphone-based self-assessment in healthy adults found wide methodological heterogeneity and only a minority of studies reporting formal validity/reliability evidence.

A 2025 systematic review of intensive longitudinal stress/health-behavior research found:
- 100 included studies;
- 18,122 participants;
- self-reported stress measures dominated;
- physiological measures were uncommon;
- associations with health behaviors were heterogeneous.

EMA location/context does not automatically establish psychometric validity.

## 31. Coping

Coping broadly refers to cognitive and behavioral efforts/processes for managing demands, resources, consequences, or one's responses to stressors.

Common families include:
- problem-focused/action coping;
- emotion-focused coping;
- meaning-focused coping;
- support seeking;
- avoidance/disengagement;
- acceptance;
- planning;
- cognitive reframing.

Taxonomies vary substantially.

## 32. Coping versus emotion regulation

A 2024 scoping review found major conceptual and measurement overlap between coping and emotion regulation.

Useful working boundary:
- **emotion regulation** targets emotion generation/trajectory;
- **coping** can target the stressor, practical demands/resources, emotional response, or broader adaptation.

The frameworks overlap in strategies such as reappraisal, acceptance, avoidance, and suppression.

Do not infer distinct mechanisms merely because two questionnaires use different framework names.

## 33. Problem-focused coping is conditional

Changing the stressor can be useful when:
- the situation is controllable;
- the action is feasible;
- accurate information exists;
- costs are acceptable.

It can be futile or harmful when the stressor is genuinely uncontrollable or action has high costs.

No coping strategy is universally adaptive independent of controllability.

## 34. Avoidance is not automatically maladaptive

Avoidance can:
- prevent real danger;
- interrupt escalation;
- conserve resources;
- provide short-term recovery.

It can also:
- block corrective learning;
- narrow behavior;
- maintain feared predictions;
- postpone solvable problems.

Interpret avoidance using:
- objective risk;
- controllability;
- duration;
- function;
- opportunity cost;
- long-term consequence.

## 35. Coping effectiveness needs a named outcome

Possible outcomes:
- reduced distress;
- solved external problem;
- preserved safety;
- improved performance;
- faster physiological recovery;
- maintained relationships;
- long-term functioning.

A strategy can improve one outcome and worsen another.

Thus:
**coping used ≠ coping effective**.

## 36. Resilience requires adversity/stressor exposure

Resilience is meaningful only relative to a challenge/adversity/stressor.

Without measuring exposure, low symptom levels cannot distinguish:
- resilience under high adversity;
- ordinary functioning under low adversity.

Modern outcome-based resilience frameworks therefore emphasize both:
- stressor exposure;
- mental-health/function outcome.

## 37. Resilience as outcome, process, or capacity

Literature uses "resilience" for:
- trait/capacity;
- dynamic process;
- observed outcome;
- recovery trajectory.

These meanings must be separated.

A 2024/2025 dynamic process framework emphasizes stress exposure, reserve capacity, adaptation, and consequences.

A 2026 SSRO scaffold similarly recommends explicitly specifying:
- Stressor;
- System;
- Resilience resources/processes;
- Outcomes.

## 38. Outcome-based resilience

A common outcome definition is **maintenance or relatively rapid recovery of mental health/function despite substantial stressor exposure**.

A 2024 American Psychologist paper emphasizes that resilience trajectories require background stressor exposure to be measured because otherwise apparent "resilience" may simply reflect lower total adversity.

Therefore:
**good outcome alone ≠ resilience**.

## 39. Resilience factor versus resilience mechanism

A 2024 review distinguishes:
- **resilience factor**: a variable associated with better outcomes under stress and potentially modifiable/stable;
- **resilience mechanism**: a proximal process through which adaptation occurs.

Social support may predict resilience without being the whole proximal mechanism.

Do not convert a predictor into an established causal mechanism.

## 40. Resilience is not absence of stress response

A resilient outcome can follow:
- substantial acute response;
- effective recovery;
- adaptive learning;
- strategy change;
- support use.

The 2024 review on stress neuroplasticity explicitly treats the acute stress response as potentially contributing to resilience.

Thus "did not react" is not the only resilient pattern.

## 41. Resilience versus recovery

Recovery is one possible resilience trajectory.

Other resilience-compatible outcomes may include:
- stable functioning despite adversity;
- rapid recovery after temporary disruption;
- adaptation to persistent demands.

Claims of "growth" or "thriving" require their own outcome evidence and should not be assumed from recovery.

## 42. Daily resilience

A 2025 systematic review of 36 experience-sampling studies found daily resilience operationalized either as:
- self-reported ability to recover;
- affective recovery following daily stressors.

These are not identical measures.

Daily recovery dynamics should not automatically be generalized to resilience after major adversity.

## 43. Positive appraisal and resilience

A 2025 Nature Communications study in two observational cohorts (N=132; N=1,034) found positive appraisal style prospectively associated with resilient outcomes.

Exploratory outcomes from an RCT in distressed healthcare workers suggested appraisal style was modifiable and statistically mediated resilience improvements.

A 2026 Trends in Cognitive Sciences review highlights positive appraisal style as a candidate resilience factor/mechanism.

These findings are promising but do not establish:
- positivity should ignore real danger;
- all resilience is appraisal;
- unrealistic optimism is always adaptive;
- one appraisal intervention generalizes to all stressors/populations.

## 44. Resilience is context- and system-specific

A person/system can function well under one type of adversity and poorly under another.

Resources can include:
- biological reserve;
- skills;
- social support;
- financial/material resources;
- environmental safety;
- meaning;
- control;
- institutional support.

Avoid labeling a person globally "resilient" from one outcome.

## 45. Stress history changes later response

Prior stress exposure can produce:
- habituation;
- sensitization;
- learning;
- altered appraisal;
- changed coping;
- metaplasticity.

A 2025 review emphasizes recent and distant stress history as a determinant of later response.

Therefore the same objective task can evoke different responses after different histories without implying measurement error.

## 46. Trauma boundary

"Stressful" and "traumatic" are not synonyms.

Trauma-specific research typically concerns exposures of particular severity/type and post-traumatic processes.

A high perceived-stress score or strong cortisol response does not establish trauma exposure or a trauma-related disorder.

Detailed trauma definitions, post-traumatic adaptation, and trauma-measurement issues are deferred to a later STR block/CLN interface.

## 47. Burnout boundary

Burnout is a work-related syndrome/construct, not a direct synonym for chronic stress or allostatic load.

The chronic-stress measurement review found studies sometimes use burnout as a downstream consequence or proxy, illustrating construct contamination.

Do not infer biological chronic stress from a burnout score alone.

## 48. Stress and performance

Stress effects on performance depend on:
- task;
- timing;
- appraisal;
- resources;
- arousal;
- learning;
- complexity;
- stress history.

The 2025 challenge/threat meta-analysis supports better average performance under challenge versus threat, but small average effects and model constraints matter.

Avoid universal inverted-U claims without task-specific evidence.

## 49. Stress can modify learning and memory

Acute stress can change:
- attention;
- encoding;
- retrieval;
- consolidation;
- habit/control balance.

Direction depends on:
- timing relative to learning;
- stressor type;
- glucocorticoid/catecholamine timing;
- task;
- emotional relevance.

Detailed mechanisms belong at the LEA/NEU boundary.

Do not interpret every memory failure under stress as permanent damage.

## 50. Stress can modify motivation and emotion without replacing them

Stress can alter:
- reward valuation;
- effort allocation;
- threat sensitivity;
- emotion regulation;
- goal selection.

But:
**stress ≠ motivation deficit**
and
**stress ≠ emotion**.

Use MOT and AFF constructs rather than relabeling all changes as "stress."

## 51. Multi-system measurement principle

A strong stress study can combine:
- objective/structured stressor exposure;
- subjective appraisal;
- affect;
- autonomic measure;
- endocrine measure;
- behavioral response;
- recovery;
- contextual covariates.

Multiple channels improve coverage but do not need to correlate strongly.

Divergence can be informative.

## 52. Timing principle

Stress measures have different kinetics:
- heart rate: rapid;
- catecholaminergic/autonomic markers: rapid;
- cortisol: delayed relative to immediate autonomic changes;
- inflammatory markers: often slower;
- allostatic-load indices: cumulative/static summaries rather than immediate responses.

Sampling windows must match the hypothesized process.

## 53. Baseline principle

Reactivity depends on baseline.

A high post-stressor value may reflect:
- high baseline;
- large response;
- delayed recovery;
- measurement time.

Report both absolute level and change/trajectory where appropriate.

## 54. Stress Measurement and Inference Gate — 56 questions

### A. Stressor/exposure
1. What exact stressor or demand is being studied?
2. Is it objective, self-reported, anticipated, or experimentally induced?
3. What is its duration?
4. What is its frequency/recurrence?
5. Is it predictable?
6. Is it objectively/perceived controllable?
7. Is social-evaluative threat involved?
8. Is prior/background stressor exposure measured?

### B. Appraisal/context
9. Is relevance/importance measured?
10. Are demand and coping resources separated?
11. Is challenge versus threat operationalized?
12. Is perceived control distinguished from objective control?
13. Are uncertainty and probability specified?
14. Are social/material resources measured?
15. Could appraisal differ despite the same objective stressor?

### C. Response systems
16. Is subjective stress measured?
17. Is negative affect separated from stress?
18. Is arousal separated from stress?
19. Is autonomic response measured?
20. Is HPA response measured?
21. Is immune/inflammatory response relevant?
22. Are behavior and cognition measured?
23. Are system-specific time courses considered?
24. Is baseline available?
25. Is recovery measured?

### D. Cortisol/biomarkers
26. Is collection time/circadian phase controlled?
27. Is awakening timing relevant?
28. Are medication, sleep, food, exercise, illness confounds considered?
29. Is a single biomarker being treated as "stress itself"?
30. Is acute reactivity separated from diurnal function?
31. Are biomarker assay/sample procedures valid?
32. Does the biomarker measure acute or cumulative processes?

### E. Chronic stress/allostasis
33. Does chronic stress mean exposure, appraisal, biological dysregulation, or outcome?
34. Is repeated acute exposure separated from continuous chronic exposure?
35. Is recovery between stressors known?
36. Is allostasis distinguished from homeostasis?
37. Is allostatic load operationalization specified?
38. Which systems/biomarkers are in the AL index?
39. Is medication/clinical disease contaminating the index?
40. Is AL being mistaken for direct psychological-stress measurement?

### F. Coping/resilience
41. Is coping target specified: stressor, emotion, meaning, resources?
42. Is coping separated from emotion regulation?
43. Is strategy effectiveness tied to a named outcome?
44. Is controllability considered before judging coping?
45. Is resilience exposure-qualified?
46. Is resilience defined as capacity, process, or outcome?
47. Is recovery distinguished from resilience?
48. Are resilience factors distinguished from mechanisms?
49. Could good outcome simply reflect lower adversity?
50. Is a global resilience trait inferred from one context?

### G. Causal/inference limits
51. Is laboratory stress being generalized to chronic real-world stress?
52. Is cross-channel divergence treated as informative rather than error?
53. Is correlation being treated as causal stress mechanism?
54. Is stress being used as a placeholder for emotion/motivation/diagnosis?
55. Which competing mechanism or confound remains plausible?
56. What is the narrowest conclusion supported by exposure, timing, and measurement?

## 55. Failure modes

A. Stressor -> stress response magnitude: **not one-to-one**.

B. Negative affect -> stress: **invalid construct substitution**.

C. Arousal -> stress: **invalid**.

D. High cortisol -> high psychological stress: **invalid without timing/context**.

E. Low cortisol -> resilience: **invalid**.

F. CAR -> acute stress reactivity: **invalid construct conflation**.

G. TSST response -> chronic stress burden: **invalid transport**.

H. Acute response -> damage/pathology: **invalid universal inference**.

I. Chronic stress -> merely severe acute stress: **invalid temporal definition**.

J. High allostatic-load score -> direct cumulative psychological stress: **invalid measurement leap**.

K. One AL index -> universally standardized construct: **false**.

L. Coping strategy label -> adaptive value: **invalid without context/outcome**.

M. Avoidance -> always maladaptive: **invalid**.

N. Good mental health -> resilience without exposure measurement: **invalid**.

O. Resilience factor -> established mechanism: **causal overreach**.

P. Fast recovery -> no stress response: **invalid**.

Q. Stress -> trauma: **invalid category leap**.

R. Lab biomarker -> individual diagnosis: **invalid clinical transfer**.

6. ANS, HPA, immune, subjective and behavioral response channels separated: **PASS**.

## Canonical operating rule

**Define stressor/exposure -> assess appraisal, control, resources, and history -> identify psychological/biological response channel and timing -> separate reactivity from recovery -> distinguish acute/repeated/chronic patterns -> if cumulative, specify allostatic-load operationalization -> identify coping target -> define resilience relative to adversity and outcome -> check measurement validity/confounds -> state only the narrowest supported inference.**

Never:
**stressor = stress; distress = cortisol; cortisol = damage; chronic = severe; allostatic load = objective stress meter; coping label = adaptiveness; good outcome = resilience without adversity; biomarker = diagnosis.**

## Scope and next dependency

STR-01 establishes the conceptual and measurement foundations needed to prevent stress from becoming a catch-all explanation.

**Next block: STR-02 — Stress physiology and temporal measurement: HPA axis, autonomic regulation, cortisol/catecholamines, inflammation, circadian effects, acute reactivity/recovery, chronic dysregulation, and biomarker validity.**

STR-02 should deepen multi-system physiology and measurement before a later coping/resilience/adaptation synthesis. Trauma-specific criteria and post-traumatic processes should remain separate until their dedicated dependency.
