E1. Schrodt, Witt & Shimkowski (2014), A Meta-Analytical Review of the Demand/Withdraw Pattern of Interaction and its Associations with Individual, Relational, and Communicative Outcomes. https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2013.813632 . This is a heterogeneous composite association, not a specific signed estimate for satisfaction or individual risk. Exact outcome-specific coefficients, sampling and dependence adjustments not inspected. Direct publisher access 403.

E2. Ross, Karney, Nguyen & Bradbury (2019 issue), Communication That Is Maladaptive for Middle-Class Couples Is Adaptive for Socioeconomically Disadvantaged Couples. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30321045/ ; https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9f58m8b5 . Indexed original abstract and institutional repository extracts. Observational Study 2 follows 431 couples across four waves over 27 months; socioeconomic risk moderated the demand/withdraw association with wives' satisfaction, with withdrawal linked to more favorable outcomes under higher risk. The proposed explanation is resource-limited feasibility of requested change; it is not an identified mechanism. Exact Study 1 sample, estimates, uncertainty and confounding controls not verified. Possible shared cohort with REL-X03.1 and REL-X04.1; no independent replication asserted. Counterevidence to a universal harmful-withdrawal model, not a recommendation to ignore partners.

E3. Holley, Sturm & Levenson (2010), Exploring the Basis for Gender Differences in the Demand-Withdraw Pattern. https://doi.org/10.1080/00918361003712145 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3014221/ . Indexed original abstract and results excerpt: relative desire for change predicted demand-withdraw polarization across same-sex and different-sex couples (reported regression R-squared=.41). No direct full-text read; exact sample, covariates, confidence intervals and moderator precision not checked. Desire for change, gender and behavior are separate; findings challenge a fixed gender-role account, not proof of identical effects in all couples.

E4. McNulty & Russell (2010), When "negative" behaviors are positive: a contextual analysis of the long-term effects of problem-solving behaviors on changes in relationship satisfaction. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2845983/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20307131/ . Indexed original abstract and result excerpts. Two longitudinal newlywed studies report interactions between direct negative problem-solving behaviors and initial problem severity: less favorable satisfaction trajectories with relatively minor problems, more favorable with serious problems. Observational moderation does not establish that negative behavior caused improvement. Distinguish direct disagreement from hostility, intimidation or coercion; no endorsement of abuse. Samples, precise estimates and competing models not checked.

E5. Overall & McNulty (2017), What Type of Communication during Conflict is Beneficial for Intimate Relationships? https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28025652/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5181851/ . Indexed review abstract and manuscript excerpts. Distinguishes direct/indirect communication and opposition/cooperation, with problem severity, change feasibility and responsiveness as candidate moderators. Immediate affective comfort and long-term problem resolution are separate outcomes. Review cites E4 and related studies; no independent replication or pooled causal effect claimed.

E6. Salvatore, Kuo, Steele, Simpson & Collins (2011), Recovering From Conflict in Romantic Relationships: A Developmental Perspective. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610397055 ; https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797610397055/ . Complete APS author abstract directly inspected. 73 young adults studied since birth and their romantic partners; observed disengagement from conflict during a four-minute recovery task after a ten-minute discussion. Partner recovery associated with concurrent satisfaction and positive relationship emotions; an interaction of early attachment history and partner recovery predicted stability two years later. Small selected sample, observational design, operationalized short recovery interval and unverified estimates limit transfer. Disengagement from an argument during a recovery task is not equivalent to refusing contact, resolving the disputed issue, or long-term orientation change.

E7. Partner validation during couple conflict predicts reduced negative affect and better psychosocial adjustment to parenthood (2026). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42441643/ . Indexed original abstract extract; direct CAPTCHA. 93 expectant-parent couples, 15-minute recorded discussion coded with Specific Affect Coding System. Validation was associated with the partner's transition from negative to neutral/positive affect. This is a coded sequential association, not randomized validation or proof of durable adjustment improvement. Exact follow-up outcomes and estimates not independently checked here; do not extend the title to uninspected specific results. Acknowledgment and understanding are distinct from agreement or consent.

E8. Do Demand-Withdrawal Communication Patterns During Sexual Conflict Predict Couples' Relationship Satisfaction, Sexual Satisfaction, and Sexual Distress? An Observational and Prospective Study (2025 issue). https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2024.2386997 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39115370/ . Indexed original abstract extracts. 151 long-term couples, baseline and 12-month outcomes. Higher observed demand-withdraw associated with lower concurrent relational and sexual satisfaction, greater concurrent sexual distress and lower later relationship satisfaction. No verified claim about predicting change conditional on baseline; exact models and uncertainty not inspected. Conflict topic and relationship context limit transfer. No inference of sexual obligation.

## 3. Integrated conflict model
Separate issue/content, incompatible preferences, perceived threat, affect intensity, observable communicative act, function, recurring interaction sequence, short-term recovery, actual problem resolution, relationship evaluation, safety and autonomy. Frequency or absence of conflict is not a sufficient quality or safety measure. Expressing a specific disagreement is not coercive demand by definition; declining a request is not pathological withdrawal. A pause, ending an interaction for safety, practical delay, avoidance and punitive disengagement may look similar in sparse observations but have different functions.

Candidate sequence: circumstances and issue -> actor A's appraisal and action -> actor B's appraisal and action -> subsequent A response. Prior preferences, resources, stress and relationship history may affect both actors. This is a descriptive process map, not an identified causal graph. Demand-withdraw can vary by topic, relative desire for change, opportunities and timing. Distinguish a turn-level sequence from recurring pattern and a pattern from an enduring personality trait. Mutual escalation or mutual withdrawal can also occur; reciprocal dynamics do not imply equal responsibility for harm.

Short-term reduction of negative affect, constructive discussion, feasible change, voluntary agreement, and restored trust are separate outcomes. Some problems cannot be solved jointly because preferences are incompatible or resources absent. Acknowledging a concern does not require accepting an inaccurate claim, surrendering a boundary or agreeing to change. No obligation to resume a conversation that is unsafe or unwanted.

## 4. Practical reasoning and revision
1. Define issue, participants, exact target of inference, context and time window. Preserve observable sequence and distinguish exact statements from interpretation. Identify actual preferences and feasible options rather than assuming either.
2. Compare plausible functions of behavior: pursuing a solution, clarifying a concern, regulation, overload, opportunity restrictions, ordinary refusal, incompatible goals, avoidance, coercion or safety response. Do not infer motive from the sequence alone.
3. Assess both immediate and later outcomes: intensity, recovery, actual issue resolution, agreed action, sustained conduct, and individual safety. A calm exchange does not establish resolution; discomfort does not establish failure.
4. Compare naturally occurring episodes and voluntary accounts in equivalent contexts. A consensual pause is different from unilateral punishment; do not infer punitive intent solely from delayed replies. If alternative explanations predict the same observation, leave them unresolved.
5. Check actor/partner/shared influences, preexisting quality, resource constraints, lag, selection, outcome definition and uncertainty. A meta-analytic association is not an individual probability; moderation and sequential observational models do not identify causation. Do not treat a subgroup null as proof of equality.
6. Prefer direct, respectful communication and voluntary negotiation when safe and mutually desired. Respect a refusal and the right to end contact. Do not use silence, threat, engineered distress, family obligations or dependence as leverage. Coercion and safety must be evaluated separately from symmetric conflict models.
7. Update the smallest supported claim, record competing explanations and evidence limitations; no diagnosis or uncalibrated forecast. Distinguish conflict recovery from trust repair; reserve breach-specific evidence for REL-X04.3.

## 5. Internal synthetic checks
Rubric: construct separation, competing explanation, distinguishing evidence, inference boundary. Critical errors: unsupported motive/diagnosis, deterministic transfer, causal inflation, ignoring counterevidence, coercion or privacy breach. Original generic self-authored cases, not held-out.

A. Two people disagree about an expensive repair; one ends the discussion. Response: disagreement, withdrawal and feasibility differ; insufficient resources, need for rest and different preferences compete. Ordinary later discussion of feasible options, if mutually desired, can clarify. No inferred indifference or recommendation to pressure.
B. A person requests a scheduling change, then declines a different change requested by another. Response: issue-specific roles and stable traits differ; constraints and relative priorities compete. Compare repeated comparable topics and voluntary reasons. No fixed demanding/withdrawing type.
C. A brief conversation ends calmly but no agreement is reached. Response: recovery, agreement and resolution differ; regulation and unresolved incompatibility may coexist. Check actual freely agreed decisions and later conduct. No claim that trust is repaired.
D. A study finds opposing associations of withdrawal across resource contexts. Response: moderator, observed action and function differ; feasibility, selection and measurement may contribute. Examine direct interaction estimates and compatible settings. No universal prescription.
E. A listener acknowledges another person's disappointment but declines a proposal. Response: validation and assent differ; recognition of emotion can coexist with independent preference. Explicit choice determines action. No hidden acceptance inferred.
F. A person exits an interaction after being threatened. Response: safety response is distinct from maladaptive withdrawal; actual danger and voluntary boundary take priority. Evidence of threats and context matter. No mutual-blame assignment or pressure to return.
