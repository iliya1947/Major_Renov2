Area 05 Section 17 contains **11 exact items**:

3. Biological age.
4. Biological sex.
5. Nutrition.
6. Energy availability.
7. Glycogen status.
8. Creatine stores.

11. Environmental conditions.

Upstream dependencies:

- BIO-07 — substrate use / metabolic flexibility;
- BIO-10 — substrate depletion/repletion;
- BIO-13 — specificity / training-demand bias;
- BIO-15 — aerobic capacity/economy / central-peripheral state;
- BIO-16 — training adaptation / response variability / retraining.

Downstream ownership:

- IND — practical individualization, adaptive decision-making and repeated observation;
- NUT — exact dietary/energy/carbohydrate/creatine prescription;
- REC — sleep/recovery/readiness management;
- MED — REDs/clinical energy deficiency, sleep disorders and environmental illness;
- PRO — training dose/programming;
- TST — measurement of individual response and repeatability;
- PHY — detailed sex/age/fiber physiology.

This PASS establishes a trainer-level model of **why the same external task can produce different metabolic demands, recovery states and adaptations across people and across time within the same person**.

It does not:

- assign deterministic training prescriptions from age, sex, genetics or fiber type;
- infer a stable “responder/non-responder” identity from one training block;
- treat biological sex as a universal substrate-use rule;
- treat chronological age as equivalent to functional/metabolic age;
- use low energy availability as a single numeric diagnosis;
- treat blood/muscle glycogen or creatine stores as fixed personal traits;
- treat sleep/stress as direct isolated bioenergetic systems;
- use genetic tests for talent identification or programming;
- treat one environmental exposure as universally beneficial/harmful independent of acclimation and task.

## 1. Qualification purpose / Question Card

A trainer needs BIO-17 to avoid:

- prescribing from population averages as if exact individual response were known;
- treating observed response variation as proof of true treatment-response heterogeneity;
- labeling one person “non-responder” from one noisy measurement;
- assuming trained and untrained people experience the same internal load at the same external workload;
- treating fiber type as destiny;
- treating age as deterministic loss of adaptability;
- using “men vs women” as a universal metabolic rule;
- confusing nutrition, energy availability and glycogen status;
- assuming low glycogen affects every task equally;
- assuming creatine supplementation response can be predicted from diet alone;
- treating poor sleep/stress as a direct metabolic diagnosis rather than an indirect state modifier;
- using common genetic variants for individualized training prescription;
- ignoring heat, hypoxia/altitude or other environmental context.

Primary later decisions supported:

- distinguish stable traits from modifiable state;
- identify when an apparent “individual difference” is actually measurement noise or current context;
- know which factors can alter acute substrate use/recovery;
- know which factors can alter chronic adaptation magnitude;
- decide when individual testing/monitoring is more reliable than demographic/genetic inference;
- hand exact interventions to IND/NUT/REC/PRO/TST.

Question type:

- **individual modifier + state/trait distinction + response-heterogeneity boundary + contextual transfer**.

## 2. Core model — individual difference is not one category

BIO-17 separates four layers:

### Layer A — relatively stable / slowly changing traits
- genetics;
- sex-linked physiology;
- aspects of fiber composition;
- age-related biology.

### Layer B — trained phenotype
- training status;
- mitochondrial/capillary/enzyme adaptation;
- economy;
- substrate-transport capacity;
- PCr-recovery phenotype.

### Layer C — current metabolic state
- glycogen;
- creatine availability;
- feeding;
- hydration;
- energy availability.

### Layer D — current external/internal context
- sleep;
- stress/mental fatigue;
- heat;
- hypoxia/altitude;
- environment;
- acute illness/other contextual factors.

Canonical rule:

**trait, trained phenotype and current state must not be treated as interchangeable sources of variability.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 3. Observed variability ≠ true individual response

People often show different changes after the same training program.

But observed spread can arise from:

- measurement error;
- biological day-to-day variability;
- adherence;
- training execution;
- nutrition/sleep;
- regression to the mean;
- different baseline state;
- true treatment-response heterogeneity.

2025 systematic review PMID **41465870** found that few studies adequately accounted for random measurement error and within-person variability; meta-analytic estimates of true inter-individual response variance across several aerobic-training outcomes often included zero.

BIO-16 also retained PMID **42241643**, where individual adaptation magnitudes showed limited reproducibility across repeated endurance-training blocks.

Canonical rule:

**visible response variation ≠ proven stable responder phenotype.**

Confidence: **HIGH boundary**.

---

## 4. One training block ≠ personal response identity

A person who improves little in one block may differ in:

- adherence;
- baseline measurement;
- sleep;
- energy intake;
- exercise execution;
- time course;
- test error;
- actual treatment interaction.

Canonical rule:

**“high responder”, “low responder” and “non-responder” labels require repeated, reliable evidence and are not trait diagnoses from one block.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 5. Training status — content PASS

Training status changes both baseline capacity and response to a given task.

A trained person can differ from an untrained person in:

- VO2max/economy;
- mitochondrial content;
- capillarization;
- transporter/enzyme profile;
- glycogen storage/repletion;
- substrate oxidation;
- PCr recovery;
- tolerance of repeated work;
- absolute work achievable at a given relative intensity.

Canonical rule:

**same external workload ≠ same relative metabolic demand across training states.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 6. Training status ≠ one scalar

“Trained” can mean:

- endurance trained;
- resistance trained;
- sprint trained;
- mixed-sport trained;
- task-specific skilled.

An endurance-trained athlete may be highly trained in one metabolic profile and relatively untrained in another.

Canonical rule:

**training status is task/domain specific, not a universal binary trained/untrained variable.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 7. Baseline fitness and adaptation magnitude

BIO-15/16 retained evidence that lower baseline fitness can be associated with larger relative adaptation in some outcomes.

But:

- ceiling effects;
- dose differences;
- measurement scale;
- regression to the mean

can influence the observed relationship.

Canonical rule:

**lower baseline can permit larger relative gains, but baseline value alone does not predict an individual response.**

Confidence: **HIGH boundary**.

---

## 8. Training status and glycogen depletion

2025 resistance-exercise glycogen meta-analysis PMID **41420384** found significant average glycogen depletion and identified training status among moderators; untrained participants showed somewhat greater pooled depletion than trained participants.

Canonical interpretation:

**training status can modify substrate disturbance during a standardized task.**

Boundary:

- protocol heterogeneity was large;
- this does not establish a universal trained-vs-untrained depletion difference.

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 9. Muscle fiber composition — content PASS

Human skeletal muscle contains heterogeneous fibers with differing contractile/metabolic profiles.

Broad tendencies:

- type I fibers: slower, generally more oxidative;
- type II fibers: faster, generally greater glycolytic/high-power phenotype;
- type IIa is metabolically intermediate/plastic.

Canonical rule:

**fiber type influences local metabolic phenotype but does not assign a whole person to one energy system.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 10. Fiber type ≠ immutable destiny

Fiber phenotype can adapt with training and disuse.

2025 human review PMID **40281372** emphasizes fiber-type-specific proteomic adaptation and differential responsiveness within human skeletal muscle.

Canonical rule:

**fiber composition is partly stable and partly plastic; it is not a fixed deterministic athletic identity.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual / MODERATE exact conversion magnitude**.

---

## 11. Fiber composition ≠ whole-body energy-system label

A muscle contains mixed fibers.

Tasks recruit fibers differently depending on:

- force;
- velocity;
- fatigue;
- movement;
- training state.

Canonical rule:

**fiber composition ≠ “this athlete is glycolytic/aerobic.”**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 12. Fiber biopsy / local measurement boundary

Fiber-type composition is usually measured in a local muscle sample.

A biopsy does not automatically represent:

- all muscles;
- all regions of one muscle;
- whole-body performance.

Canonical rule:

**local fiber composition ≠ whole-body phenotype without qualification.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 13. Biological age — content PASS

Age can influence:

- muscle mass/fiber distribution;
- mitochondrial quality;
- endocrine/metabolic regulation;
- cardiovascular capacity;
- recovery;
- adaptation magnitude.

But exercise remains effective in older adults.

2026 meta-analytic compendium PMID **41349896** synthesized 146 controlled exercise studies in older adults and found improvements across many metabolic/fitness outcomes.

Canonical rule:

**older age modifies baseline physiology and response context but does not imply absence of trainability.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 14. Chronological age ≠ one biological-age score

People of the same chronological age can differ in:

- training history;
- disease burden;
- muscle mass;
- cardiorespiratory fitness;
- metabolic health;
- functional capacity.

Canonical rule:

**chronological age is a context variable, not a complete metabolic phenotype.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 15. Age and mitochondria

2026 review PMID **41988385** describes age-related mitochondrial quality-control dysregulation and exercise-responsive pathways.

BIO-17 retains only:

**aging can alter mitochondrial baseline/state, while exercise remains a modifier.**

Detailed mitochondrial-aging mechanisms remain PHY/BIO-16.

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 16. Age ≠ deterministic training prescription

Age alone does not specify:

- intensity;
- volume;
- rest interval;
- energy-system focus.

Canonical rule:

**program from measured capacity, health, recovery and goals rather than age alone.**

Exact prescription remains IND/PRO/MED.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 17. Biological sex — content PASS

Sex-linked physiology can influence average:

- body composition;
- hemoglobin;
- hormone environment;
- substrate use;
- thermoregulation;
- absolute VO2/power.

But sex effects are context dependent and strongly moderated by:

- body size/composition;
- fitness;
- exercise mode;
- relative intensity;
- age.

Canonical rule:

**biological sex is a relevant modifier, not a universal metabolic rule.**

Confidence: **HIGH boundary**.

---

## 18. Sex and fat oxidation

2026 systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **41569814**:

- 36 studies;
- n=8,990;
- absolute maximal fat oxidation was lower in females;
- relative MFO and FATmax were not significantly different overall;
- age, BMI, cardiorespiratory fitness and exercise modality moderated results.

Canonical rule:

**“women always oxidize more fat during exercise” is not retained as a universal rule.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 19. Relative-intensity matching matters

2026 direct study PMID **41060461** found no sex difference in fat oxidation when exercise was prescribed relative to ventilatory threshold or %HRmax in the tested active young adults.

Canonical rule:

**apparent sex differences can change when exercise intensity and body-composition scaling are matched differently.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 20. Sex ≠ individual prediction

Group-level sex differences do not specify one person’s:

- substrate oxidation;
- VO2max;
- glycogen use;
- recovery;
- trainability.

Canonical rule:

**sex category shifts probability/distribution; it does not replace direct individual measurement.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 21. Nutrition — content PASS

Acute/recent nutrition can modify:

- glycogen;
- blood glucose;
- free fatty acids;
- insulin;
- substrate oxidation;
- hydration/electrolytes;
- training performance.

Canonical rule:

**nutritional state is a current-state modifier of bioenergetics, not a fixed trait.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 22. Nutrition ≠ energy availability

Nutrition describes:

- food/nutrient intake and composition.

Energy availability describes:

- energy remaining for physiological function after exercise energy expenditure relative to FFM.

Canonical rule:

**diet quality/intake pattern ≠ energy availability.**

A person can have:

- high carbohydrate intake but problematic LEA;
- adequate energy availability but a specific nutrient deficiency;
- adequate calories but low glycogen after demanding training.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 23. Nutrition and substrate selection

BIO-07/10 remain authoritative:

- carbohydrate availability can shift carbohydrate oxidation/glycogen use;
- feeding/fasting alters substrate selection;
- exact effects depend on task/intensity/duration.

Canonical rule:

**substrate use reflects current availability + task demand + training state, not diet label alone.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 24. Energy availability — content PASS

IOC 2023 REDs consensus defines energy availability as:

`(energy intake − exercise energy expenditure) / fat-free mass`.

Problematic low energy availability can impair physiological and psychological function.

Current 2026 reviews/meta-analyses reinforce associations with:

- endocrine disruption;
- bone/metabolic effects;
- impaired performance/training response in some athlete populations.

Relevant current sources:

- PMID **43018825**;
- PMID **42618779**;
- PMID **41794545**;
- PMID **39485653**.

Canonical rule:

**energy availability is a systemic recovery/adaptation context, not merely “calories eaten.”**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 25. No universal LEA cut-off

The IOC consensus explicitly moved away from universal single-threshold interpretation because:

- individual responses differ;
- body systems respond differently;
- sex/population context matters;
- real-world measurement error is substantial.

Canonical rule:

**30 kcal/kg FFM/day is not retained as a universal diagnostic threshold for every athlete.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 26. LEA ≠ REDs diagnosis

Low energy availability is an exposure state.

REDs is a broader clinical syndrome related to problematic/prolonged/severe LEA.

Canonical rule:

**LEA ≠ REDs diagnosis.**

Clinical assessment belongs MED/NUT.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 27. Energy availability and adaptation

2024 systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **39485653** reported impaired training/performance outcomes among athletes with LEA/REDs indicators.

2026 experimental-LEA review PMID **41794545** reports biochemical changes after experimentally induced short-term LEA.

Canonical rule:

**problematic LEA can constrain adaptation/performance, but magnitude and affected systems vary between individuals.**

Confidence: **HIGH boundary / MODERATE exact effect size**.

## 28. Glycogen status — content PASS

Current muscle glycogen state depends on:

- prior exercise;
- carbohydrate intake;
- time since exercise;
- training status;
- muscle/fiber recruitment.

Glycogen can alter:

- high-volume work capacity;
- substrate choice;
- signaling context;
- perceived effort in some settings.

Canonical rule:

**glycogen status is a transient local state, not a fixed personal trait.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 29. Glycogen depletion is task specific

2025 meta-analysis PMID **41420384** confirms resistance exercise can substantially deplete muscle glycogen, with depletion moderated by:

- number of sets;
- session duration;
- intensity;
- training status.

Canonical rule:

**same person can enter different sessions with very different glycogen demand/depletion depending on protocol.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 30. Glycogen loading / inter-individual variability

2026 systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **42828472** reported substantial between-person variability in achieved muscle glycogen despite similar carbohydrate intakes and identified carbohydrate dose, exposure duration, training status and prior exercise as important effectors.

Canonical rule:

**same carbohydrate intake ≠ same muscle glycogen concentration across individuals.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 31. Glycogen status ≠ carbohydrate intake alone

Muscle glycogen concentration cannot be inferred precisely from:

- one meal;
- daily carbohydrate intake alone;
- body weight alone.

Canonical rule:

**dietary carbohydrate exposure and measured glycogen state are related but non-equivalent.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 32. Creatine stores — content PASS

Creatine/phosphocreatine availability varies between people due to combinations of:

- dietary intake;
- endogenous synthesis;
- muscle mass;
- muscle creatine uptake;
- supplementation history.

Canonical rule:

**baseline muscle creatine/PCr status is an individual state variable relevant to brief high-intensity bioenergetics.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 33. Diet and creatine status

2026 review PMID **41689538** notes creatine is largely stored in skeletal muscle and dietary exposure differs substantially with animal-source food intake.

2025 RCT PMID **40939139** in vegans/vegetarians showed large increases in muscle creatine/total creatine after supplementation but no change in repeated-sprint peak/mean power over 7 days.

Canonical rule:

**lower dietary creatine exposure can alter muscle creatine status, but raising muscle creatine does not guarantee improvement in every performance endpoint.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 34. Creatine response ≠ fixed responder identity

2026 review PMID **42832309** notes individual responses may be influenced by age, sex, baseline creatine status, diet and training context.

BIO-17 does not retain:

- a deterministic “creatine responder genotype”;
- vegan diet = guaranteed performance response;
- baseline low stores = guaranteed magnitude of performance gain.

Canonical rule:

**creatine-store response and performance response are distinct outcomes.**

Confidence: **HIGH boundary**.

## 36. Sleep/stress indirectly — content PASS

Sleep and psychological/cognitive stress can affect performance and recovery through:

- perceived effort;

- attention/motivation;
- appetite/energy regulation;
- recovery behavior.

Canonical rule:

**sleep/stress are indirect modifiers of bioenergetic performance context, not separate ATP-producing systems.**

## 37. Sleep deprivation and endurance performance

Meta-analysis PMID **36472094** found a moderate negative effect of sleep deprivation on endurance performance, with longer exercise tending to be more affected.

2026 review PMID **41963169** also identifies sleep as an important performance/recovery factor.

Canonical rule:

**sleep loss can reduce performance even without proving a primary metabolic-pathway limitation.**

Confidence: **HIGH performance boundary**.

## 38. Mental fatigue and resistance exercise

2026 meta-analysis PMID **42168782**:

- 11 RCTs;
- >205 participants;
- cognitive effort reduced resistance-exercise volume on average;
- evidence certainty/quality limitations require caution.

Canonical rule:

**mental fatigue can reduce realized work capacity without being interpreted as depleted ATP/glycogen by default.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 39. Stress ≠ metabolic diagnosis

Psychological stress can change:

- perceived exertion;
- recovery behavior;
- sleep;
- adherence;
- autonomic/endocrine state.

Canonical rule:

**stress-related performance decline does not identify one metabolic deficit.**

Confidence: **HIGH boundary**.

## 40. Genetic variability — content PASS

Genetic variation contributes to differences in:

- baseline traits;
- performance-related phenotypes;
- some adaptation pathways.

But athletic/metabolic performance is highly polygenic and context dependent.

2026 systematic review PMID **42931598**:

- 204 studies;
- common candidate variants such as ACE and ACTN3 showed heterogeneous associations;
- only limited replicated genome-wide findings;
- PGS/TGS approaches remain limited by validation and ancestry transferability;
- current evidence is insufficient for individual talent identification, injury prediction or training prescription.

Canonical rule:

**genetic contribution is real; current sports-genetic prediction for individual programming is not ready for routine use.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 41. Single-gene determinism rejected

Reject:

- ACTN3 = sprint athlete;
- ACE = endurance athlete;
- one SNP = training-response prescription.

Canonical rule:

**single common variants have small/context-dependent effects and cannot define an individual metabolic training plan.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 42. Polygenic scores boundary

Polygenic scores can capture more architecture than one candidate gene.

But performance/response prediction is limited by:

- ancestry dependence;
- external-validation weakness;
- phenotype definition;
- environmental interaction;
- training exposure.

Canonical rule:

**polygenic score ≠ validated individual training prescription.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 43. Genetics vs observed phenotype

## 44. Environmental conditions — content PASS

Environment can acutely modify:

- oxygen availability;
- thermoregulation;
- cardiovascular strain;
- substrate use;
- perceived effort;
- sustainable output.

Important contexts include:

- heat;
- cold;
- altitude/hypoxia;
- air quality;
- humidity.

Canonical rule:

**same external task ≠ same internal metabolic/cardiovascular demand across environments.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 45. Heat/hypoxia acute environment

2026 network meta-analysis PMID **43020048** included 26 controlled trials and found environment-specific changes in ventilation, VO2 and HR; heat and hypoxia altered acute cardiorespiratory burden differently.

2026 network meta-analysis PMID **41826976** found acute heat/hypoxia/combined exposure generally impaired exercise capacity and altered physiological strain.

Canonical rule:

**environment alters acute demand and can invalidate direct comparison with thermoneutral/normoxic performance.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 46. Acclimation ≠ acute exposure

Repeated environmental exposure can produce adaptation.

2026 heat meta-analysis PMID **42637035** found exercise in heat produced greater heat-specific physiological adaptations than thermoneutral training.

Hypoxia/altitude evidence is more heterogeneous:

- overview PMID **42043079** reports potential aerobic/hematological adaptation with low methodological quality across many reviews;
- running meta-analysis PMID **42891559** found small time-trial benefit and increased hemoglobin, but inconsistent VO2max/economy effects;
- RCT meta-analysis PMID **42302183** found no clear RE/VO2max/TT superiority, though TTE improved.

Canonical rule:

**acute environmental impairment and chronic acclimation/training adaptation are separate questions.**

Confidence: **HIGH boundary**.

---

## 47. Environment ≠ universally ergogenic stress

Heat/hypoxia can:

- raise internal load;
- reduce absolute output acutely;
- produce selected adaptations chronically.

Canonical rule:

**greater environmental stress ≠ better training by definition.**

Exact environmental prescription/safety remains PRO/MED.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 48. Individual environment response

Response to heat/hypoxia can vary with:

- acclimation history;
- fitness;
- hydration;
- iron/hematological status;
- health;
- exposure dose;
- genetics.

Canonical rule:

**environmental context and individual susceptibility must be separated from the nominal workout.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

## 49. Stable trait vs current state — trainer decision model

When two athletes differ, ask in this order:

1. Was the external task truly equivalent?
2. Was relative intensity equivalent?
3. Was measurement reliable?
4. Was current state equivalent?
   - glycogen;
   - feeding;
   - sleep;
   - stress;
   - environment;
5. Was training status/task familiarity equivalent?
6. Only then consider slower-changing biological traits:
   - age;
   - sex;
   - fiber composition;
   - genetics.

Canonical rule:

**do not explain current performance by immutable traits before excluding modifiable state and measurement factors.**

Confidence: **HIGH practical**.

---

## 50. Individualization boundary

BIO-17 identifies scientifically plausible modifiers.

IND owns the actual decision process:

- what to measure;
- which modifier matters;
- whether to change programming;
- how to update after repeated observations.

Canonical rule:

**BIO-17 explains sources of variation; IND decides how to act on an individual.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 51. Practical trainer implications — non-prescriptive layer

1. Match workloads by relative as well as absolute demand when comparing athletes.
2. Treat training status as domain specific.
3. Do not label fiber type or genetics as destiny.
4. Use age/sex as contextual priors, not prescriptions.
5. Separate nutrition, energy availability and glycogen status.
6. Treat low energy availability as a health/adaptation context, not a single universal number.
7. Recognize glycogen and creatine stores as changeable states.
8. Do not infer metabolic depletion from sleep/stress-related performance decline.
9. Do not classify stable responders/non-responders from one block.
10. Account for heat/hypoxia/environment when comparing sessions.
11. Prefer repeated individual performance/state data over demographic/genetic stereotypes.
12. Exact intervention remains IND/PRO/NUT/REC/MED.

## 53. Measurement boundaries

### Training status
Domain/task-specific construct, not binary identity.

### Fiber composition
Local tissue phenotype; not whole-body energy-system identity.

### Age
Chronological context; not full biological phenotype.

### Sex
Group-level biological modifier; not deterministic individual prediction.

### Nutrition
Current exposure/state; not identical to energy availability.

### Energy availability
Systemic energy-allocation construct; measurement error substantial and no universal diagnostic cutoff.

### Glycogen
Local/state-dependent substrate pool.

### Creatine
Muscle store/state; change in store ≠ guaranteed performance response.

### Sleep/stress
Indirect state modifiers; performance impact ≠ one metabolic mechanism.

### Genetics
Probabilistic/polygenic influence; not current prescription tool.

### Environment
External context that changes internal load.

Canonical rule:

**measurement/construct level must match the individual-difference claim.**

## 56. Evidence-quality interpretation

### Stronger trainer-level evidence

- individual observed response must be separated from measurement error/within-person variability;
- training status modifies relative metabolic demand;
- fiber phenotypes have distinct metabolic tendencies but remain plastic/local;
- age modifies baseline physiology but does not eliminate trainability;
- sex effects on substrate use are context dependent rather than universal;
- energy availability is non-equivalent to calorie intake and lacks one universal clinical threshold;
- glycogen is a state variable strongly affected by prior exercise/CHO exposure;
- creatine stores vary and store response is non-equivalent to performance response;
- sleep loss/mental fatigue can impair realized performance without identifying a metabolic pathway deficit;
- sports performance genetics is polygenic and insufficient for individual prescription;
- environment modifies internal physiological demand.

### Translation cautions

- response-heterogeneity evidence often suffers from poor control of measurement error;
- fiber-type measurements are local;
- sex meta-analyses can mix intensity-scaling/body-composition methods;
- age evidence often mixes healthy, clinical and older populations;
- LEA measurement in free-living athletes is difficult;
- glycogen studies are muscle/task specific;
- creatine-store studies do not prove a simple baseline-store→performance-response law;
- sleep/stress are indirect modifiers;
- genetics is ancestry/phenotype dependent;
- environmental studies vary widely in exposure dose/acclimation.
