## 1. Qualification purpose / Question Card

A trainer needs this model to avoid:

- programming “ATP-PC,” “glycolytic,” and “aerobic” sessions as isolated biochemical compartments;
- assigning energy systems by one rep/time chart;
- assuming intensity alone defines the target;
- assuming work:rest ratio is sufficient without absolute work/rest durations;
- assuming the same ratio creates the same stimulus across modes;
- assuming more intervals or more weekly sessions always create more adaptation;
- assuming shorter recovery is always better because it creates more metabolic stress;
- confusing high VO2 time with guaranteed VO2max adaptation;
- confusing high lactate with a superior glycolytic training stimulus;
- treating modality as irrelevant to mechanical/neuromuscular specificity;
- inferring sport transfer from generic physiological improvement;
- applying population-level “optimal” meta-regression points as individual prescriptions.

Primary later decisions supported:

- choose which acute energetic demand to bias;
- understand how intensity × duration × recovery jointly alter energetic contribution;
- distinguish power-maintenance work from metabolically accumulating work;
- understand why long intervals create different oxidative demand from short all-out sprints;
- understand why mode and task mechanics matter;
- understand why frequency is a dose-distribution variable rather than a standalone adaptation switch;
- combine energy-system demands when the target task itself is mixed;
- preserve specificity to actual performance demands.

Question type:

- **training-variable manipulation + acute energetic bias + adaptation/specificity boundary**.

Target contexts:

- healthy adults;
- recreationally active adults;
- trained endurance/team-sport athletes where evidence exists;
- cycling, running, swimming and field-based interval structures.

Closure criterion for PASS-01:

- all 10 exact Section 13 items covered at content/scope level;
- “focused” clearly separated from “isolated”;
- acute responses separated from chronic adaptation;
- no content-level blocker preventing independent closure audit.

## 2. Core model — energy systems can be biased, not isolated

All major ATP-resynthesis pathways remain active to varying degrees during exercise.

Training design changes their **relative demand**.

Project model:

`energetic demand profile`
← intensity  
+ work-bout duration  
+ recovery duration/mode  
+ repetitions/total work  
+ exercise mode  
+ frequency / accumulated exposure  
+ starting physiological state  
+ task mechanics.

Canonical rule:

**energy-system development means biasing an integrated energetic system toward the demands of the target task, not switching one pathway on while switching others off.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 3. Acute stimulus vs chronic adaptation

BIO-13 must distinguish:

### Acute response
What happens during one session:

- PCr depletion/recovery;
- VO2;
- lactate;
- tissue deoxygenation;
- power decrement;
- time near VO2max;
- substrate use.

### Chronic adaptation
What changes after repeated exposure:

- VO2max;
- oxidative capacity;
- sprint/repeated-sprint performance;
- power/capacity;
- enzyme/transport/buffering adaptations;
- task performance.

Project rule:

**an acute energetic profile is a training input, not proof of a specific chronic adaptation.**

Detailed chronic mechanisms remain BIO-16.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 4. Manipulating intensity — content PASS

Intensity changes:

- ATP turnover rate;
- force/power demand;
- attainable duration;
- PCr use;
- glycolytic demand;
- oxidative demand;
- recruitment;
- fatigue rate.

At higher intensities:

- phosphagen and glycolytic ATP rates can rise sharply;
- oxidative metabolism remains active;
- duration generally becomes more limited.

At lower sustainable intensities:

- oxidative ATP production generally supplies a larger relative share;
- long duration can still create large absolute energetic demand.

Canonical rule:

**intensity changes relative pathway flux and sustainable duration; it does not assign exclusive system ownership.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 5. Intensity category ≠ adaptation guarantee

2026 meta-analysis of 115 HIIT vs moderate-to-vigorous continuous-training trials (PMID **41804294**) found:

- HIIT produced a modestly larger average improvement in VO2max;
- effects varied by training status, sex, interval type and exercise mode;
- several other endurance/performance outcomes showed no clear superiority.

2026 SIT vs MICT systematic review/meta-analysis also found broadly comparable effects for many physiological outcomes, with only selected small between-group differences.

Canonical rule:

**higher training intensity can change the stimulus, but “harder” does not mean universally superior adaptation.**

Confidence: **HIGH boundary / MODERATE-HIGH exact comparative effects**.

---

## 6. Intensity and individual state

The same external speed/power can represent different relative intensity depending on:

- VO2max/MAP/MAV;
- critical power/speed;
- threshold profile;
- training status;
- fatigue;
- environment.

Project rule:

**absolute intensity ≠ relative physiological intensity.**

Detailed threshold prescription remains PRO/TST.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 7. Manipulating duration — content PASS

Work-bout duration changes how long high ATP demand must be sustained.

Shorter bouts can better preserve:

- peak velocity/power;
- phosphagen contribution;
- technical quality.

Longer bouts tend to permit:

- greater cumulative oxidative contribution;
- greater metabolite accumulation depending on intensity;
- more time for VO2 to rise;
- larger within-bout fatigue.

Canonical rule:

**work duration changes the time available for metabolic redistribution; it cannot be interpreted independently of intensity and recovery.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 8. Same total sprint time ≠ same acute physiology

PMID **38451737** compared:

- 4 × 10-s sprints / 80-s recovery;
- 2 × 20-s sprints / 160-s recovery;

with:

- equal total sprint duration;
- same sprint:rest ratio.

Findings:

- 20-s sprints produced greater average local deoxygenation in vastus lateralis and rectus femoris;
- increasing repetitions of the shorter sprints did not necessarily increase oxidative stimulus.

Canonical rule:

**absolute work-bout duration matters even when total work time and work:rest ratio are matched.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**, with small healthy-male sample.

---

## 9. Longer HIIT intervals and high VO2 exposure

2026 systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID **42237396**):

- 86 articles;
- 239 HIIT protocols;
- longer work intervals (≥2 min) produced greater accumulated time near high percentages of VO2max than shorter intervals on average;
- variable-intensity work intervals also increased tVO2max;
- heterogeneity was substantial.

Important boundary:

**tVO2max is an acute internal-load metric, not a validated direct dose of future adaptation.**

Confidence: **HIGH acute direction / MODERATE adaptation implication**.

---

## 10. Manipulating work:rest ratio — content PASS

Work:rest influences:

- PCr restoration;
- power maintenance;
- residual Pi/H+/ion disturbance;
- VO2 during recovery;
- glycolytic accumulation;
- total attainable work;
- session density.

Shorter recovery generally:

- reduces restoration before the next bout;
- increases residual metabolic/cardiorespiratory load;
- can reduce subsequent sprint power.

Longer recovery generally:

- permits greater restoration;
- can preserve sprint quality/power;
- reduces density.

Canonical rule:

**work:rest changes the starting state of each subsequent bout.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 11. Work:rest ratio ≠ complete interval prescription

A ratio such as 1:4 can mean:

- 5 s : 20 s;
- 10 s : 40 s;
- 30 s : 120 s.

These are not physiologically identical.

PMID **38451737** directly supports different acute responses despite a matched ratio.

2026 circuit study PMID **41507409** similarly reported different acute physiology for 10:10, 20:20 and 30:30 despite all being 1:1.

Canonical rule:

**a ratio without absolute work/rest durations is physiologically incomplete.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 12. Work:rest and repeated sprint oxygen demand

2026 replication study PMID **42712884**:

- n=21 men;
- 10 × 6-s maximal cycle sprints;
- active rests of 24, 18 or 12 s;
- 1:4, 1:3 and 1:2 ratios.

Shorter rest:

- increased VO2 during work and recovery;
- reduced sprint performance;
- reproduced the direction of the original VO2-rest finding with a smaller effect.

Canonical rule:

**shortening recovery can increase oxidative/cardiorespiratory demand while decreasing repeated-sprint performance quality.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 13. Work:rest and acute metabolic bias

PMID **40833321** in 19 elite track endurance cyclists under hypoxia compared:

- 6-s sprint / 24-s rest (1:4);
- 10-s sprint / 20-s rest (1:2);
- mixed 6/10-s configuration.

Longer 10-s / shorter-relative-rest bouts produced:

- more work per sprint;
- greater sprint decrement;
- higher blood lactate;
- greater muscle oxygen desaturation.

Shorter 6-s / longer-rest bouts produced:

- greater peak/mean power;
- greater torque;
- greater reoxygenation.

Canonical interpretation:

**short, well-recovered sprints bias toward preserving power; longer/denser sprints bias toward greater metabolic accumulation.**

Boundary:

- study used hypoxia and elite cyclists;
- acute responses do not prove chronic “system development.”

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 14. Fixed work:rest ratio does not guarantee fixed chronic effect

2026 SIT meta-analysis PMID **42477971**:

- 93 trials / 78 studies / n=1188;
- work:rest ratio had an uncertain association with VO2max improvement;
- credible interval crossed both positive and negative effects.

Canonical rule:

**work:rest strongly changes acute physiology but does not have one universal chronic dose-response for VO2max.**

Confidence: **HIGH boundary**.

---

## 15. Manipulating mode — content PASS

Exercise mode changes:

- active muscle mass;
- contraction pattern;
- force/velocity profile;
- mechanical load;
- skill requirement;
- local perfusion;
- achievable power;
- test/task specificity.

Running, cycling, rowing, swimming and field-based sprinting can create similar broad energetic categories but not identical internal or mechanical demands.

Canonical rule:

**mode is part of the stimulus, not merely a container for heart rate or VO2.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 16. Mode can moderate group-level outcomes

PMID **41804294** found exercise mode moderated HIIT-vs-continuous VO2max effects at the group level.

2025/2026 interval-training network meta-analysis PMID **40605061** also reported training-mode moderation within some interval-training analyses.

But:

- subgroup/meta-regression evidence is not randomized mode comparison;
- mode differences can be confounded by population and protocol.

Canonical rule:

**mode can modify adaptation, but meta-regression moderation does not prove a universal hierarchy of modes.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 17. Cross-training transfer boundary

2026 running-vs-cycling cross-training systematic review/meta-analysis PMID **42267259**:

- only 7 eligible studies;
- no clear between-mode differences detected for treadmill VO2max, cycle VO2max or running performance;
- CIs were wide and crossed the null;
- evidence was heterogeneous and often methodologically limited.

Canonical interpretation:

**current evidence does not justify claiming either full interchangeability or strong non-transfer between running and cycling.**

Project rule:

**central aerobic adaptations may transfer, while task-specific performance should still be evaluated separately.**

Confidence: **MODERATE** due limited evidence.

---

## 18. Manipulating frequency — content PASS

Frequency determines how training exposure is distributed across time.

Frequency interacts with:

- per-session dose;
- total weekly dose;
- recovery;
- accumulated fatigue;
- technical quality;
- adherence.

Canonical rule:

**frequency is a dose-distribution variable, not an independent energy-system switch.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 19. More sessions ≠ guaranteed more adaptation

2026 SIT meta-analysis PMID **42477971** found:

- +1 session/week estimated VO2max association was uncertain;
- 95% credible interval crossed negative and positive effects;
- total weekly sprint time also showed no consistent association;
- intervention duration showed a clearer positive relationship than session sprint count.

Canonical rule:

**within the observed SIT literature, increasing weekly frequency does not show a simple guaranteed VO2max dose-response.**

Confidence: **HIGH for this evidence set**.

---

## 20. More repetitions ≠ guaranteed more VO2max

PMID **42477971**:

- +2 sprint repetitions per session had a trivial estimated association with VO2max change;
- sprint number was not a meaningful moderator in untrained populations;
- intervention duration was associated with larger VO2max improvements.

Canonical rule:

**more work inside one SIT session is not automatically more aerobic adaptation.**

Confidence: **HIGH for untrained SIT literature**.

---

## 21. Frequency evidence is outcome specific

The absence of a simple frequency→VO2max relation does not mean frequency is irrelevant to:

- sprint performance;
- repeated-sprint ability;
- mechanical skill;
- fatigue management;
- total training load.

Canonical rule:

**frequency effects must be tied to the target outcome and total training context.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 22. Phosphagen-focused training — content PASS

“Phosphagen-focused” means designing work to emphasize:

- very high power;
- brief efforts;
- high-quality repeated output;
- substantial PCr use;
- sufficient recovery to restore enough power/PCr for the next repetition.

It does **not** mean:

- glycolysis is off;
- oxidative metabolism is off;
- PCr is fully restored before every bout;
- one exact sprint duration is universally phosphagen-only.

Canonical rule:

**phosphagen-focused training biases toward brief high-power work and adequate recovery, not phosphagen isolation.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 23. Power maintenance as a practical phosphagen-focus marker

PMID **40833321** supports the acute pattern that 6-s sprints with longer relative recovery preserved:

- peak/mean power;
- torque;
- reoxygenation

better than longer/denser 10-s bouts.

2026 work:rest replication PMID **42712884** showed shorter recovery reduced sprint performance.

Canonical rule:

**if the intended acute bias is high-quality phosphagen-heavy power, preserving output is more informative than maximizing lactate or density.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH practical inference**.

---

## 24. PCr-focused ≠ PCr-only

Even a 6-s maximal sprint includes:

- glycolytic ATP contribution;
- oxidative ATP contribution;
- oxidative recovery between repetitions.

Canonical rule:

**“phosphagen focused” describes relative energetic bias, not exclusive biochemical supply.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 25. Glycolytic-focused training — content PASS

“Glycolytic-focused” means designing work that sustains high ATP turnover long enough and/or with limited enough recovery to increase reliance on glycolytic ATP provision and metabolite accumulation.

Potential characteristics:

- longer high-intensity efforts than pure power-maintenance work;
- incomplete recovery;
- repeated bouts;
- substantial glycogen use;
- high lactate/metabolic disturbance.

But:

- lactate is not direct glycolytic ATP flux;
- high lactate is not proof of superior chronic glycolytic adaptation;
- oxidative contribution remains active.

Canonical rule:

**glycolytic-focused training biases glycolytic demand but does not isolate glycolysis.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

---

## 26. Acute glycolytic-bias evidence

PMID **40833321**:

- 10-s / 1:2 repeated-sprint condition produced higher lactate and muscle desaturation than 6-s / 1:4.

PMID **42712884**:

- shorter recovery increased VO2 demand while performance fell.

Together:

**increasing effort duration and/or reducing recovery can create greater metabolic accumulation, but the resulting stimulus is mixed rather than purely glycolytic.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 27. “Maximum lactate” is not the target definition

BIO-04/BIO-12 boundaries remain authoritative:

- blood lactate depends on production, transport, oxidation and sampling;
- blood lactate ≠ glycolytic ATP flux.

Canonical rule:

**glycolytic-focused training is defined by task demand, not by chasing the highest lactate number.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 28. Oxidative-focused training — content PASS

“Oxidative-focused” means designing work to create substantial sustained oxidative ATP demand.

This can include:

- continuous moderate/severe-domain work;
- long-interval HIIT;
- shorter intervals with sufficiently high cumulative VO2;
- mixed interval formats.

Canonical rule:

**oxidative-focused does not mean low intensity only.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 29. Long intervals and oxidative exposure

PMID **42237396** found long HIIT intervals (≥2 min) produced more accumulated high-VO2 time than shorter intervals on average.

2026 HIIT-vs-SIT review PMID **41740126** found larger average VO2max/MAP-MAV effects with HIIT than SIT, particularly in longer-interval/longer-intervention subgroups, but most evidence certainty was low/very low.

Canonical rule:

**longer high-intensity intervals are a robust way to increase acute high-VO2 exposure; exact chronic superiority remains context/certainty dependent.**

Confidence: **HIGH acute / MODERATE chronic comparative**.

---

## 30. Oxidative adaptation ≠ tVO2max alone

PMID **42237396** explicitly notes that the dose-response between tVO2max and long-term adaptation remains uncertain.

Canonical rule:

**time near VO2max is an acute internal-load marker, not a validated standalone adaptation dose.**

Confidence: **HIGH boundary**.

---

## 31. SIT can improve aerobic fitness

2026 SIT meta-analysis PMID **42477971** found an average VO2max increase in untrained populations.

2026 SIT vs MICT systematic review/meta-analysis found SIT broadly comparable or modestly superior for selected aerobic outcomes.

Therefore:

**an all-out sprint-based stimulus can produce aerobic adaptations; “anaerobic-looking” work is not aerobically irrelevant.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 32. Mixed demands — content PASS

Many real tasks simultaneously demand:

- high peak power;
- repeated high-power output;
- glycolytic tolerance/capacity;
- rapid oxidative recovery;
- sustained aerobic support.

Examples include:

- team sports;
- combat/intermittent sports;
- repeated sprint tasks;
- high-density resistance circuits.

Canonical rule:

**mixed-demand tasks should not be reduced to one energy-system label.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 33. Team-sport HIIT supports mixed adaptation

2026 team-sport-athlete meta-analysis PMID **42961026** found HIIT improved:

- VO2max;
- maximal aerobic power;
- ventilatory threshold;
- Yo-Yo intermittent-recovery performance;
- Wingate peak/mean power.

Canonical interpretation:

**the same interval-training family can improve both aerobic and anaerobic-related performance outcomes.**

Boundary:

- meta-regression moderators were study-level and exploratory;
- this does not mean one protocol maximizes every outcome simultaneously.

Confidence: **HIGH mixed-adaptation evidence / MODERATE protocol ranking**.

---

## 34. Repeated-sprint training and mixed performance

2026 field-based RST meta-analysis PMID **41983005** in soccer players found improvements in:

- short sprint ability;
- repeated-sprint ability;
- change of direction;
- high-intensity running;

but no clear overall improvement in aerobic capacity.

Canonical rule:

**repeated-sprint training can be highly specific to intermittent high-intensity performance without guaranteeing a large VO2max effect.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 35. Mixed energy-system demand ≠ concurrent-training interference by definition

2026 meta-analysis PMID **41734815**:

- 9 studies / 177 participants;
- SIT + resistance training vs resistance training alone;
- no clear decrement in pooled strength, jump or sprint outcomes;
- VO2max improved more with concurrent SIT;
- short-sprint subgroup suggested possible jump benefit.

Canonical boundary:

**combining different energetic demands does not automatically create an interference effect.**

Detailed concurrent programming remains PRO/GPP.

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**, limited study count.

## 36. Specificity to actual task demands — content PASS

Training specificity has multiple dimensions:

- movement mode;
- contraction pattern;
- velocity;
- force/power;
- work-bout duration;
- recovery duration;
- duty cycle;
- active muscle mass;
- metabolic environment;
- technical/decision demands.

Canonical rule:

**“energy-system specificity” is only one layer of task specificity.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 37. Match the energetic pattern, not a label

A target task should be analyzed by:

- peak power requirement;
- mean/sustained power;
- bout duration;
- number of repeated bouts;
- between-bout recovery;
- degree of performance decrement;
- total event duration;
- movement mode.

Project rule:

**train the demand profile rather than a named energy system.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

## 38. Specificity ≠ exact mimicry

Training need not reproduce competition identically every session.

Useful training can:

- isolate a limiting component;
- overload one characteristic;
- reduce mechanical cost while retaining central aerobic stimulus;
- simplify technical demands.

But transfer must ultimately be judged against the target task.

Canonical rule:

**specificity is graded, not binary.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual**.

## 39. Mode-transfer evidence is uncertain, not absent

PMID **42267259** found no clear run-vs-cycle between-group differences but had:

- only seven studies;
- wide confidence intervals;
- old protocols;
- methodological concerns.

Therefore do not claim:

- running and cycling are interchangeable;
- running adaptations never transfer to cycling;
- cycling cannot support running performance.

Canonical rule:

**mode transfer exists as an empirical question; absence of detected difference is not proof of equivalence.**

Confidence: **HIGH evidence-interpretation boundary**.

## 40. Specific performance can diverge from general physiology

An intervention can improve:

- VO2max

without proportionally improving:

- sprint ability;
- running economy;
- race performance;
- repeated-sprint ability;
- sport skill.

PMID **41804294** showed outcome-dependent differences between HIIT and continuous training.

Canonical rule:

**general physiological adaptation ≠ guaranteed task-performance transfer.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 41. No universal “optimal interval”

2025 network meta-analysis PMID **40605061** modeled apparent optima for HIIT work duration/work:recovery and reported frequency/mode moderators.

However:

- these are study-level model estimates;
- direct differences among HIIT/SIT/RST were not significant;
- different outcomes/populations produce different optima;
- later 2026 analyses show uncertainty for several protocol moderators.

Canonical rule:

**population-level meta-regression optima are hypotheses/summary patterns, not universal individual prescriptions.**

Confidence: **HIGH anti-overgeneralization**.

---

## 42. Frequency / duration / work:rest are outcome dependent

PMID **42477971** in untrained SIT found:

- intervention duration had a clearer association with VO2max change;
- sprint count, frequency and work:rest ratio showed uncertain associations.

PMID **42961026** in team-sport athletes found:

- longer intervention duration associated with greater VO2max effects;
- shorter recovery associated with greater VO2max effects in exploratory study-level analyses.

These are not contradictory because:

- populations differ;
- protocols differ;
- models differ;
- evidence certainty differs.

Canonical rule:

**training-variable effects are population-, protocol- and outcome-dependent.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 43. Acute work:rest findings ≠ chronic adaptation rules

Shorter rest can acutely:

- raise VO2;
- increase lactate;
- increase fatigue;
- reduce power.

That does not prove it produces better chronic:

- VO2max;
- sprint speed;
- glycolytic capacity;
- sport performance.

Canonical rule:

**acute internal load must not be automatically converted into chronic programming superiority.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 44. Training volume is multidimensional

Interval-training “volume” can refer to:

- number of intervals;
- work duration;
- total work time;
- distance;
- mechanical work;
- time above an internal threshold;
- session duration;
- weekly sessions.

These are non-equivalent.

PMID **42477971** showed sprint repetitions and weekly sprint time did not map simply onto VO2max change.

Canonical rule:

**state the dose construct before interpreting training volume.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 45. Practical trainer implications — non-prescriptive layer

1. Start from the target task, not from an energy-system name.
2. Use intensity to alter ATP-turnover rate and sustainable duration.
3. Use work duration to control how long power/metabolic demand must be sustained.
4. Use recovery to control restoration vs accumulation.
5. Always report absolute work and rest durations in addition to ratio.
6. Use mode deliberately because mechanical and technical demands differ.
7. Treat frequency as distribution of total exposure/recovery, not an isolated magic variable.
8. For high-power/phosphagen bias, preserve output rather than maximizing burn.
9. For glycolytic bias, extend high-rate demand and/or reduce recovery without pretending oxidative metabolism is absent.
10. For oxidative bias, accumulate substantial oxidative demand; long intervals are one route, not the only route.
11. Mixed-demand sports need mixed training, but not every session must reproduce every demand.
12. Judge success by target performance/adaptation, not acute lactate or tVO2max alone.
13. Exact prescription remains PRO after individualized assessment.

## 47. Measurement boundaries

### VO2
Whole-body oxidative signal; not total ATP production.

### tVO2max
Acute exposure metric; not proven chronic adaptation dose.

### Blood lactate
Systemic concentration; not direct glycolytic ATP flux.

### PCr
Local muscle state; recovery varies by muscle/task/method.

### Power / sprint decrement
Performance outputs; not direct biochemical pathway measurements.

### NIRS / tissue oxygenation
Local oxygenation/hemodynamic proxy; not mitochondrial ATP flux.

### Heart rate
Cardiovascular response; not energy-system percentage.

Canonical rule:

**no single acute marker certifies the intended energy-system adaptation.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 50. Evidence-quality interpretation

### Stronger trainer-level evidence

- major energy pathways overlap;
- intensity, duration and recovery jointly change energetic demand;
- same work:rest ratio can produce different physiology when absolute durations differ;
- shorter recovery can raise VO2/internal load and reduce repeated-sprint performance;
- longer HIIT intervals generally produce more acute high-VO2 exposure;
- SIT can improve VO2max;
- more sprint repetitions do not necessarily produce more VO2max adaptation;
- mode and task structure matter;
- mixed-demand training can improve multiple qualities;
- acute response is not a chronic adaptation surrogate.

### Translation cautions

- many acute interval studies are small and male-dominant;
- hypoxia studies should not be numerically transferred to normoxia;
- tVO2max is not validated as a standalone chronic adaptation dose;
- mode moderation in meta-regression is not randomized mode evidence;
- cross-training evidence currently has few trials and wide uncertainty;
- many “optimal” interval values arise from study-level meta-regression;
- frequency findings are outcome/population specific;
- estimated energy-system contributions depend on method.
