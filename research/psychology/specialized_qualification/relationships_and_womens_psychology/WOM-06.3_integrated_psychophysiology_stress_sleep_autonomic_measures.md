# WOM-06.3 — Integrated women's psychophysiology: stress, sleep and autonomic measures

Date: 2026-11-26. Status: COMPLETE_FOR_SPECIFIED_SCOPE; internal assessment only. WOM-06 integration and direction 13 closure are bounded to selected evidence and analytic methodology, not clinical or independent competence certification.

## 1. Question and scope
How can endocrine, sleep, autonomic and stress processes be considered as interacting contextual contributors without treating physiological markers as readouts of motives, emotional states or individual preferences? Reopened WOM-06.1 and WOM-06.2 from the active branch in full. Integrate their documented findings with WOM-05 role/context and IND-03 individual inference rules. Prior source-access assertions are inherited records, not newly independently verified evidence. Target: adult women, with mixed-population evidence explicitly marked. Claims: measurement, association, limited experimental causal inference, applicability. Distinguish acute versus chronic stress, external exposure versus appraisal, baseline versus response/recovery, and biological versus psychological outcomes.

## 2. Evidence ledger
Public targeted searches 2026-11-26: experimental sleep loss emotion meta-analysis; cycle-related cardiac vagal activity meta-analysis; cortisol sex differences TSST; subjective/physiological acute stress concordance; ecological momentary stress; cycle and sleep continuity; HRV measurement recommendations; within-person progesterone/HRV studies. Selected primary studies and syntheses, not exhaustive. Search results were inspected and selected source pages opened. No article was read end-to-end. Directly inspected abstracts and sections are distinguished from indexed extracts. PubMed/PMC CAPTCHA and publisher 403/429 prevented some direct reads.

E1. Palmer et al. (2024), Sleep loss and emotion: A systematic review and meta-analysis of over 50 years of experimental research. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000410 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38127505/ . Full author abstract directly inspected. Preregistered synthesis of 154 experimental studies, 5,717 participants aged 7–79, 1,338 effect sizes. Across sleep-loss paradigms, positive affect declined (SMD -0.27 to -1.14) and anxiety increased (0.57 to 0.63); emotional-stimulus arousal decreased (-0.20 to -0.53). Negative affect and depressive outcomes were mixed. These ranges are across paradigms, not confidence intervals. Experimental evidence supports effects of sleep loss on selected outcomes, not universal irritability or a specific person's relational intentions. Mixed age/sex population; female-specific moderators and precision not established here.

E2. Alzueta & Baker (2023), The Menstrual Cycle and Sleep. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38501513/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11562818/ . Indexed author abstract and selected full-text extracts; direct opens blocked. Review differentiates poorer perceived sleep in symptomatic or painful cycles from broadly stable objective sleep continuity in asymptomatic regular cycles, with phase-related EEG spindle changes. These are distinct outcomes. Narrative synthesis, not a pooled causal estimate. Age, symptoms and study methods limit generalization. No universal sleep disruption inferred.

E3. Menstrual variations of sleep–wake rhythms in healthy women (2024 online). https://doi.org/10.1007/s41105-024-00543-y . Publisher abstract, participant description and discussion excerpts directly inspected. Long-term wearable observations in ten Japanese university-recruited women, including four low-dose pill users. Sleep duration did not vary significantly by phase in the reported analysis, whereas sleep timing and activity-rhythm robustness varied. Basal temperature alone did not reliably resolve ovulation. Tiny heterogeneous sample, observational design and wearable measurement limit inference. Correlations with social jetlag do not establish direction or hormonal causation. Counterexample to equating every rhythmic difference with shorter sleep.

E4. Schmalenberger et al. (2019), A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Within-Person Changes in Cardiac Vagal Activity across the Menstrual Cycle. https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm8111946 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31726666/ . Indexed author abstract and full-text excerpts inspected; PubMed/PMC access failed and publisher returned 429. Repeated-measures synthesis of 37 studies, 1,004 individuals; average follicular-to-luteal decrease d=-0.39 (95% CI -0.67 to -0.11). Some component studies reported opposite directions. Phase ascertainment and metric heterogeneity matter. Average within-person contrast does not identify each person's response, a specific hormonal mechanism or emotion. Detailed moderator and bias analyses not verified.

E5. Schmalenberger et al. (2020), Menstrual Cycle Changes in Vagally-Mediated Heart Rate Variability Are Associated with Progesterone: Evidence from Two Within-Person Studies. https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm9030617 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32106458/ . Indexed author abstract and publisher methods/results extracts inspected. Two naturally cycling samples (40 weekly-assessed participants; 50 assessed in three ovulation-test-defined phases). Repeated person-centered progesterone/estradiol and cardiac HRV modeling; reported progesterone association with lower vagally mediated HRV. Observational association despite improved exposure timing and within-person centering; residual time-varying confounding, small samples and possible shared study lineage with E4 require caution. Exact coefficients and sensitivity estimates not independently checked.

E6. Liu et al. (2017), Sex differences in salivary cortisol reactivity to the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST): A meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2017.04.007 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28486178/ . Full author abstract directly inspected. 34 studies, 1,350 participants (640 women, 710 men); higher average male peak and recovery cortisol concentrations with significant heterogeneity. Acclimation, sampling and recovery timing moderated findings. Do not treat cortisol magnitude as overall stress, resilience or emotion intensity; task-specific physiology and study procedures limit transfer. Full effect and subgroup tables not inspected.

E7. Campbell & Ehlert (2012), Acute psychosocial stress: does the emotional stress response correspond with physiological responses? https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22260938/ . Indexed author abstract; direct open CAPTCHA. 49 studies, significant cortisol-emotional-stress associations in approximately one quarter of studies. This is a proportion of studies reporting significance, not a correlation coefficient or a proportion of people with discordant responses. Sampling time, statistical power and measures vary. Counterevidence to assuming reliable one-to-one mapping of physiological and subjective stress.

E8. Weber, Angerer & Apolinário-Hagen (2022), Physiological reactions to acute stressors and subjective stress during daily life: A systematic review on ecological momentary assessment (EMA) studies. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0271996 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35895674/ . Full author abstract directly inspected. 107 publications, 104 unique studies. Positive direct associations with concurrent cortisol in 11/25 studies, systolic pressure 8/18, diastolic pressure 8/15, heart rate 9/17. Multiple moderators including activity, chronic stress and resources; many studies had moderate confounding risk. Counts are not individual probabilities or pooled effect sizes. Naturalistic evidence cannot isolate causal direction, but challenges naive laboratory-to-daily-life transfer.

E9. Publication guidelines for human heart rate and heart rate variability studies in psychophysiology—Part 1 (2024). https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.14604 . Indexed publisher sections inspected; direct full-text open returned 403. Respiratory frequency/depth affect respiratory-linked HRV; concurrent respiration measurement supports interpretation. LF and LF/HF are not selective sympathetic indicators. The recommendation is methodological, not proof that a particular HRV value indicates emotion. Also directly inspected respiratory-control excerpts from Laborde, Mosley & Thayer (2017), https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00213 ; distinguishes monitoring respiration from routine statistical removal of potentially meaningful shared variance. Both support context-sensitive interpretation rather than blanket adjustment. No clinical device validation assessed.

## 3. Integrated framework
Separate stressor exposure, appraisal, experienced emotion, expression, HPA-axis response, autonomic cardiovascular response, sleep duration/timing/continuity/architecture, pain, and performance. HPA and autonomic response timing differs; cortisol has circadian and pulsatile variation, while cardiac measures vary with respiration, movement and posture. Heart-rate variability is not a direct measure of overall autonomic balance or a specific mental state. A wearable-derived metric is not equivalent to a validated clinical measurement. Distinguish between-person means, within-person deviations, response latency, recovery and sustained burden.

Candidate causal models (not identified DAGs):
- reproductive physiology -> somatic symptoms/sleep disruption -> affect or performance;
- endocrine variation x individual sensitivity -> selected emotional or physiological outcome;
- contextual demands/appraisal -> sleep and stress responses -> changes in attention/behavior;
- prior health, medication, habitual sleep and social context -> multiple observed variables;
- reciprocal dynamics, e.g. distress and disrupted sleep, with shared causes.
Do not automatically adjust for a mediator or condition on selection/collider variables; specify whether estimating total or direct effect. Concurrent associations and group means cannot establish individual mechanism. Prior WOM-06.1/06.2 findings concerning clinical hormonal sensitivity are compatible with null or mixed average cognitive/mood results and stage-related physiological variation.

Practical inference protocol:
1. Specify target construct and timescale before explanatory attribution; do not conflate tiredness, task capacity, motive and chosen action.
2. Establish only voluntarily available contextual or physiological data; unknown reproductive/health status remains unknown. No inference of phase, diagnosis, reproductive status or consent from social or physiological cues.
3. Evaluate exposure validity and plausible alternative pathways: sleep opportunity, pain, illness, daily demands, medications, stimulant use, activity, circadian timing, stable traits and relationship-specific context.
4. Distinguish observed repeated associations from causal mechanisms. Naturally occurring comparable observations can support or weaken a hypothesis, not guarantee unique cause. Consider reverse causality, lag, autocorrelation, measurement artifacts and prior baseline.
5. Treat biomarker/self-report disagreement as information requiring interpretation, not proof of deception or lack of emotional awareness. No stress biomarker alone can identify motive.
6. Apply population findings conditionally; effect estimates from mixed groups or selected clinical samples do not establish female-specific or individual effect sizes. Respect stated preferences and autonomy. No physiological discounting of an individual's judgment or consent.
7. Clinical symptoms, wearable abnormalities and treatment questions remain outside this generic analytic scope; appropriate clinical evaluation when warranted.

## 4. Internal transfer assessment
Rubric: one criterion each for construct separation, competing explanation, discriminating evidence, inference boundary. Critical errors: unsupported diagnosis, sex determinism, causal inflation, disregard of disconfirmation, coercion, privacy breach. Newly authored examples below; no independent or held-out assessment.

A. A participant sleeps four hours and reports little enjoyment of a hobby. Response: distinguish present affect from enduring preference; fatigue, pain, context and changing interests remain alternatives. Comparable observations after ordinary sleep recovery and stated preferences can discriminate; no conclusion about lasting motivation. Four criteria met.
B. In a laboratory sample, men have higher peak cortisol while women report greater anxiety. Response: biomarkers and reported anxiety are distinct outcomes; task appraisal, timing, assay and sampling differences matter. Assess time-aligned multimodal responses, not gender ranking or resilience. Four criteria met.
C. A wearable shows a lower HRV value on a day of intense exercise. Response: HRV is distinct from emotion; movement, respiration, posture, recovery and sensor artifacts compete. Compare validated recordings in equivalent resting contexts; no mental-state diagnosis. Four criteria met.
D. A study reports stable sleep duration and altered EEG spindle activity. Response: continuity and architecture differ; age, symptoms and methods may explain other studies' differences. Compare like endpoints and sampling; no universal sleep deterioration. Four criteria met.
E. An endocrine marker covaries with HRV, but not with self-reported mood. Response: physiological association does not establish a mood pathway; shared phase/context and measurement limitations remain. Assess repeated timing and relevant mediator measures; no inference of concealed emotion. Four criteria met.
F. Naturalistic stress and cortisol correlate in one interval but not another. Response: consider lag, circadian variation, activity and baseline; compare repeated matched measurements and precision. Null or inconsistent association weakens a simple universal mapping without proving no stress. Four criteria met.
