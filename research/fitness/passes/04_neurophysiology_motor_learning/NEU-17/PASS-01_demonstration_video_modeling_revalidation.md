## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a defensible observational-learning/modeling framework for later resistance-exercise coaching without:

- assuming demonstration is equivalent to physical practice;
- treating expert modeling as universally superior;
- treating peer/novice modeling as inherently inferior;
- treating self-modeling as universally superior or as durable learning by default;
- conflating self-modeling with ordinary video replay;
- treating video feedback as automatically better because it is visual;
- assuming slow motion is universally superior;
- assuming all demonstrations should occur only before practice;
- assuming observation between every attempt is universally optimal;
- showing a model without directing attention to task-relevant information;
- copying another lifter's exact joint geometry despite different segment lengths, mobility or task constraints;
- inferring durable motor learning from immediate visual-feedback performance changes.

### Primary downstream context

Healthy adult novice/intermediate resistance trainees learning or refining compound exercise technique.

### Critical outcomes

- movement-form acquisition;
- delayed retention;
- transfer;
- cue-free/model-free execution;
- learner error detection;
- technical understanding;
- acute performance;
- self-efficacy only as a secondary outcome.

## 2. Operational taxonomy

### Visual demonstration

Observation of another performance or movement representation intended to communicate task structure before/during practice.

### Expert model

Observation of a highly skilled/correct model.

### Peer / novice / learning model

Observation of a model closer to the learner's skill level, including imperfect/error-containing performance.

### Self-modeling

Observation of one's own performance or an edited/selected self-representation.

This is not identical to simple video feedback.

### Video feedback

Viewing one's own recent attempt to obtain information about current performance/error.

### Feedforward / enhanced self-modeling

A modified self-representation showing a higher level than the learner has actually demonstrated.

This is a separate and methodologically weaker literature.

Project rule:

**model identity, observed performance quality, video function and timing must remain separate variables.**

## 4. Visual demonstration

The 2022 systematic review (PMID 36011744):

- included 18 studies;
- used best-evidence synthesis;
- found **strong evidence** that observational learning improves motor-skill learning compared with no observational learning in PE contexts.

Observational learning can provide:

- task-goal representation;
- temporal/spatial relations;
- examples of successful coordination;
- error-detection information.

However observational practice is not equivalent to physical practice.

The 2024 reaching/adaptation review found:

- observational practice produced adaptation vs no practice;
- direct effects were smaller than physical practice;
- aftereffects were absent in the reviewed adaptation paradigms.

Therefore:

**demonstration can accelerate/shape learning, but it does not replace physical practice or establish the same internal adaptation process.**

Confidence: **HIGH for usefulness / HIGH against equivalence to physical practice**.

---

## 5. Expert model

An expert model provides:

- a stable task-successful solution;
- clearer timing/coordination relations;
- a reference of successful execution.

Evidence:

PMID 30872661:

- 60 participants;
- expert-model observation interspersed during acquisition;
- expert observation caused rapid improvement in movement planning;
- benefits persisted through 24-h retest;
- observation could condense acquisition relative to extra physical-practice control in that task.

PMID 21986667:

- expert observation supported retention/transfer;
- expert was more effective than novice-only observation in one sequential timing task;
- mixed expert+novice observation was best on some stability/generalization outcomes.

But the 2022 systematic review found **moderate evidence of no significant difference between expert model and self-model** in PE studies.

Canonical rule:

**expert modeling is useful for communicating a task-successful reference, but it is not universally superior to all other models.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 6. Peer / novice / learning model

A less-skilled model can provide information absent from a perfect expert demonstration:

- common errors;
- visible error correction;
- contrast between unsuccessful and successful attempts;
- possibly greater perceived similarity.

PMID 1574658:

- skilled-model and learning-model observation both improved later performance relative to no observation;
- no significant difference between model skill levels in that task.

PMID 21986667:

- novice-only observation was weaker than expert/mixed observation in one experiment;
- mixed expert+novice observation produced the most stable timing/generalization on some outcomes.

Thus:

**peer/novice models are not inherently inferior and may add error-comparison information, but a poor model without diagnostic framing can also transmit a poor reference.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for usefulness / LOW for universal model hierarchy**.

---

## 7. Expert + novice mixed modeling

Mixed observation can combine:

- a correct reference from expert performance;
- error-detection opportunities from novice performance.

PMID 21986667 directly supports this mechanism in a sequential timing task.

However:

- this is not a general meta-analytic rule;
- the optimal mix/frequency is not established;
- direct resistance-exercise evidence is sparse.

Project rule:

**mixed models are a plausible strategy when contrast between successful and unsuccessful execution teaches something task relevant.**

Status: **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC grounded in task-specific evidence**.

---

## 8. Self-modeling

Self-modeling uses the learner's own performance as the observed model.

Potential advantages:

- identity/similarity;
- direct mapping between observed and felt movement;
- visible progress;
- enhanced self-efficacy;
- easier comparison with prior attempts.

### 2026 meta-analysis

PMID 42688263:

- pooled post-test movement effect favored self-modeling:
  - SMD ≈ **0.81**;
  - 95% CI ≈ **0.28 to 1.33**;
- heterogeneity was high:
  - I² ≈ **73%**;
- 1-week follow-up did **not** show a significant persistent movement benefit:
  - SMD ≈ **0.59**;
  - 95% CI ≈ **−0.09 to 1.27**;
  - p ≈ **0.09**;
  - I² ≈ **70%**.

Thus post-test performance benefit does not establish durable learning.

### Current methodological challenge

Critical review published 2026-11-09 (DOI 10.3390/reports9040384):

- 18 primary studies in the core evidence base;
- only four used movement actually belonging to the participant;
- 16/17 appraised intervention studies were underpowered for a medium effect;
- assessor blinding was reported in only two;
- delayed retention/transfer in only seven.

The review concluded that current evidence does not support strong practice recommendations for feedforward/self-modeling variants.

Canonical rule:

**self-modeling is promising, but durability and methodological certainty are insufficient for universal superiority claims.**

Confidence: **MODERATE for immediate/post-test usefulness / LOW-MODERATE for durable superiority**.

## 9. Self-modeling ≠ video feedback

These must not be collapsed.

### Self-modeling
The learner observes themselves as a model to support learning/representation.

### Video feedback
The learner sees a recent attempt to detect current performance/error.

A video replay can function as both, but the **instructional purpose** differs.

Project rule:

**classify the function of the video, not merely the fact that a video is shown.**

Confidence: **HIGH taxonomy**.

## 10. Video feedback

Video feedback can provide movement information not reliably available from first-person proprioception/vision.

Direct squat evidence:

PMID 37916599:

- 30 healthy novices;
- own-performance video feedback after each set vs tactile feedback vs control;
- visual/video group improved several squat variables;
- selected changes persisted:
  - 3 days for neutral knee position;
  - 1 week for trunk lean, anterior knee displacement and AP COP;
- tactile group did not show comparable retention.

This is direct evidence that video feedback can support squat acquisition/retention.

But:

- n=10/group;
- one exercise;
- selected kinematic/COP outcomes;
- video was combined with information on what needed correction;
- no evidence that video is universally superior to all other feedback.

Canonical rule:

**video can make otherwise hidden movement information observable; its value depends on what the learner is asked to detect/change.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for novice squat under this protocol**.

---

## 11. Self-controlled / interactive video feedback

Video is not only a visual modality; how and when it is inspected matters.

PMID 42413171 (2026):

- 64 novice adults;
- dart throwing;
- compared self-controlled timing and interactive playback (pause/slow-motion/rewind);
- full-control group had the strongest and most durable gains at 24 and 72 h;
- yoked group with the same interactive functions performed worse.

This suggests:

**access to video functions alone is not sufficient; learner control over when/how the video is inspected may matter.**

But:

- one novice dart task;
- exploratory design;
- cannot define compound-lift video schedule.

Confidence: **MODERATE**.

---

## 12. Slow-motion review

Slow motion can make fast events easier to inspect.

It can help expose:

- timing relationships;
- joint/segment sequence;
- brief errors not visible at full speed.

But current evidence does not support:

`slow motion > real time`

as a universal learning rule.

PMID 33421652:

- 51 participants;
- slow-motion, real-time, or combined video demonstration;
- all groups improved a dance pirouette;
- no significant differences among video-speed conditions.

PMID 42413171 supports interactive playback as a package, but pause/slow-motion/rewind were not isolated from learner control.

Canonical rule:

**use slow motion for diagnostic visibility when it reveals a relevant relation; retain real-time viewing to preserve actual temporal structure.**

Status: **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**.

Confidence: **HIGH against universal slow-motion superiority**.

---

## 13. Observation before execution

Pre-practice demonstration can establish:

- task goal;
- gross movement sequence;
- spatial/temporal structure;
- safety/setup information.

PMID 10900708 compared demonstration schedules:

- all-pre-practice;
- interspersed;
- combined early pre-practice + interspersed observation.

All-pre-practice was useful, but the combined schedule produced the highest form scores during acquisition and retention.

Therefore:

**pre-practice observation is useful for initial representation, but “show once before practice and never again” is not a universal optimum.**

Confidence: **MODERATE**.

---

## 14. Observation between attempts

Interspersed observation can support:

- comparison between own attempt and model;
- updating error representation;
- refreshing task relations after physical practice.

PMID 30872661 showed step-wise improvement when expert observation was interspersed during acquisition.

PMID 10900708 found that a **combined** schedule—several demonstrations before practice plus several early in practice—outperformed all-pre and purely interspersed schedules for form in that task.

Canonical rule:

**observation can be repeated during practice when the learner has new error information to compare against the model; there is no universal “watch after every attempt” rule.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for usefulness / LOW for exact schedule**.

---

## 15. What exactly should the learner observe?

“Watch the model” is incomplete instruction.

Observation can be directed toward:

- task outcome;
- implement trajectory;
- timing relation;
- segment relation;
- critical event;
- error-success contrast;
- gaze behavior.

PMID 14620026:

- soccer pass;
- video modeling with combined visual + verbal cues produced less error and better form through retention than several comparison conditions.

PMID 27224057:

- visual guidance highlighted relevant golf-swing areas;
- guided learners looked more at those areas;
- swing execution improved at post-test and 1-week retention.

PMID 36435171:

- basketball free throw;
- externally directed observation and modeled gaze behaviors improved accuracy/quiet-eye metrics relative to comparison conditions in that task.

However NEU-14 remains binding:

**this does not re-establish universal external-focus superiority.**

Canonical rule:

**direct attention toward the task-relevant relation the demonstration is intended to teach; do not overload observation with many features.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 16. Observation can develop error-detection mechanisms

PMID 10994232 showed:

- observing a model allowed participants to estimate errors similarly to physical-practice participants in one task;
- observing a model receiving biased KR could bias the observer's reference/error-detection mechanisms.

This is important because modeling can teach:

- what “correct” looks like;
- what error looks like;
- how to compare performance.

It also shows risk:

**a misleading model/feedback stream can train a misleading reference.**

Canonical rule:

**the quality and correctness of the demonstrated relation matter; observation is not informationally neutral.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 17. Expert model ≠ one universal geometry

A skilled model demonstrates one successful solution.

Upstream NEU-09 established:

- motor abundance;
- multiple task-successful solutions.

Therefore the learner should generally copy:

- task constraints;
- outcome relations;
- critical safety/competition criteria;
- useful coordination relationships;

not necessarily every:

- joint angle;
- stance width;
- torso inclination;
- segment trajectory.

This is especially important in resistance exercise.

## 18. Anthropometry limits exact technique copying

Direct evidence supports a cautious, non-deterministic boundary.

### Squat

PMID 29997725:

- 32 healthy adults;
- stance width changed joint ROM;
- trunk/thigh and thigh/shank ratios were associated with ankle/knee angles under some stance conditions.

PMID 41196246:

- limited ankle dorsiflexion was associated with greater pelvis/hip/lumbar ROM and greater trunk lean during squat.

PMID 34712337:

- squat biomechanics related to strength/flexibility and some physical characteristics.

These findings establish that the same task can yield different movement geometry because of body proportions and mobility.

### Deadlift counterweight

PMID 37123458:

- small preliminary sample of trained men;
- arm, torso and shank length did **not** clearly predict most conventional-deadlift outcomes;
- thigh length related to one ankle-work outcome.

Thus anthropometry is **not** a deterministic formula for technique.

Canonical rule:

**do not copy another lifter's exact geometry solely because they are skilled; match the task constraints and evaluate the learner's own morphology, mobility, load and outcome.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH against exact geometric copying / LOW for deterministic anthropometric prescription**.

---

## 19. Camera angle / viewpoint

Video viewpoint can alter what information is visible.

PMID 37964620:

- 42 university students;
- learner-chosen video self-modeling;
- rear-angle group had faster acquisition in cup stacking;
- no retention benefit attributable to angle.

Therefore:

**camera angle can affect acquisition/visibility but is not automatically a learning enhancer.**

For lifting:

- sagittal view may reveal trunk/tibia/bar relations;
- frontal/rear view may reveal lateral symmetry/stance;
- no universal single camera angle is established.

Status: **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**.

---

## 20. Video resolution / measurement boundary

Ordinary video has limits.

Visual impression can be affected by:

- perspective;
- parallax;
- camera height;
- frame rate;
- lens distortion;
- occlusion;
- movement plane.

A 2D or markerless estimate can be useful but is not equivalent to laboratory 3D biomechanics.

Project rule:

**use video for coaching information at the precision it can support; do not infer exact joint kinetics or hidden causal mechanisms from appearance alone.**

Exact measurement validity belongs TST/MEC.

---

## 21. Direct resistance-exercise evidence boundary

Direct resistance-exercise observational evidence is much thinner than general sport/PE evidence.

The strongest direct learning evidence checked is:

- own-video feedback for squat acquisition/retention (PMID 37916599).

Other resistance-training video/visual studies often assess:

- acute kinematics;
- biofeedback;
- velocity;
- performance adaptation;

rather than pure demonstration-modeling retention/transfer.

Therefore:

**general observational-learning principles constrain RT coaching logic, but exact model type, frequency, camera angle and slow-motion schedule for compound lifts remain unestablished.**

Confidence: **HIGH for indirectness boundary**.

## 22. Practical modeling synthesis

A defensible future coaching heuristic is:

1. show a task-successful demonstration before first attempts when the movement is unfamiliar;
2. direct attention to one or a few task-relevant relations rather than “watch everything”;
3. combine observation with physical attempts;
4. repeat observation during early practice when the learner now has an attempt to compare;
5. use expert models for a clear successful reference;
6. use peer/novice/error examples when contrast teaches a relevant correction;
7. use self-video to compare intended vs actual execution;
8. use slow motion only when it reveals a relation hidden at real speed;
9. preserve real-time viewing for actual timing;
10. test retention without requiring the video/model;
11. copy task constraints and critical relations, not another person's exact anthropometric geometry.

Status:

**PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not a universal modeling algorithm.

## 24. Measurement boundaries

### Demonstration
Observation of a model to establish task representation.

### Video feedback
Observation of own recent attempt for error/performance information.

### Self-modeling
Own performance used as the modeled reference.

### Feedforward
Modified self-representation showing a not-yet-achieved performance.

### Expert/peer model
Model identity/skill level; separate from whether the specific shown trial is correct.

### Acquisition
Performance during observational/physical practice.

### Retention
Delayed performance without the model/video dependency being tested.

### Transfer
Performance in changed load/task/context.

### Model dependence
Failure to execute independently without repeated observation.

### Observation target
Specific relation/event the learner is instructed to inspect.
