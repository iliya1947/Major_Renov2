## 3. Core principle — anthropometry changes geometry, not destiny

Segment lengths and joint morphology alter:

- relative position of joint centers;
- required segment inclinations to keep center of mass/support relation;
- feasible ROM before another segment must compensate;
- external moment arms for a given implement/path;
- comfortable stance/grip/path options.

But exercise geometry also depends on:

- mobility/ROM;
- stance width and foot angle;
- heel elevation;
- bar/load location;
- task instructions;
- balance constraints;
- motor strategy;
- symptoms.

Project rule:

**anthropometry influences exercise geometry but does not uniquely prescribe one technique.**

---

## 4. Segment length

A segment length is the distance between defined anatomical reference points representing a body segment.

Relevant segments:
- upper arm;
- forearm;
- thigh;
- shank;
- trunk;
- hand/foot where task-specific.

Project rule:

**absolute stature is insufficient; relative segment proportions can matter mechanically even at similar height.**

Exact measurement protocol → TST.

---

## 5. Humerus / forearm proportion

Upper-arm and forearm lengths can change:

- elbow location relative to torso/load;
- wrist/hand position for a given shoulder angle;
- required shoulder/elbow excursion in pressing/pulling;
- external moment arms in curls/extensions;
- comfort of grips/implements.

But:

**there is no validated universal humerus:forearm ratio that dictates one correct grip or exercise.**

Exercise-specific implications → MEC/TEC.

---

## 6. Femur / tibia proportion

Femur and tibia lengths change lower-limb segment geometry.

In squat/lunge-like tasks they can influence:
- knee travel;
- trunk inclination needed for balance;
- hip position;
- feasible depth under a given stance/load setup.

However current evidence also shows squat kinematics are influenced by:
- ankle dorsiflexion;
- pelvic/spinopelvic geometry;
- stance/load modifications;
- motor strategy.

Project rule:

**femur:tibia ratio is one contributor, not a deterministic squat-technique classifier.**

A ratio alone does not justify:
- “you must squat wide”;
- “you cannot squat deep”;
- “long femurs require unsafe forward lean”.

---

## 7. Trunk length

Trunk length affects:
- center-of-mass distribution;
- required torso inclination;
- load-to-hip/knee geometry;
- reach and setup in pulling/pressing.

A longer or shorter trunk can interact with femur/tibia proportions.

Project rule:

**trunk length must be interpreted as part of a multi-segment system rather than in isolation.**

---

## 8. Pelvic and shoulder-girdle breadth

Relevant structural variables include:
- bi-iliac/bi-trochanteric dimensions;
- acetabular spacing/orientation;
- biacromial/shoulder breadth;
- ribcage/thoracic width;
- scapular/clavicular geometry.

These can affect:
- stance comfort;
- arm path around torso;
- grip width comfort;
- relative segment clearance.

But breadth alone does not prescribe:
- squat stance;
- bench grip;
- shoulder “safe zone”.

Exact mechanics → MEC/TEC.

---

## 9. Joint shape is individually variable

Joint geometry varies in healthy humans.

Examples already established in earlier ANA topics:
- glenoid/scapular morphology;
- femoral version;
- acetabular version/orientation;
- condylar/trochlear geometry;
- tibial slope;
- patellar morphology;
- ankle/foot morphology.

Current femoral-version literature shows:
- wide asymptomatic ranges;
- reported “normal” values depend materially on measurement method.

Current healthy-hip studies show:
- substantial variation in combined femoral/acetabular version.

Project rule:

**a single textbook angle is not a universal personal joint shape.**

---

## 10. Joint geometry can change comfortable ROM/path

Individual joint morphology can alter:
- available bony clearance;
- end-range orientation;
- comfortable stance/rotation;
- where soft-tissue/bony approximation occurs.

However:

**anatomical variation does not automatically establish pathology or exercise contraindication.**

A person’s comfortable path is an interaction of:
- bone geometry;
- soft tissue;
- neuromuscular control;
- task setup.

Diagnosis → MED.

---

## 11. Muscle attachment variability

Origin/insertion patterns can vary normally.

Current examples include:
- pes anserinus accessory bands/insertions;
- tibialis-anterior tendon insertion patterns;
- pectoralis-minor attachment variation;
- adductor-magnus insertion architecture;
- other accessory slips/tendons.

Project rule:

**atlas attachments are modal descriptions, not exact personal maps in every individual.**

But most anatomical-variant literature is:
- cadaveric;
- imaging/surgical;
- not direct exercise-outcome evidence.

Therefore attachment variation does **not** by itself justify exercise-targeting claims.

---

## 12. Muscle-belly / tendon length variability

Within the same named muscle-tendon unit, people can differ in:
- free-tendon length;
- intramuscular tendon length;
- muscle-belly length;
- MTJ location;
- fascicle architecture.

Current semitendinosus, semimembranosus and BFlh morphometry reinforces substantial human variability.

Potential consequences:
- visible contour;
- excursion/architecture;
- tendon leverage/strain distribution.

Project rule:

**visible “short/long muscle belly” is not a direct measure of force capacity or hypertrophy potential.**

Those require ANA-11/PHY/MEC evidence.

---

## 13. Muscle insertion site vs training-induced shape

Normal insertion location is an anatomical constraint.

Training can change:
- muscle size;
- regional size;
- architecture.

Training does not ordinarily relocate:
- the bone origin;
- the tendon insertion.

Project rule:

**individual attachment geometry may influence appearance and mechanics, but training cannot move an anatomical insertion to reshape a muscle arbitrarily.**

---

## 14. Carrying angle

Carrying angle is the frontal-plane valgus relation of arm/forearm around the elbow, typically assessed near extension.

It varies with:
- sex/group;
- side;
- age/sample;
- measurement method;
- elbow flexion position.

Current data show:
- population-level sex differences can exist;
- carrying angle decreases as the elbow flexes.

Project rule:

**carrying angle is a variable anatomical/positional measure, not one fixed universal number.**

It can affect visual elbow/forearm alignment but does not by itself diagnose deformity or dictate grip.

---

## 15. Right/left asymmetry

Humans are not perfectly bilaterally symmetric.

Normal side-to-side differences can occur in:
- bone geometry;
- segment dimensions;
- muscle size;
- tendon morphology;
- ROM;
- dominant-limb adaptations.

Project rule:

**small asymmetry is not pathology by default.**

Interpretation requires:
- measurement reliability;
- magnitude;
- symptoms/function;
- history;
- whether asymmetry is stable or changing.

Thresholds → TST/MED.

---

## 16. Exercise geometry — what anatomy can legitimately predict

Anatomy can justify **conditional** statements such as:

- longer femur relative to other segments may require a different trunk/knee strategy under a fixed squat setup;
- longer forearms may change bar/handle reach and elbow position;
- wider/narrower skeletal dimensions can alter clearance/comfort;
- joint version/morphology can change comfortable rotation/stance;
- tendon/belly proportions can change visible shape and local MTU geometry.

Anatomy cannot by itself justify:

- one universal stance;
- one universal grip width;
- one “perfect” squat;
- pathology from deviation from a model;
- hypertrophy superiority of an exercise.

Project rule:

**geometry informs options; biomechanics/technique evidence selects among them.**

---

## 17. Current squat evidence — anti-deterministic synthesis

Recent squat literature shows multiple interacting contributors:

- trunk/tibia inclination changes hip-vs-knee extensor moment distribution;
- limited ankle dorsiflexion changes trunk/pelvic strategy;
- heel elevation changes ankle/knee ROM;
- pelvic incidence relates to squat pelvic/lumbar/hip motion;
- body composition and movement strategy also associate with squat outcomes.

Project conclusion:

**no single anthropometric ratio is sufficient to classify a person’s optimal squat technique.**

This is a central ANA-15 fitness-use rule.

---

## 18. Growth and skeletal maturation

Longitudinal bone growth in children/adolescents occurs through epiphyseal growth plates.

Growth plates:
- are specialized cartilage structures;
- contain zonal chondrocyte organization;
- are progressively replaced by bone with maturation;
- ultimately fuse/close.

Current 2026 review emphasizes:
- endocrine regulation;
- growth-plate senescence;
- species differences;
- variable timing of maturation.

Project rule:

**skeletal maturity is not defined by one universal chronological age.**

Pediatric training safety/disease → MED/PRO.

---

## 19. Epiphyseal growth plates — project boundary

In immature skeleton:
- physis is structurally distinct from adult fused bone;
- some injuries/pathologies have different implications than in mature skeleton.

But ANA-15 does not infer:
- that resistance training inherently damages growth plates;
- that one age universally prohibits strength training.

Those are evidence/safety questions for MED/PRO.

---

## 20. Age-related skeletal-muscle structure

Human aging is commonly associated with:
- lower muscle volume/CSA;
- myofiber atrophy, especially affecting fast/type-II populations in many studies;
- changes in motor-system integrity;
- fatty infiltration/fibrosis in some contexts;
- reduced regenerative capacity.

Current human work shows:
- age-related muscle loss is multilevel;
- not every proposed tissue feature is universal.

Important 2026 systematic review:
**intramuscular collagen accumulation is not an inevitable universal feature of healthy chronological aging.**

Project rule:

**aging changes average tissue structure, but older age does not define one fixed muscle phenotype in every person.**

Mechanisms → PHY/NEU/MED.

---

## 21. Age-related tendon structure

Current evidence supports age-associated changes in tendon:
- cell responsiveness;
- ECM organization;
- collagen cross-linking patterns;
- regenerative capacity;
- mechanical properties.

But exact direction/magnitude can differ by:
- tendon type;
- loading history;
- health;
- measurement method.

Project rule:

**“all tendons simply become stiffer/looser with age” is not an acceptable universal anatomy statement.**

Detailed mechanics → MEC/PHY.

---

## 22. Age-related bone structure

Broad age-associated skeletal changes can include:
- declining bone mass after peak;
- trabecular thinning/loss/connectivity changes;
- increased cortical porosity;
- altered remodeling balance.

Current 3D human cortical-bone work supports age-related changes in remodeling-space morphology.

Project rule:

**chronological age is not a direct personal bone-quality measurement.**

Bone health/pathology → MED/TST.

---

## 23. Sex-related structural differences — group level

Population studies show average sex-related differences in:
- body size;
- long-bone dimensions;
- articular breadths;
- pelvic morphology;
- shoulder/scapular dimensions;
- some body proportions.

But magnitude varies with:
- ancestry/population;
- allometry/body size;
- environment/secular trends;
- measurement definition.

Current 2026 anthropometric data demonstrate that body proportions and dimorphism are population/context dependent.

Project rule:

**sex is a group-level predictor, not a substitute for measuring the individual.**

---

## 24. Sex / joint-geometry caution

Current healthy-joint data can show average sex associations in variables such as:
- femoral version;
- spinopelvic/femoroacetabular orientation;
- carrying angle.

But:
- within-sex ranges overlap;
- method matters;
- population matters.

Project rule:

**do not assign a personal joint angle, stance or exercise technique from sex category.**

---

## 25. Population and secular variation

Anthropometric proportions are not frozen universal human constants.

Large modern datasets show:
- population differences;
- secular changes across birth cohorts/time;
- different trends in height, breadth and girths.

Therefore:

**reference tables are population/protocol descriptors, not personal truth.**

This matters when using normative ratios.

---

## 26. Normal variation vs potential pathology

Normal variation can include:
- segment ratio differences;
- joint-version differences;
- attachment variants;
- carrying-angle differences;
- side asymmetry;
- body-proportion differences.

Potential medical concern is not defined by deviation from average alone.

Escalation becomes relevant when structural difference is accompanied by:
- pain;
- neurological signs;
- progressive loss of function;
- instability;
- trauma/deformity history;
- large/progressive unexplained asymmetry;
- clinically significant ROM loss.

Project rule:

**variation ≠ pathology; symptoms/function/history determine whether MED evaluation is needed.**

Exact red flags → MED.

---

## 27. What individual anatomy can change in exercise selection

Potentially relevant individualized variables:

1. segment lengths;
2. skeletal breadth;
3. joint version/morphology;
4. ROM associated with geometry/soft tissue;
5. tendon/muscle-belly proportions;
6. fixed attachment variants;
7. side-to-side structure.

These may change:
- comfort;
- achievable ROM;
- segment angles;
- external moment arms;
- implement clearance;
- stance/grip options.

They do **not** directly determine:
- adaptation response;
- muscle activation;
- injury risk;
- best exercise.

Those require downstream evidence.

## 28. Measurement priority for later IND/TST

For practical individualization, direct personal measurements are usually preferable to demographic guesses.

Priority examples:

1. standing/sitting height;
2. segment lengths;
3. shoulder/pelvic breadth where useful;
4. ROM;
5. symptom-free comfortable positions;
6. standardized photos/video;
7. only then specialized imaging if a real decision requires it.

Project rule:

**measure what changes the decision; do not image or quantify anatomy merely because it can be measured.**

## 31. Established findings

### HIGH

1. Segment proportions vary materially between individuals.
2. Relative segment geometry can alter feasible exercise kinematics and external moment arms.
3. No single segment ratio uniquely determines optimal exercise technique.
4. Healthy joint geometry shows substantial interindividual variation.
5. Reported joint “normal ranges” can depend on measurement method.
6. Muscle/tendon attachments and belly/tendon proportions can vary.
7. Small bilateral asymmetries can be normal.
8. Growth plates drive longitudinal growth in immature skeleton and fuse with maturation.
9. Aging changes average muscle/tendon/bone structure, but exact phenotype varies substantially.
10. Sex-related structural group means exist but overlap and are population/allometry dependent.
11. Individual measurement is more appropriate than demographic stereotyping for exercise individualization.
12. Anatomical deviation from average alone is not pathology.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

1. Magnitude of effect of a given segment ratio on squat/press/pull mechanics.
2. Exact exercise consequence of a specific joint-shape variant.
3. Exact functional effect of a muscle-attachment variant.
4. Exact age-related tendon mechanical phenotype.
5. Exact group-level sex difference for a specific anthropometric ratio across populations.
