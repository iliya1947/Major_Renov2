# PER-01 — Personality foundations: traits, states, situations, structure, stability/change, and measurement boundaries

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**
Scope: public personality-science evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## Purpose

SOC established that isolated social behavior does not transparently reveal personality.

PER begins from the unresolved dependency:

**When does repeated behavior justify trait inference, how should traits be separated from states and situations, what do Big Five and HEXACO structures actually represent, and what kinds of stability/change can personality measures support?**

High-risk errors to prevent:
- "one act reveals a trait";
- "trait = behavior";
- "trait = fixed essence";
- "high trait score means the person behaves that way in every situation";
- "state and trait are interchangeable";
- "Big Five are five biological modules";
- "HEXACO is simply Big Five plus Honesty–Humility";
- "one framework is universally the true personality taxonomy";
- "domain score captures the whole trait";
- "same trait name means same construct across inventories";
- "rank-order stability means nobody changes";
- "mean-level change means every individual changes that way";
- "stable variance means no individual change";
- "adult personality is fixed after age 25";
- "self-report is the ground truth";
- "informant report is objective truth";
- "self–informant disagreement is measurement failure";
- "cross-cultural mean differences are interpretable after translation alone";
- "high Honesty–Humility = moral goodness";
- "low Agreeableness/Honesty–Humility = diagnosis";
- "personality correlation with an outcome proves causal mechanism".

## Core rule

**A personality trait is a relatively enduring individual-difference tendency in distributions of thoughts, feelings, motives, and behaviors across time and situations. It is probabilistic, not deterministic; descriptive, not automatically mechanistic; and distinct from the momentary state observed at any one occasion.**

Canonical reasoning path:

\`repeated observations across representative contexts -> separate state and situation -> choose trait model and level -> establish reliability/validity -> estimate cross-situational and temporal consistency -> compare self/informant evidence -> distinguish kinds of stability/change -> make probabilistic trait inference with uncertainty\`

## 1. Personality is broader than trait scores

Personality science can study:
- dispositional traits;
- momentary personality states;
- characteristic adaptations such as goals, values, motives, roles, and coping patterns;
- self/narrative organization;
- person–situation transactions.

PER-01 focuses primarily on dispositional traits and states.

A Big Five or HEXACO profile is therefore **a trait description**, not a complete psychological model of a person.

## 2. Trait versus state

A **trait** describes relatively stable individual differences in typical tendencies across time/context.

A **state** describes thoughts, feelings, behaviors, or goals at a particular time.

The 2025 review of personality states defines states at the occasion level and reports both convergence and meaningful differences between average states and traits.

Therefore:
**current state ≠ trait**.

## 3. Trait is not an invariant behavior

A highly extraverted person can sometimes be quiet.
A low-extraversion person can sometimes be socially active.

Trait differences concern:
- average levels;
- probabilities;
- frequencies;
- distributions;
- context-dependent tendencies.

They do not require the same behavior at every occasion.

## 4. Trait scores summarize distributions

A useful conceptual model is:

\`observed state = person tendency + situation effect + Person×Situation response + measurement/error components\`

This is not a complete generative model, but it blocks the essential error:

**one observed state cannot identify the latent trait uniquely**.

## 5. Aggregation improves trait inference

Repeated observations across:
- occasions;
- situations;
- methods

reduce the influence of:
- transient state;
- idiosyncratic event;
- measurement noise.

Trait inference should therefore become stronger when evidence is:
- repeated;
- representative;
- cross-context;
- convergent.

One vivid episode is weak evidence for a broad trait.

## 6. Representative sampling matters

Ten observations from one setting can be less informative than fewer observations across relevant settings.

Examples of context restriction:
- work-only behavior;
- family-only behavior;
- high-stress-only behavior;
- online-only behavior.

A trait claim should match the behavioral universe actually sampled.

## 7. Personality states fluctuate substantially

The 2025 personality-states review emphasizes:
- meaningful within-person state fluctuations;
- convergence between average states and traits;
- significant differences between them;
- possible contribution of repeated state patterns to long-term trait change.

Thus within-person variability is part of personality functioning, not merely measurement noise.

## 8. Average state and questionnaire trait are related but not identical

A questionnaire trait can reflect:
- self-knowledge across many situations;
- memory/generalization;
- self-schema;
- response style.

An average sampled state reflects:
- the sampled situations;
- measurement period;
- state instrument;
- event distribution.

Agreement should be expected, but perfect equivalence should not.

## 9. Situation characteristics matter

Situations differ in psychologically relevant characteristics such as:
- task demand;
- sociality;
- threat/adversity;
- positivity/negativity;
- deception;
- intellectual demand;
- duty;
- mating/affiliation cues.

One widely used descriptive framework is DIAMONDS:
- Duty;
- Intellect;
- Adversity;
- Mating;
- pOsitivity;
- Negativity;
- Deception;
- Sociality.

This is a situation taxonomy, not a complete causal model.

## 10. Objective and perceived situations differ

A situation can be described by:
- external features;
- consensus ratings;
- the person's subjective perception.

Perceived situation characteristics may partly reflect:
- actual context;
- current state;
- expectations;
- personality.

Thus:
**perceived situation ≠ purely external environment**.

## 11. Person × Situation interaction

A Person×Situation interaction means that:
- people differ in how they respond to situations;
or
- trait–state relations differ across situations.

This is broader than one simple Trait×Situation product term.

## 12. Four P×S interaction types

The 2024 preregistered framework tested four related forms:
1. broad Person×Situation variance in personality states;
2. individual differences in situation-characteristic → state contingencies;
3. between-situation differences in trait → state associations;
4. specific Personality Trait × Situation Characteristic moderator effects.

These should not be collapsed into one "interaction effect."

## 13. Large broad interaction variance does not imply large named interactions

In two studies:
- N=622;
- N=818;
- 62 picture situations;
- 62 first-person video situations,

researchers found:
- large general P×S variance;
- sizeable individual differences in situation→state contingencies;
- smaller between-situation trait–state differences;
- some statistically significant but **very small** specific Trait×Situation Characteristic effects.

Therefore:
**people differ in context sensitivity, but simple named trait×situation rules may explain little of that variation.**

## 14. "If–then" patterns require reliability

A pattern such as:
- "if criticized, then becomes withdrawn"

can be a meaningful person-specific hypothesis.

But stable contingency claims require:
- enough repeated situations;
- reliable situation measurement;
- repeated state measurement;
- test–retest evidence.

A memorable anecdotal contingency is not yet a personality signature.

## 15. Person–situation transactions

Personality and environments can become correlated through:
- **selection** — choosing situations;
- **evocation** — eliciting responses from others;
- **modification** — changing situations;
- **reaction** — responding differently to the same situation.

Thus trait–outcome correlations can partly reflect environmental pathways.

## 16. Trait → outcome is rarely one-way

Example analytic alternatives:

\`trait -> situation selection -> behavior -> outcome\`

\`environment -> repeated states -> trait change\`

\`trait <-> social response\`

A trait association with life outcomes does not establish one direct causal mechanism.

## 17. Big Five / Five-Factor Model

Broad Big Five domains commonly include:
- Neuroticism / Emotional Stability;
- Extraversion;
- Openness to Experience / Intellect;
- Agreeableness;
- Conscientiousness.

These broad dimensions summarize recurring covariance in personality descriptors/items.

They are useful taxonomic dimensions.

They are not five independent biological organs or causal modules.

## 18. Factor structure is descriptive

Factor models answer:
**Which individual-difference indicators covary?**

They do not by themselves answer:
- why the covariance exists;
- which biological mechanism causes it;
- whether one domain is a unitary mechanism.

A latent trait factor is a statistical/psychological construct, not automatically a biological entity.

## 19. Broad domains have facets

Big Five instruments differ in lower-order organization.

Examples across systems include:
- aspects;
- facets;
- narrower subtraits.

The exact facet structure is instrument/model dependent.

A broad domain score can hide meaningful lower-level differences.

## 20. Personality nuances

Research increasingly treats individual questionnaire items or very narrow tendencies as possible **personality nuances**.

A 2025 HEXACO meta-analysis found item-level residuals retained:
- cross-rater agreement;
- heritable variance;
- rank-order stability

beyond broad domains/facets.

Thus narrow trait information should not automatically be dismissed as error.

## 21. Narrower is not automatically better

Facets/nuances can:
- improve criterion prediction;
- increase specificity.

But they also introduce:
- more parameters;
- lower individual-item reliability;
- multiple-comparison risk;
- overfitting;
- instrument dependence.

Use the level matching the question.

## 22. Same domain label can hide different content

"Agreeableness" is not operationalized identically across all Big Five inventories.

A 2025 review highlights substantial differences among Big Five Agreeableness operationalizations and warns about **jingle fallacies**:
same name, meaningfully different content.

Canonical rule:
**construct name ≠ construct equivalence**.

## 23. HEXACO structure

HEXACO commonly includes:
- Honesty–Humility;
- Emotionality;
- eXtraversion;
- Agreeableness versus Anger;
- Conscientiousness;
- Openness to Experience.

It is another broad trait taxonomy with lexical roots.

## 24. HEXACO is not simply Big Five + Honesty–Humility

Some dimensions correspond closely, especially:
- Extraversion;
- Conscientiousness;
- Openness.

But mappings involving:
- Big Five Agreeableness;
- Big Five Neuroticism;
- HEXACO Honesty–Humility;
- HEXACO Agreeableness;
- HEXACO Emotionality

are more complex.

Re-rotation research shows the personality spaces can be related without becoming identical.

## 25. Honesty–Humility is descriptive, not a moral verdict

Honesty–Humility covers tendencies related to content such as:
- sincerity;
- fairness;
- greed avoidance;
- modesty.

A low score is not by itself:
- dishonesty proof;
- immoral character;
- a diagnosis.

Trait scores are probabilistic summaries under a particular instrument.

## 26. Big Five versus HEXACO is not a universal winner contest

Framework choice depends on:
- target construct;
- criterion;
- theoretical question;
- instrument quality;
- population;
- cultural validity.

HEXACO can capture some content not well represented by some Big Five measures, especially prosocial/ethicality-related variance.

This does not make HEXACO universally superior for every purpose.

## 27. Jingle and jangle fallacies

**Jingle:** same label assumed to mean same construct.

**Jangle:** different labels assumed to mean different constructs.

Personality research is especially vulnerable because:
- inventories use overlapping language;
- facets differ;
- broad factors rotate differently.

Construct comparison requires item/content/nomological evidence.

## 28. Rank-order stability

Rank-order stability asks:

**Do people preserve their relative ordering compared with others over time?**

High rank-order stability does not mean:
- scores do not change;
- everyone is stable in absolute level;
- mean levels remain constant.

It concerns relative position.

## 29. Mean-level change

Mean-level change asks:

**Does the population average of a trait increase or decrease?**

A group average can change while individual rank order remains stable.

Example:
everyone increases similarly -> mean changes, rank order can remain high.

## 30. Rank-order stability and mean-level change can coexist

The 2022 meta-analysis included:
- 189 samples/studies for rank-order stability;
- N=178,503;
- 276 for mean-level change;
- N=242,542.

It found:
- rank-order stability increased in early life;
- plateaued around young adulthood;
- little evidence for further increases after 25;
- mean-level changes broadly followed maturity-related trends;
- cumulative mean changes were smaller than older estimates for many traits.

Personality can therefore be both stable and changing.

## 31. "After 25 personality is fixed" is false

The 2022 meta-analysis found a plateau in **rank-order stability**, not absence of change.

Mean-level changes still occur.

The 2026 meta-analysis further found significant individual differences in trait change across the entire lifespan.

Therefore:
**rank-order plateau ≠ immutable personality**.

## 32. Individual differences in change

Two people with the same baseline trait can change differently.

The 2026 meta-analysis integrated:
- 229 longitudinal studies;
- N=230,492.

Significant individual differences in change appeared for all traits.

They were:
- largest in childhood;
- smaller in young adulthood;
- still significant across the entire lifespan.

## 33. Trait-specific change heterogeneity

The 2026 change meta-analysis found:
- neuroticism showed the largest individual differences in change;
- openness and agreeableness showed smaller differences;
- facet-level traits showed larger individual-change variance than broad domains.

Thus one universal "personality plasticity" coefficient is inadequate.

## 34. Population variance change is another question

Trait variance asks:
**Does the spread between people increase or decrease with age?**

This is separate from:
- mean change;
- rank-order stability;
- individual slope variance.

## 35. Population variance is mostly stable

A 2026 meta-analysis of:
- 362 longitudinal studies;
- N=398,124

found personality-trait variance was remarkably stable across much of the lifespan.

Exceptions included:
- increasing neuroticism variance in late childhood/early adolescence;
- decreasing extraversion variance in middle/late adulthood.

This does **not** imply individuals do not change.

## 36. Stable variance can coexist with individual change

If some people increase and others decrease, population variance can remain similar.

Therefore:
**variance stability ≠ individual stability**.

Different developmental statistics answer different questions.

## 37. Profile / ipsative stability

Profile stability asks whether a person's relative configuration across traits is preserved.

For example:
- Extraversion higher than Conscientiousness within the same person across time.

This differs from normative rank-order stability.

A person can:
- preserve their trait profile shape;
- while changing absolute levels;
or vice versa.

## 38. Normative versus ipsative interpretation

**Normative score:** compares a person with a reference population.

**Ipsative/profile comparison:** compares traits within the same person.

If a person's highest trait is Openness, that does not mean:
- Openness is high relative to the population.

"Highest within profile" and "high normatively" are different claims.

## 39. Reliable/absolute change

Observed score change contains:
- true change;
- measurement error;
- occasion effects.

Individual change inference should consider:
- scale reliability;
- standard error;
- measurement invariance;
- retest effects.

A numerical difference between two scores is not automatically meaningful change.

## 40. Measurement invariance across time

Longitudinal comparison assumes the scale measures the construct comparably across occasions.

Without adequate longitudinal invariance:
- apparent mean change;
- slope differences

can partly reflect measurement change.

Psychometric comparability is required before developmental interpretation.

## 41. Life events do not produce one uniform trait change

Life-event effects on personality are often:
- small;
- heterogeneous;
- inconsistent across studies/events.

Current 2026 work emphasizes individual differences in responses to life events.

Thus:
**event X -> trait Y change**
should not be treated as deterministic.

## 42. Personality change can predict outcomes without revealing mechanism

Longitudinal research shows trait change can prospectively relate to changes in:
- health;
- education;
- work;
- relationships;
- civic outcomes

in some datasets.

But a trait-change association does not identify:
- causal direction;
- intervening behavior;
- environmental mechanism.

Prediction and explanation remain distinct.

## 43. Self-report personality

Self-report can access:
- private thoughts/feelings;
- broad history across situations;
- self-concept.

Potential limitations:
- self-enhancement;
- impression management;
- memory;
- response styles;
- limited self-knowledge;
- reference-group effects.

Self-report is evidence, not absolute ground truth.

## 44. Observer/informant report

Observers can access:
- repeated outward behavior;
- interpersonal impact;
- comparative impressions.

Limitations:
- restricted contexts;
- halo;
- stereotypes;
- relationship bias;
- limited access to private experience.

Observer report is not inherently more objective.

## 45. Self–observer convergence

The 2025 Annual Review reports generally substantial self–observer convergence for full-length Big Five/HEXACO assessments, while agreement varies by trait.

Cooperation-related traits can show somewhat lower convergence.

This supports:
**multiple perspectives can converge meaningfully without being interchangeable**.

## 46. Disagreement can be informative

Self–informant disagreement may reflect:
- different contexts observed;
- observability of the trait;
- self-knowledge differences;
- assumed similarity;
- evaluative bias;
- method effects.

Do not automatically average disagreement away or call one reporter wrong.

## 47. Observability matters

Traits expressed overtly in behavior can be easier for observers to judge.

Internal/evaluative content can be harder.

Agreement should therefore be interpreted trait-by-trait and reporter-by-reporter.

## 48. Acquaintanceship matters

An observer who sees a person:
- rarely;
- in one role;
- in one emotional context

has limited sampling.

More familiarity can improve information but may also introduce:
- relationship-specific expectations;
- shared-context bias.

"Close informant" is not automatically unbiased.

## 49. Social desirability versus substantive trait variance

A socially valued response can arise from:
- genuine trait;
- self-presentation;
- norm awareness.

Scales need designs/validity evidence to distinguish substantive variance from response distortion.

A desirable answer is not automatically fake.

## 50. Faking/impression management is a context effect

Applicants or high-stakes respondents may alter responses strategically.

But:
- faking ability varies;
- not all score elevation is intentional deception;
- contextual self-presentation can itself be meaningful behavior.

High-stakes personality testing needs separate validity evidence.

## 51. Short scales trade bandwidth for efficiency

Very short measures can be useful when:
- burden is critical;
- only broad rank ordering is needed.

But fewer items can reduce:
- reliability;
- facet coverage;
- nuance representation.

A two-item domain score should not support the same inference as a well-validated full inventory.

## 52. Measurement framework and instrument are separate

"Big Five" does not name one test.

"HEXACO" does not name one test.

Different inventories vary in:
- item wording;
- facet content;
- response format;
- normative samples;
- reliability.

Framework-level validity does not automatically transfer to every questionnaire.

## 53. Cross-cultural transport is a major limit

The 2026 scoping review examined:
- 233 publications

across traits, characteristic adaptations, and narratives.

It found no reviewed personality instrument with full cross-cultural scalar invariance and cautioned against average-level cross-cultural comparisons.

Thus:
**translation ≠ cross-cultural comparability**.

## 54. Structural replication is weaker than scalar comparability

A scale may show:
- similar factor structure;
- similar loadings;

yet still lack comparable intercepts/thresholds needed for mean comparisons.

Therefore:
**configural/metric invariance ≠ scalar invariance**.

## 55. Specific instruments can show local invariance

A 2025 HEXACO Adjective Scale study found strong invariance across specific:
- Dutch;
- French;
- English

versions.

This is evidence for that instrument/language set.

It does not override the broader 2026 finding that full scalar invariance is generally lacking across the field.

## 56. Cultural mean differences require restraint

Possible sources of score differences include:
- true trait differences;
- translation;
- reference-group effects;
- response styles;
- item familiarity;
- differential item functioning.

Without appropriate invariance/validation, causal cultural interpretations are especially weak.

## 57. Trait–criterion prediction is probabilistic

Traits can predict:
- performance;
- health behavior;
- relationships;
- life outcomes

at population level.

But prediction is:
- probabilistic;
- criterion-specific;
- moderated by situation/opportunity.

A trait score is not a deterministic forecast.

## 58. Trait prediction is not mechanism

Example:

Conscientiousness may correlate with an outcome.

Possible mediators:
- planning;
- persistence;
- rule adherence;
- health behavior;
- situation selection.

The broad trait does not identify which mechanism operated.

## 59. Same trait score can arise from different profiles

Two people can have similar broad-domain scores but different:
- facets;
- nuances;
- behavior patterns;
- contexts.

Broad-score equality does not imply psychological equivalence.

## 60. Big Five / HEXACO domains are not diagnoses

Normative trait models describe individual differences.

They do not by themselves establish:
- psychopathology;
- personality disorder;
- functional impairment.

Clinical personality pathology belongs to CLN/PSYCHOMETRICS interfaces.

## 61. Moral labels must not be inferred from trait scores

Examples of invalid translations:
- low Agreeableness -> "bad person";
- low Honesty–Humility -> "liar";
- high Neuroticism -> "weak";
- low Extraversion -> "antisocial".

These are moral/clinical leaps beyond trait measurement.

## 62. Digital/AI personality inference boundary

Algorithms may predict questionnaire labels from:
- language;
- digital traces;
- behavior.

Performance against questionnaire scores does not prove:
- latent personality essence decoded;
- cross-context validity;
- causal mechanism;
- individual certainty.

External validation and consent/context remain separate issues.

## 63. Trait inference from social media is context-restricted

Online behavior is selected by:
- platform norms;
- audience;
- incentives;
- self-presentation;
- algorithmic exposure.

A digital trace can predict a trait measure while failing to represent offline behavior broadly.

The sampled behavioral universe matters.

## 64. Trait inference should match the consequence level

For low-stakes descriptive reasoning:
- one validated questionnaire may be sufficient for a tentative summary.

For high-stakes person-level decisions:
- reliability;
- multiple methods;
- relevant criterion validity;
- uncertainty;
- population norms

become more important.

Measurement quality should scale with consequence.

## 65. Personality Foundations Inference Gate — 60 questions

### A. Observation and trait target
1. What exact behavior/state was observed?
2. Is the claim about a state or a trait?
3. Is the proposed trait broad domain, facet, or nuance?
4. Was behavior repeated across occasions?
5. Were situations representative of the claimed trait domain?
6. Could a temporary state explain the observation?
7. Could role, incentive, skill, or opportunity explain it?
8. Is one vivid event being over-weighted?

### B. Situation and transaction
9. What situation characteristics are present?
10. Is perceived situation separated from objective context?
11. Could the person have selected the situation?
12. Could the person have evoked responses from others?
13. Could the person have modified the environment?
14. Is a Person×Situation effect plausible?
15. Is a specific Trait×Situation effect actually supported?
16. Is an "if–then" pattern reliable across repeated observations?
17. Is the same behavior expected across all contexts without evidence?

### C. Trait structure
18. Which framework is used: Big Five, FFM, HEXACO, or another?
19. Which exact instrument operationalizes the trait?
20. Is the broad domain being treated as a mechanism?
21. Are facets/aspects relevant?
22. Could nuances add criterion-specific information?
23. Is a jingle fallacy possible?
24. Is a jangle fallacy possible?
25. Is Big Five Agreeableness assumed equivalent across instruments?
26. Is HEXACO being treated as merely Big Five + Honesty–Humility?
27. Is Honesty–Humility being moralized?

### D. Stability and change
28. Is the question about rank-order stability?
29. Is it about mean-level change?
30. Is it about individual slope/change variance?
31. Is it about population variance?
32. Is it about profile/ipsative stability?
33. Is observed score change larger than measurement error?
34. Is longitudinal measurement invariance adequate?
35. Is rank-order stability being mistaken for no change?
36. Is mean-level change being assigned to every individual?
37. Is stable variance being mistaken for stable people?
38. Is age 25 being treated as a hard fixation point?
39. Is follow-up long enough for the claim?

### E. Self and observer measurement
40. Is self-report the relevant information source?
41. Is observer report based on enough contexts?
42. Is self–observer disagreement being treated automatically as error?
43. Could observability explain reporter differences?
44. Could assumed similarity/halo affect informant ratings?
45. Could social desirability/impression management affect self-report?
46. Is a short scale being used for a high-resolution inference?
47. Is reliability adequate at the domain/facet level?
48. Is criterion validity known for the intended outcome?

### F. Culture and score interpretation
49. Was the instrument validated in the target language/population?
50. Is measurement invariance established?
51. Is scalar invariance adequate for mean comparisons?
52. Is translation being treated as full cultural validity?
53. Is a normative score being confused with within-person profile rank?
54. Is a group mean being transferred to an individual?
55. Are population norms appropriate?

### G. Inference
56. Is trait prediction being interpreted as causal mechanism?
57. Is a trait score being treated as diagnosis or moral character?
58. Could another personality level/model explain the pattern?
59. Which competing state/situation/method explanation remains?
60. What is the narrowest probabilistic trait conclusion justified by observations and measurement?

## 66. Failure modes

A. One behavior -> stable trait: **invalid**.

B. Trait = behavior on every occasion: **invalid**.

C. State = trait: **invalid**.

D. Trait score = causal mechanism: **invalid**.

E. Big Five = five biological modules: **invalid**.

F. HEXACO = Big Five + H only: **overcompression**.

G. Same trait label = same construct across inventories: **jingle fallacy risk**.

H. Domain score = whole personality: **invalid**.

I. Rank-order stability = no personality change: **invalid**.

J. Mean-level change = every individual changes that way: **invalid**.

K. Stable population variance = individuals do not change: **invalid**.

L. Personality fixed after 25: **invalid**.

M. Self-report = ground truth: **invalid**.

N. Observer report = objective truth: **invalid**.

O. Reporter disagreement = measurement failure: **invalid universal claim**.

P. Translation = cross-cultural equivalence: **invalid**.

Q. High Honesty–Humility = moral goodness: **invalid moral inference**.

R. Low trait score = diagnosis: **invalid clinical leap**.

S. Trait–outcome correlation = causal pathway: **invalid**.

T. AI/digital prediction = personality mind-reading: **invalid construct leap**.

## 67. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries
- traits and states are distinct but related;
- traits are probabilistic tendencies, not invariant behaviors;
- within-person state variation is substantial;
- broad P×S interaction variance exists;
- specific named Trait×Situation interactions are often small;
- Big Five/HEXACO are descriptive trait taxonomies, not biological modules;
- broad domains, facets, and nuances are distinct levels;
- rank-order stability and mean-level change answer different questions;
- significant individual differences in trait change exist across the lifespan;
- self and observer reports are complementary rather than absolute gold standards;
- cross-cultural mean comparisons require measurement invariance.

### Supported but context-sensitive
- average states converge with questionnaire traits;
- repeated state patterns may contribute to trait change;
- facets/nuances can add criterion prediction;
- Big Five and HEXACO cover overlapping but nonidentical trait spaces;
- self–observer agreement is substantial for many broad traits;
- life events can relate to personality change for some people/events;
- personality changes can prospectively relate to life-outcome changes.

### Not established as universal
- one best personality taxonomy for every purpose;
- one stable person-specific "if–then" signature from sparse data;
- one biological mechanism corresponding to each broad trait;
- one invariant age at which personality becomes fixed;
- one reporter as the true personality source;
- universal scalar invariance across cultures;
- trait score as deterministic life-outcome forecast.

## Canonical operating rule

**Sample behavior across representative contexts -> separate momentary state and situation from trait -> select a validated trait framework and level (domain/facet/nuance) -> test reliability and reporter/cultural validity -> model Person×Situation and person–environment transactions -> distinguish rank-order, mean-level, individual, variance, and profile change -> interpret trait scores probabilistically rather than mechanistically -> state only the narrowest supported personality inference.**

Never:
**one act = trait; trait = fixed behavior; factor = mechanism; Big Five/HEXACO = biological modules; rank stability = no change; mean change = individual destiny; self/informant = truth; translated scale = comparable scale; trait score = diagnosis or moral character.**
