# Part A — Human mechanistic perturbation: canonical structure

## 1. Core structure

### Project synthesis
**Confidence: HIGH**

Human experimental mechanistic perturbation deliberately changes one or more controllable system inputs/components and measures whether the observed response matches a prespecified mechanism hypothesis.

Canonical:

`assign/manipulate A → verify perturbation/target engagement M → observe mechanistic/downstream response Y`

The manipulated variable can be:

- environmental;
- behavioral;
- mechanical;
- nutritional;
- physiological;
- pharmacological;
- device-based.

---

## 2. Intervention can be a treatment OR an experimental probe

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Mechanistic human experiments do not require the intervention to be intended as therapy.

Examples of probe-like manipulations:

- hypoxia;
- sleep deprivation;
- high-sodium exposure;
- nutrient challenge;
- exercise bout;
- exercise-load change;
- pharmacological receptor blockade;
- neural stimulation.

### Project rule

Classify:

> **what is experimentally manipulated and what mechanism hypothesis it tests**,

not merely:

> “intervention study”.

---

# Part B — BESH vs mechanistic clinical trial

## 3. Current NIH BESH

### Current administrative definition
**Confidence: HIGH**

For current NIH application classification:

BESH:

- involves human participants;
- prospectively assigns/manipulates conditions;
- assesses biomedical/behavioral outcomes;
- aims at fundamental understanding;
- lacks direct specific health-process/product application.

After 2026-05-25:

> BESH is not an NIH clinical trial.

---

## 4. Mechanistic clinical trial

### Current scientific/NIH-institute usage
**Confidence: HIGH**

Mechanistic clinical trial:

- is conducted in humans;
- prospectively assigns intervention where the clinical-trial definition applies;
- targets a biological/behavioral process, pathophysiology or mechanism of action;
- has direct enough health/intervention relevance to remain within the clinical-trial framework.

### Project rule

Do not distinguish BESH vs mechanistic clinical trial from:

- randomization;
- crossover;
- sample size;
- biomarker presence.

The key current NIH boundary is primarily:

> **fundamental phenomenon without direct health application**  
vs  
> **health-related mechanistic investigation fitting current clinical-trial definition**.

---

## 5. BESH and mechanistic clinical trial are administrative/scientific-purpose metadata

They are **not** replacements for structural design labels.

A BESH can be:

- parallel;
- crossover;
- factorial;
- within-person.

A mechanistic clinical trial can use the same structures.

# Part C — Mechanistic hypothesis must be perturbation-testable

## 6. Minimum hypothesis architecture

Before the experiment:

1. define manipulation A;
2. define proposed target/process M;
3. define downstream readout Y;
4. predict direction/timing of M;
5. predict consequence for Y;
6. define plausible competing explanation(s).

### Example

Hypothesis:

> group III/IV muscle afferent feedback contributes to cardiovascular regulation during exercise.

Perturbation:

> attenuate afferent feedback.

Prediction:

> selected cardiovascular response should change.

This is more mechanistically informative than merely observing that afferent activity correlates with cardiovascular output.

---

## 7. Outcome must discriminate the mechanism hypothesis

### Current NHLBI principle
**Confidence: HIGH**

A mechanistic experiment should not merely measure many biomarkers.

Outcomes should be selected because they can distinguish:

- the hypothesized mechanism;
- credible competing mechanisms;
- successful vs unsuccessful target perturbation.

### Project rule

“More biomarkers” ≠ “more mechanistic”.

---

# Part D — Target engagement

## 8. Target engagement asks whether A actually changed M

Canonical:

`A → M`

Examples:

- receptor occupancy;
- pathway inhibition;
- reduced afferent feedback;
- altered substrate availability;
- changed oxygen tension;
- changed motor-unit input;
- changed signaling target.

---

## 9. Target engagement requires a valid assay/readout

### Established principle
**Confidence: HIGH**

A mechanism test becomes uninterpretable if the experiment cannot tell whether the intended target changed.

Need, where applicable:

1. target assay validity;
2. correct tissue/compartment;
3. correct timing;
4. sufficient sensitivity;
5. dose/exposure confirmation;
6. intervention fidelity.

---

## 10. Target engagement ≠ downstream mechanism proof

If:

`A changes M`

this supports the A→M link.

It does not establish:

`M causes Y`.

### Project rule

Mechanistic evidence should report separately:

1. perturbation/exposure;
2. target engagement;
3. downstream response;
4. causal link between target and downstream response.

---

# Part E — Interpretability matrix for target engagement

## 11. A does not engage M; Y does not change

Interpretation:

> experiment failed to test the proposed target adequately OR target was not modifiable at chosen dose/time.

Cannot cleanly conclude:

> M is irrelevant.

---

## 12. A does not engage M; Y changes

Interpretation:

Possible:

- off-target pathway;
- wrong target assay;
- indirect mechanism;
- measurement error.

This is evidence **against the simple proposed mechanism**, not proof of a new mechanism.

---

## 13. A engages M; Y does not change

Interpretation:

Under adequate measurement/time/power:

> evidence against the claim that this degree/timing of M engagement is sufficient to produce Y.

May also suggest:

- M not causally important;
- insufficient engagement magnitude;
- wrong downstream timing;
- compensatory pathway.

---

## 14. A engages M; Y changes as predicted

Interpretation:

> supports the hypothesized perturbation chain.

But still does not by itself establish:

- mediation;
- exclusivity;
- necessity;
- sufficiency under all conditions.

---

# Part F — Randomized human perturbation

## 15. Randomization strengthens the perturbation contrast

### Established design principle
**Confidence: HIGH**

Random allocation helps balance:

- measured baseline causes;
- unmeasured baseline causes,

in expectation.

For mechanism experiments this strengthens causal inference for:

> effect of assigned perturbation A on measured response(s).

---

## 16. Randomization does not guarantee mechanism specificity

### Critical rule
**Confidence: HIGH**

Even a perfect randomized trial can have ambiguous mechanism if:

- drug acts off-target;
- blockade incomplete;
- manipulation changes several physiological variables;
- assay does not measure intended target;
- target and downstream response measured at wrong times;
- compensation occurs.

Therefore:

> **randomized A → causal effect of perturbation**  
does not automatically equal  
> **specific M proven as mechanism**.

---

# Part G — Nonrandomized human perturbation

## 17. Nonrandomized manipulation can still be experimentally informative

Examples:

- fixed-order before/after blockade;
- physiologic challenge without randomized order;
- single-arm dose escalation;
- laboratory provocation with baseline control.

It can establish:

- temporal response;
- within-person physiological change;
- plausibility of perturbation effects.

But it is more vulnerable to:

- period/order effects;
- habituation;
- regression to mean;
- learning/familiarization;
- concurrent changes;
- confounding by assignment.

### Project classification

Record:

> mechanistic perturbation + nonrandomized allocation/order.

Do not upgrade it to randomized causal evidence.

---

# Part H — Parallel-group mechanistic design

## 18. Best fit

Parallel structure is useful when manipulation effects are:

- persistent;
- long-lived;
- irreversible;
- carryover-prone;
- unsafe/impractical to repeat.

Examples:

- chronic training adaptation;
- long-acting pharmacological modulation;
- prolonged dietary intervention.

---

## 19. Limitation

Between-person biological heterogeneity can reduce precision for intensive mechanism outcomes.

Randomization helps causal balance but does not remove:

- measurement noise;
- post-randomization nonadherence;
- assay heterogeneity.

---

# Part I — Crossover mechanistic design

## 20. Best fit

Crossover is particularly useful for:

- acute;
- reversible;
- transient;

human physiology perturbations.

Each participant receives multiple conditions.

Strength:

> within-person mechanistic contrast reduces stable between-person heterogeneity.

---

## 21. Requirements

Need explicit control of:

1. condition order;
2. randomization/counterbalancing;
3. washout/recovery;
4. period effects;
5. carryover;
6. learning/familiarization;
7. fatigue/residual exercise effects.

### Project rule

A repeated-measures physiology study is not automatically a valid crossover experiment.

---

## 22. Fitness relevance

Good candidates:

- acute caffeine;
- hypoxia;
- pharmacological blockade;
- rest interval;
- acute load/velocity condition;
- BFR pressure condition;
- nutrient/substrate manipulation.

Poor candidate:

> chronic hypertrophy training blocks where adaptation persists into later periods.

---

# Part J — Within-person / split-body mechanistic design

## 23. Core advantage

Different body regions/limbs can receive different local conditions within the same participant.

Potential uses:

- local loading;
- blood-flow restriction;
- stimulation;
- local treatment.

Controls stable person-level factors.

---

## 24. Critical mechanistic assumption

The local conditions must be sufficiently isolated.

Threats:

- systemic endocrine responses;
- circulating metabolites;
- neural cross-education;
- behavioral compensation;
- contralateral effects.

### Project rule

Split-body randomization can identify **local differential response** only insofar as systemic spillover does not invalidate the contrast.

---

# Part K — Factorial mechanistic perturbation

## 25. Core use

Factorial design can manipulate two mechanism components simultaneously.

Canonical 2×2:

- A absent / M blocker absent;
- A present / blocker absent;
- A absent / blocker present;
- A present / blocker present.

Potential question:

> does the downstream effect of A depend on pathway M?

---

## 26. Interaction is central

If the mechanistic claim is:

> M modifies/transmits the response to A,

the relevant evidence may be an interaction/difference in A effect across M perturbation.

### Project rule

Do not infer pathway dependence merely because:

- A is significant in one subgroup/condition;
- A is nonsignificant in another.

The contrast itself must be evaluated.

Detailed interaction statistics are deferred.

---

# Part L — Challenge / provocation studies

## 27. Definition

A challenge deliberately stresses or changes a physiological system to reveal:

- responsiveness;
- regulatory control;
- reserve;
- pathway behavior.

Examples:

- exercise challenge;
- hypoxia;
- glucose/nutrient challenge;
- sleep deprivation;
- sodium loading;
- thermal stress.

---

## 28. What challenge can establish

A controlled challenge can show:

> how the system behaves under the experimentally imposed state.

It can help identify:

- dose sensitivity;
- dynamic regulation;
- latent physiology not visible at rest.

---

## 29. What challenge cannot establish automatically

A response under challenge does not by itself prove:

- same process controls resting physiology;
- same mechanism drives chronic adaptation;
- same effect occurs at real-world dose/exposure;
- observed response is beneficial/harmful long-term.

---

# Part M — Dose / exposure perturbation

## 30. Graded perturbation

Multiple doses/exposure levels can test:

- whether target engagement increases with exposure;
- thresholds;
- saturation;
- shape of downstream response.

Examples:

- pharmacological dose;
- exercise intensity;
- BFR pressure;
- oxygen concentration;
- nutrient dose.

---

## 31. Dose-response strengthens but does not prove mechanism

A graded A→M→Y pattern can strengthen a mechanism hypothesis.

But dose changes may also alter:

- off-target activity;
- discomfort/effort;
- systemic stress;
- kinetics;
- compensatory responses.

### Project rule

Dose-response evidence must be interpreted together with target specificity and measured engagement.

---

# Part N — Pharmacological blockade / inhibition

## 32. Core logic

Manipulate:

> reduce/inhibit candidate component M.

Then observe downstream Y.

If Y is attenuated/lost:

> M may be required/dependent for that response under the tested conditions.

---

## 33. Blockade requires proof of blockade

Before interpreting Y:

need evidence that:

- target was reached;
- blockade magnitude was sufficient;
- timing covered the relevant biological window.

### Project rule

“No downstream effect after intended blocker” is uninterpretable if actual blockade was not demonstrated.

---

## 34. Selectivity is a separate validity requirement

A blocker may:

- affect related receptors;
- alter unrelated physiological processes;
- change effort/pain;
- change hemodynamics;
- alter substrate delivery.

Therefore a downstream response can reflect:

> off-target or system-wide consequences.

### Project rule

Mechanistic blockade evidence requires:

> **engagement + specificity + downstream response**.

---

# Part O — Human exercise blockade examples

## 35. Group III/IV muscle afferent attenuation

Human exercise physiology has used:

- intrathecal/epidural pharmacological attenuation of locomotor muscle afferent feedback

to test contributions to:

- cardiovascular regulation;
- ventilation;
- neural drive;
- fatigue;
- exercise performance.

### Mechanistic value

This directly perturbs a candidate feedback pathway.

### Interpretation limit

Blockade is often partial and may alter multiple downstream functions simultaneously.

Thus claims should be:

> contribution/dependence under the tested condition,

rather than universal exclusive mechanism.

---

## 36. AT1 receptor blockade + exercise challenge

A 2025 human study used:

- randomized;
- blinded;
- crossover;
- valsartan blockade vs placebo;
- exercise challenge.

Finding:

- blockade altered/abolished the acute EPO response.

### Taxonomy

`randomized crossover + pharmacological blockade + exercise challenge + endocrine mechanistic target`

This is a clean example of compositional mechanistic design classification.

# Part P — Activation / agonism / gain-of-function logic

## 37. Core logic

Increase/activate candidate component M and ask whether Y is produced or enhanced.

If isolated activation produces Y:

> supports an **inducing / sufficiency-like** role under the experimental conditions.

---

## 38. Limitations

Activation may be:

- supraphysiological;
- spatially unnatural;
- temporally unnatural;
- broader than endogenous activation.

Therefore:

> artificial induction of Y does not prove that natural A normally works through M.

---

# Part Q — Rescue / reversal logic

## 39. Core logic

After disrupting a mechanism component:

1. create loss/attenuation of M;
2. observe change/loss of Y;
3. restore M or downstream function;
4. ask whether Y returns.

### Value

Rescue can strengthen:

- specificity;
- causal-pathway interpretation;
- argument against nonspecific damage from the blocking manipulation.

---

## 40. Human constraints

True rescue experiments are often difficult in humans because of:

- safety;
- pharmacological specificity;
- reversibility;
- ethical constraints;
- incomplete control of biological components.

Therefore absence of human rescue evidence is not automatically a flaw.

But claims should remain proportional to available perturbation evidence.

---

# Part R — Necessity claims

## 41. Strict logical necessity is rarely established in complex human biology

A loss-of-function/blockade experiment can support:

> the manipulated component is **required for the full response under these experimental conditions**.

Stronger evidence:

- effective selective blockade;
- substantial/complete loss of response;
- preserved upstream inputs;
- preserved unrelated functions;
- replication with orthogonal perturbation.

---

## 42. Failure after blockade does not prove universal necessity

Reasons:

- blocker may damage general system function;
- manipulation may be nonspecific;
- response may depend on context;
- pathway may only be required at that dose/intensity/population;
- network redundancy may differ elsewhere.

### Preferred project wording

Instead of:

> “M is universally necessary”,

write:

> “selective attenuation of M reduced/abolished Y under condition C, supporting dependence on M in that context.”

---

# Part S — Sufficiency claims

## 43. Strict sufficiency also needs careful wording

If activation of M produces Y without A:

> M is **capable of inducing Y under the tested conditions**.

This is stronger than correlation.

But does not prove:

- M is the natural mediator of A;
- M is the only route to Y;
- the artificial activation level is physiological.

### Preferred wording

> “activation of M was sufficient to induce Y under the experimental protocol”

only when the experimental isolation is defensible.

Otherwise use:

> “supports an inducing role”.

---

# Part T — Necessity and sufficiency are independent

## 44. A component can be

- necessary but not sufficient;
- sufficient but not necessary;
- neither alone;
- context-dependent;
- redundant with parallel pathways.

Complex exercise physiology frequently contains:

- redundancy;
- compensation;
- distributed control.

### Project rule

Do not infer necessity from sufficiency or vice versa.

---

# Part U — Negative blockade experiments

## 45. No effect after blockade has several explanations

Possible:

1. M truly not important;
2. blockade incomplete;
3. target assay insensitive;
4. compensatory pathway replaced M;
5. wrong time window;
6. inadequate perturbation dose;
7. downstream outcome too noisy;
8. study underpowered.

### Project rule

A negative mechanistic experiment requires evidence that the intended perturbation actually occurred.

---

# Part V — Positive blockade experiments

## 46. Y changes after blockade

Possible interpretations:

1. intended M contributes causally;
2. off-target effect;
3. nonspecific physiological disruption;
4. altered perception/effort;
5. changed upstream input;
6. generalized toxicity/stress.

### Project rule

Positive result still requires mechanistic specificity checks.

---

# Part W — Orthogonal perturbations

## 47. Converging manipulations strengthen mechanism inference

If possible, test same pathway using different perturbations:

- pharmacological;
- physiological;
- behavioral;
- electrical/neural;
- genetic evidence from another context.

### Value

If distinct perturbations with different off-target structures converge:

> confidence in the target/pathway increases.

This is stronger than repeatedly using the same tool.

---

# Part X — Timing architecture

## 48. Manipulation must precede target engagement

Minimal:

`A → M`

Target measurement must occur when the perturbation should biologically act.

---

## 49. Target engagement must precede downstream mechanistic consequence

For pathway claim:

`A → M → Y`

Need temporal compatibility:

- A first;
- M second;
- Y after/along the predicted pathway.

Simultaneous M and Y measurement weakens temporal interpretation.

---

## 50. Acute and chronic timings are different mechanisms questions

Acute experiment can validly test:

> acute control/regulation.

It does not automatically test:

> mechanism of chronic adaptation.

A chronic mechanism claim may require:

- repeated perturbation;
- longitudinal target behavior;
- downstream adaptation.

---

# Part Y — Measurement architecture

## 51. Target measurement must represent the hypothesized component

Examples:

- receptor occupancy;
- muscle afferent attenuation;
- phosphorylation state;
- blood flow;
- substrate turnover;
- neural drive.

Proxy quality matters.

### Project rule

If proxy validity is weak:

> strength of mechanism conclusion must be downgraded.

---

## 52. Correct biological compartment matters

Blood biomarker may not reflect:

- skeletal muscle;
- CNS;
- tendon;
- bone;
- local receptor occupancy.

### Project rule

Record:

> where the target was measured relative to where the proposed mechanism operates.

---

# Part Z — Placebo / sham / blinding relevance

## 53. Human physiology mechanisms can be expectancy-sensitive

Especially outcomes such as:

- effort;
- pain;
- fatigue;
- voluntary performance;
- autonomic responses.

Blinding/placebo/sham can reduce:

- expectancy;
- behavioral adaptation;
- observer bias.

### Project rule

Blinding is not required to call a study mechanistic, but lack of feasible blinding can weaken causal specificity for subjective/behavior-dependent outcomes.

Detailed blinding methodology belongs later RES-03/RES-04.

# Part AA — Manipulation fidelity

## 54. Mechanistic experiment needs evidence that the assigned perturbation actually differed

Record, where relevant:

- administered dose;
- exposure concentration;
- compliance;
- exercise intensity/load;
- BFR pressure;
- achieved oxygen saturation;
- receptor occupancy;
- physiologic blockade marker.

### Project rule

Assigned condition without verified physiological separation may create:

> manipulation failure.

# Part AB — Mechanistic trial sample size

## 55. Power should target the mechanistic hypothesis

Current NHLBI guidance explicitly says sample size should be:

> adequate to inform mechanism of action.

### Project rule

A mechanistic experiment is not justified as “small because mechanistic”.

Intensive measurements may reduce variance, but:

- low N;
- multiple endpoints;
- unstable effects;

still create false-positive/false-negative risk.

Exact sample-size methods are deferred.

# Part AC — Exploratory vs confirmatory mechanistic endpoints

## 56. Prespecified target

Strongest mechanism test has:

- prespecified target;
- prespecified assay;
- predicted direction/timing;
- defined primary mechanistic analysis.

---

## 57. Post hoc biomarker discovery

Can be useful for:

- hypothesis generation;
- identifying candidate pathways.

But:

> finding one changed marker among many after seeing data is not equivalent to a prespecified target-engagement test.

This boundary becomes especially important in omics and belongs in more depth to PASS-06C4.

# Part AD — Acute human physiology examples

## 58. Exercise afferent blockade

Question:

> does sensory feedback from working muscle causally contribute to exercise cardiorespiratory regulation?

Structure:

- human;
- acute exercise challenge;
- pharmacological attenuation;
- within-person/crossover-like comparison;
- mechanistic physiological outcomes.

Can support:

> contribution of blocked feedback pathway to acute regulation.

Cannot alone establish:

> mechanism of chronic training adaptation.

---

## 59. Acute load manipulation + signaling

Question:

> does altered mechanical loading change prespecified mechanotransduction signaling?

Structure:

- randomized/counterbalanced acute exercise condition;
- target engagement/signaling readout.

Can support:

> acute A→M response.

Cannot alone establish:

> M mediates months-long hypertrophy.

---

## 60. BFR pressure manipulation

Question:

> does graded restriction alter local oxygenation/metabolic stress and downstream acute response?

Structure:

- dose/challenge perturbation;
- often within-person/crossover.

Mechanistic strength depends on:

- actual pressure/occlusion achieved;
- local measurement;
- systemic spillover;
- downstream target timing.

# Part AE — Claims hierarchy for human perturbation evidence

## Level 0 — Descriptive response

> M changed after exposure.

No strong causal perturbation logic.

---

## Level 1 — Perturbation effect

> assigned/manipulated A changed M or Y versus comparator.

Randomization/control strengthens causal A→response inference.

---

## Level 2 — Target engagement

> A measurably engaged the hypothesized target M.

Supports A→M.

---

## Level 3 — Pathway dependence

> selective attenuation/blockade of M reduced downstream Y.

Supports conditional dependence/necessity-like claim.

---

## Level 4 — Inducing capability

> selective activation of M produced Y.

Supports sufficiency-like/inducing claim.

---

## Level 5 — Specificity strengthened by rescue/orthogonal evidence

> disruption changes Y and restoration/independent perturbation recovers/converges on Y.

Supports a stronger mechanism interpretation.

### Important limitation

This is a **project evidence-description ladder**, not a universal published hierarchy.

It must not be converted into automatic certainty grades.

# Practical rules produced by PASS-06C2

1. Human mechanistic perturbation deliberately manipulates a system component/condition to test a mechanism hypothesis.
2. An intervention can function as an experimental probe rather than a treatment.
3. Current NIH BESH prospectively manipulates human conditions to study fundamental phenomena without direct specific health application.
4. For NIH application due dates on/after 2026-05-25, BESH is no longer classified as an NIH clinical trial.
5. Mechanistic clinical trials remain human health-related intervention studies whose primary aim is mechanism/process/pathophysiology rather than ordinary efficacy/effectiveness.
6. BESH/mechanistic-clinical-trial labels do not replace structural labels such as crossover/parallel/factorial.
7. Mechanistic hypothesis should specify A, target/process M, downstream Y and plausible alternatives.
8. Outcomes should discriminate the mechanism hypothesis, not merely maximize biomarker count.
9. Target engagement establishes whether A actually changed M.
10. Target engagement requires a valid assay, correct timing, relevant compartment and adequate perturbation.
11. A→M target engagement does not establish M→Y mediation.
12. Failure to engage M means a negative Y result may be an uninterpretable manipulation failure.
13. Engagement of M without Y can count against the proposed link if timing/measurement/power are adequate.
14. Randomization strengthens causal inference about the assigned perturbation.
15. Randomization does not automatically establish mechanism specificity.
16. Nonrandomized human perturbation can be informative but remains more vulnerable to order/time/confounding.
17. Parallel design fits persistent/carryover-prone perturbations.
18. Crossover fits acute/reversible perturbations only when washout/order/carryover are handled.
19. Within-person/split-body designs require biological isolation; systemic spillover can invalidate local causal contrast.
20. Factorial mechanistic experiments can test whether a response to A depends on manipulation of M.
21. Significance in one condition but not another is not itself evidence of interaction/pathway dependence.
22. Challenge/provocation studies reveal response under an imposed physiological state, not automatically baseline or chronic mechanism.
23. Dose/exposure-response can strengthen mechanism inference but dose changes can also change off-target/systemic effects.
24. Blockade/inhibition requires verified blockade and specificity before downstream effects are attributed to the target.
25. Attenuation of Y after selective blockade supports conditional dependence/necessity-like evidence, not universal necessity.
26. No effect after blockade cannot refute a mechanism when blockade was incomplete or compensation occurred.
27. Activation/gain-of-function producing Y supports an inducing/sufficiency-like role under the experimental conditions.
28. Artificial activation does not prove the same target mediates the natural effect of A.
29. Rescue/reversal can strengthen specificity and causal interpretation.
30. Necessity and sufficiency are independent and context-dependent in complex biological systems.
31. Prefer condition-specific wording over universal “necessary/sufficient” claims.
32. Orthogonal perturbations with different off-target structures strengthen mechanism inference.
33. Manipulation must precede target engagement and target engagement must temporally precede the proposed downstream consequence.
34. Acute human mechanistic experiments establish acute physiology more directly than chronic adaptation mechanism.
35. Target measurement must match the tissue/compartment where the mechanism is proposed to operate.
36. Placebo/sham/blinding can be important for effort, pain, fatigue, performance and autonomic outcomes.
37. Manipulation fidelity must be verified, not assumed from assignment.
38. Mechanistic study sample size should be justified for its mechanism target; “mechanistic = small” is not a validity rule.
39. Prespecified target-engagement tests are stronger confirmatory evidence than post hoc biomarker fishing.
40. Human exercise pharmacological blockade is a valid mechanistic perturbation strategy when engagement, specificity and downstream interpretation are defensible.
41. Human perturbation can support proof-of-mechanism but does not automatically establish clinical effectiveness or practical training superiority.
42. Detailed M→Y causal mediation remains a separate branch for PASS-06C3.

# Evidence limitations

1. Current NIH BESH classification is an administrative policy boundary, not a universal mechanistic epistemology.
2. NIH institute webpages can retain legacy clinical-trial terminology; NIH-wide 2026 BESH notices govern current BESH classification.
3. Human experimental perturbations often cannot isolate a biological component as selectively as animal/genetic models.
4. Pharmacological blockers/agonists can have off-target and systemic actions.
5. Partial blockade supports contribution/dependence claims more safely than absolute necessity claims.
6. Biological redundancy and compensation make strict necessity/sufficiency language hazardous.
7. Acute physiology can differ from repeated/chronic adaptation.
8. Target engagement assays can be proxies rather than direct target measurements.
9. Small intensive mechanistic experiments remain vulnerable to imprecision and multiplicity.
10. Human mechanistic external validity may be limited by homogeneous healthy samples and tightly controlled laboratory settings.
11. Formal mediation is needed when the claim specifically concerns how much of A→Y operates through measured M; that is deferred to PASS-06C3.
