# CLN-02 — Psychopathology mechanisms and course: transdiagnostic processes, onset, maintenance, remission, recurrence, risk/protective factors, state–trait separation, and causal-model limits

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Establish the mechanism-and-course layer of psychopathology required after CLN-01.

CLN-01 separated symptom, syndrome, diagnosis, formulation, mechanism, categorical/dimensional classification, comorbidity, and differential diagnosis.

CLN-02 asks:

> How can psychopathology emerge, persist, fluctuate, remit, recur, and interact with the person's environment without turning longitudinal association, transdiagnostic correlation, mediation, or diagnostic course into unsupported causal stories?

This block is not a disorder-by-disorder encyclopedia.

Its purpose is to prevent:

- treating a risk marker as a cause;
- treating a transdiagnostic correlate as a universal mechanism;
- treating a cross-sectional mediator as a causal mediator;
- treating temporal precedence as sufficient causal identification;
- treating onset, maintenance, remission, and recurrence as one process;
- treating symptom improvement as proof that the hypothesized mechanism changed;
- treating recurrence as evidence that the same mechanism returned;
- treating trait-like between-person differences as within-person mechanisms;
- treating within-person symptom change as a stable trait;
- treating protective factors as simple opposites of risk factors;
- treating resilience as an immutable person property;
- treating an environmental exposure as one homogeneous dose;
- treating person–environment feedback as one-way exposure.

Canonical course rule:

`vulnerability/liability + exposures/context + dynamic processes + feedback loops -> symptom trajectory`

with each arrow requiring independent evidence.

Never:

`predictor -> cause -> mechanism -> inevitable course`.

---

# I. Mechanism vocabulary

## 1. Correlate

A correlate is associated with psychopathology.

It may be a cause, consequence, shared outcome, confounded marker, or measurement overlap.

Therefore:

`correlate != mechanism`.

## 2. Risk marker

A **risk marker** predicts later psychopathology or adverse course.

It does not automatically imply that changing the marker would change the outcome.

A marker can be useful for prediction without being causal.

## 3. Risk factor

In broad clinical literature, “risk factor” is often used for an exposure or characteristic associated with higher future risk.

For causal reasoning, distinguish:

- **predictive risk factor/marker**;
- **putative causal risk factor**;
- **causally identified exposure**.

Do not upgrade terminology beyond the design.

## 4. Protective factor

A protective factor is associated with lower probability, severity, persistence, or adverse outcome.

It is not automatically the inverse of a risk factor, a causal intervention target, or universally protective.

## 5. Vulnerability / liability

A vulnerability is a relatively enduring propensity that can increase probability of psychopathology under some conditions.

It can be genetic, developmental, temperamental, cognitive, social, or environmental.

Vulnerability is probabilistic, not destiny.

## 6. Trigger / precipitant

A trigger is an event or change temporally proximal to symptom onset or exacerbation.

A trigger can reveal a vulnerability without being sufficient by itself.

Temporal proximity alone does not prove causality.

## 7. Maintenance process

A maintenance process contributes to persistence of symptoms or impairment once the problem is active.

Examples can include avoidance, repetitive negative thinking, reinforcing contingencies, sleep disruption, interpersonal feedback, or safety behavior.

A process is not a maintenance mechanism merely because it is present during illness.

## 8. Mediator

A mediator lies on a causal pathway from an exposure/intervention to an outcome **under a specified causal model**.

A statistically significant indirect effect does not by itself prove causal mediation.

## 9. Moderator

A moderator indicates that an association/effect differs across values of another variable.

Moderation is not mechanism by itself.

## 10. Mechanism

A mechanism is a process through which a cause produces an effect.

Strong mechanism evidence typically requires convergence across temporal order, specificity, perturbation where feasible, mediation/process evidence, replication, and appropriate causal assumptions.

# II. Onset, maintenance, persistence, remission, relapse, and recurrence

## 11. Onset is a transition

Onset refers to transition from a lower-symptom/noncase state into a clinically meaningful symptom pattern or disorder.

An onset predictor must precede onset.

But:

`predicts onset -> causes onset`

is invalid without causal identification.

## 12. Maintenance is not onset

A variable can:

- predict onset but not persistence;
- maintain symptoms but not trigger first onset;
- affect recurrence but not initial onset.

Mechanisms must therefore be stage-specific.

## 13. Persistence

Persistence refers to continued symptoms/disorder over a specified interval.

Persistence can be continuous, intermittently symptomatic, or threshold-dependent.

Operational definitions must be stated.

## 14. Chronicity

“Chronic” is not one universal psychometric state.

It can refer to long episode duration, persistent subthreshold symptoms, repeated episodes, or long-term functional impairment.

Define time horizon and outcome.

## 15. Remission

Remission generally refers to substantial reduction or absence of clinically significant symptoms under an operational definition.

Remission does not automatically imply:

- full functional recovery;
- removal of vulnerability;
- disappearance of all maintaining processes;
- low recurrence risk.

## 16. Recovery

Recovery can be defined differently across disorders and research traditions.

It may include sustained symptom remission, functional recovery, subjective well-being, and social/occupational participation.

Do not assume one universal duration threshold.

## 17. Relapse versus recurrence

Traditional clinical terminology often distinguishes:

- **relapse** — return of symptoms before full recovery;
- **recurrence** — new episode after recovery.

However, empirical work in depression has questioned whether fixed duration thresholds cleanly distinguish these states.

Therefore:

**always state the operational definition rather than treating relapse and recurrence as natural kinds.**

## 18. Recurrence does not identify mechanism identity

If symptoms return, possibilities include persistent vulnerability, reactivation of a prior maintenance loop, new exposure, a different mechanism producing a similar syndrome, or measurement/threshold fluctuation.

Therefore:

`same diagnosis returns -> same mechanism returned`

is invalid.

## 19. Remission does not identify mechanism removal

Symptoms can remit because exposure changed, treatment altered one process, compensatory processes increased, environmental demands decreased, spontaneous fluctuation occurred, or threshold crossing occurred without full process normalization.

Thus:

`symptoms remitted -> causal mechanism eliminated`

is invalid.

# III. Course is heterogeneous

## 20. Mental disorders do not follow one canonical trajectory

Possible trajectories include:

- single episode with sustained remission;
- recurrent episodes;
- persistent symptoms;
- fluctuating subthreshold symptoms;
- diagnostic shifts;
- heterotypic continuity.

## 21. Recent population evidence confirms multiple trajectories

A 2026 NEMESIS longitudinal study followed 6,646 adults over nine years.

Among those without a lifetime depressive/anxiety disorder at baseline, about 13% developed a depressive and/or anxiety disorder.

Reported nine-year recurrence rates were approximately:

- 28% for depressive disorders;
- 26% for anxiety disorders;
- 38% for comorbid depressive/anxiety disorders.

For people with a current disorder at baseline, recurrence/persistence was higher.

These rates describe population trajectories.

They do not predict one person's course with certainty.

## 22. Prevalence can rise through different processes

A 2026 comparison of NEMESIS cohorts showed that higher prevalence of common mental disorders reflected changes in incidence, recurrence, and persistence.

Population prevalence is therefore not one disease-process variable.

## 23. Diagnostic shifts matter

A 2026 nationwide Danish cohort of 273,400 people found substantial diagnostic shifts and recurrence patterns across age, sex, and initial diagnosis.

Diagnostic change can reflect true evolution, developmental change, comorbidity, threshold crossing, different clinical encounters, or classification limitations.

# IV. Transdiagnostic processes

## 24. Definition

A transdiagnostic process occurs across more than one diagnostic category or psychopathology dimension.

It may involve cognition, emotion, behavior, physiology, interpersonal processes, learning, or environmental feedback.

## 25. Transdiagnostic does not mean universal

A process can be transdiagnostic while being absent in many people, differing in strength/function across disorders, having different causes across people, or being relevant only at particular stages.

## 26. Transdiagnostic does not mean causal

If a process is elevated across disorders, that establishes breadth of association.

Causal status requires stronger evidence.

## 27. Repetitive negative thinking

Current evidence supports repetitive negative thinking (RNT), including overlapping forms of rumination and worry, as a strong transdiagnostic cognitive process.

The 2025 Nature Reviews Psychology synthesis concluded that rumination and worry are strongly related, share phenomenological features, and predict/maintain multiple psychopathologies.

Measurement, treatment, and prevention evidence supports a transdiagnostic conceptualization.

But:

`RNT present -> RNT is the cause of this case`

is still invalid.

## 28. RNT intervention evidence

A 2025 transdiagnostic meta-analysis found cognitive-behavioral interventions can reduce RNT/rumination/worry.

This supports modifiability.

It does not prove that RNT is the sole mechanism of symptom change, that all symptoms are caused by RNT, or that reducing RNT is necessary for all recovery.

## 29. Emotion regulation

A 2026 systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis included 619 studies, 41,590 clinical participants, and 36,787 controls.

Across disorders, clinical groups reported pronounced emotion-regulation difficulties and broad differences in strategy use, including more rumination/avoidance/suppression and less adaptive regulation in several comparisons.

This strongly supports transdiagnostic association.

Temporal and causal relations remain less certain.

Therefore:

`emotion-regulation difficulty = universal causal mechanism`

is too strong.

## 30. Avoidance

Avoidance can reduce distress in the short term while preserving threat beliefs, reducing corrective learning, or narrowing behavior.

But “avoidance” is heterogeneous:

- experiential avoidance;
- behavioral avoidance;
- social avoidance;
- safety behavior;
- cognitive avoidance.

Do not collapse these automatically.

## 31. Intolerance of uncertainty

Intolerance of uncertainty (IU) is associated with multiple emotional-disorder presentations and maladaptive emotion regulation.

However much evidence is correlational, IU overlaps with worry/RNT, effects can be context-dependent, and intervention effects are heterogeneous.

Therefore IU is a candidate transdiagnostic process, not a universal causal primitive.

## 32. Cognitive control

Cognitive-control deficits are often proposed as transdiagnostic.

A 2026 meta-analysis found relations between individual differences in cognitive control and emotion-regulation strategies were small despite conceptual consistency.

Therefore:

`executive-control deficit -> broad psychopathology mechanism`

should not be assumed from conceptual plausibility alone.

## 33. Sleep

Sleep disturbance can be antecedent, symptom, maintenance factor, consequence, or amplifier.

Its role must be identified for the specific trajectory.

## 34. Interpersonal processes

Interpersonal conflict, rejection, isolation, accommodation, reinforcement, and role strain can precede symptoms, result from symptoms, maintain symptoms, or moderate recovery.

The causal direction can be reciprocal.

# V. Stress generation and reciprocal feedback

## 35. Psychopathology can alter the environment

The stress-generation model proposes that people with or at risk of psychopathology can, through behavior and interpersonal processes, experience more **dependent** stressful events.

This creates a feedback possibility:

`vulnerability/symptoms -> behavior/interactions -> stressor -> symptom amplification`.

## 36. Stress generation is not blame

The 2026 expert consensus explicitly cautions against stigmatizing interpretation.

“Dependent stress” means statistically/personally influenced occurrence, not moral responsibility.

## 37. Independent and dependent stressors must be distinguished

A natural disaster may be largely independent.

Interpersonal conflict may be partly dependent on behavior.

Job loss can contain both external and person-linked causes.

Simple binary coding can be inadequate.

## 38. Stress generation requires careful measurement

The 2026 consensus recommends attention to stressor operationalization, assessment method, longitudinal design, intensive longitudinal design, statistical analysis, openness, and nonstigmatizing language.

## 39. Feedback loops can produce persistence without one fixed cause

Possible loop:

`negative affect -> withdrawal -> relationship strain -> reduced support -> more negative affect`.

The loop may maintain symptoms even if the original trigger has ended.

A plausible feedback story still requires longitudinal/process evidence.

# VI. Risk and protective factors

## 40. Association strength and causal credibility are different dimensions

A robust meta-analytic association can still arise from confounding, reverse causation, selection, or measurement overlap.

Grade:

1. consistency;
2. temporality;
3. bias sensitivity;
4. causal identification;
5. modifiability.

## 41. Broad evidence atlas

A large meta-umbrella review of non-genetic risk/protective factors across mental disorders synthesized 390 meta-analyses and 1,180 associations.

It found wide variation in evidence credibility and used prospective sensitivity analyses to address temporality.

Canonical implication:

**the literature contains many reported risk factors, but only a subset have robust evidence and fewer have established causal status.**

## 42. Disorder-specific evidence can be stronger than transdiagnostic evidence

A 2026 World Psychiatry umbrella review of psychotic disorders synthesized 69 systematic reviews/meta-analyses, 970 primary studies, and 221 candidate risk/protective factors.

Only a minority reached convincing or highly suggestive evidence.

Thus:

`many candidate predictors -> many established causal risk factors`

is false.

## 43. Population attributable fraction is not individual causation

PAF estimates potential population burden associated with an exposure under assumptions.

It does not mean the exposure caused that proportion of one person's illness, that removing the exposure would necessarily prevent the same fraction, or that the exposure is the sole mechanism.

## 44. Modifiable does not equal causally validated

A variable can be modifiable yet noncausal.

A causal factor can be difficult or impossible to modify.

Keep these separate.

## 45. Protective factors are not mirror images

For example, social connection may buffer some risks but effects can depend on relationship quality, timing, context, and fit to needs.

“More” is not always uniformly protective.

## 46. Risk accumulation is not simple arithmetic

Multiple risks can combine through additive effects, interaction, mediation, shared causes, or correlated exposures.

A count of risk factors is a summary, not a mechanistic dose.

## 47. Timing matters

The same exposure can differ in effect depending on developmental period, duration, recurrence, chronicity, and proximity to onset.

Timing-specific questions route back to DEV and STR.

---

# VII. Antecedents, predictors, and causes

## 48. Antecedent

An antecedent is measured before disorder onset.

That establishes temporal order but not causal status.

## 49. Prospective transdiagnostic antecedents exist

A 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis of prospective studies covering depressive, bipolar, and psychotic disorder onset found several antecedents associated across diagnoses, including psychotic symptoms, depressive symptoms, anxiety, disruptive behaviors, affective lability, and sleep problems.

This supports early-risk prediction.

It does not establish that each antecedent is independently causal.

## 50. Prediction can be clinically useful without mechanism

A marker can help identify elevated risk even if the process is unknown.

Use:

`predictive marker -> monitoring/prevention relevance`

not automatically:

`predictive marker -> mechanism`.

---

# VIII. State–trait separation

## 51. State

A state is a time-localized condition that can fluctuate within a person.

Examples include current anxiety, momentary sadness, sleepiness, urge, or stress response.

## 52. Trait

A trait is a relatively stable between-person propensity or long-term pattern.

Trait does not mean immutable.

## 53. Symptoms can contain state and trait components

A questionnaire administered once can mix stable propensity, current episode, recent context, recall bias, and measurement error.

Therefore a single score cannot cleanly identify state versus trait.

## 54. Between-person and within-person effects are different

Across people:

`people with higher average rumination have higher average depression`.

Within one person:

`on days when rumination rises above that person's usual level, symptoms later rise`.

These are different estimands.

The first does not imply the second.

## 55. Ecological fallacy

A between-person association cannot automatically be used as a within-person mechanism.

This is a central limit in personalized psychopathology reasoning.

## 56. Repeated measurement enables but does not guarantee state–trait separation

Longitudinal/EMA data can estimate person mean, within-person deviation, inertia, variability, and lagged association.

Causal interpretation still depends on sampling frequency, measurement validity, confounding, temporal alignment, and model assumptions.

## 57. Daily-life psychopathology research exposes this gap

A 2025 Clinical Psychological Science study noted that much transdiagnostic research is cross-sectional and between-person even though many proposed processes are within-person.

This is a major inference constraint.

## 58. Temporal resolution changes the process observed

Hourly, daily, weekly, and monthly measurements can produce different apparent dynamics.

A process with a short timescale can disappear in coarse measurement.

## 59. Aggregation can create apparent traits

A stable average across many observations can emerge from fluctuating states.

Conversely, a trait-like questionnaire can conceal strong day-to-day dynamics.

## 60. Trait-like does not mean causal vulnerability

Stable individual differences may reflect enduring liability, stable environment, chronic symptoms, measurement style, or long-term adaptation.

Causal vulnerability requires more evidence.

---

# IX. Onset versus maintenance evidence

## 61. Cross-sectional presence cannot distinguish onset from maintenance

If avoidance is high during an anxiety disorder, possibilities include:

- avoidance helped cause onset;
- disorder increased avoidance;
- avoidance maintains symptoms;
- both share another cause.

## 62. Onset evidence requires pre-onset measurement

For a proposed onset mechanism, prefer prospective cohorts, high-risk designs, natural experiments, randomized prevention, or explicit causal designs.

## 63. Maintenance evidence requires active-course dynamics

Relevant evidence can include repeated measurements during active symptoms, perturbation of the process, experimental manipulation, treatment process studies, or randomized N-of-1/time-series designs.

## 64. Treatment-associated change is not enough

If process X decreases during treatment as symptoms decrease:

`X change correlates with symptom change`

does not establish:

`X caused symptom change`.

Possible alternatives include reverse causation, common treatment factors, measurement overlap, and regression to the mean.

## 65. Mechanism-of-change evidence is stronger when

1. process change precedes outcome change;
2. intervention manipulates the process;
3. process change mediates intervention effect under defensible assumptions;
4. competing mediators are tested;
5. replication occurs.

---

# X. Remission and recovery mechanisms

## 66. Symptom remission and functional recovery are different outcomes

A person can have low symptoms but persistent impairment, or improved function with residual symptoms.

Measure both.

## 67. Residual symptoms matter

Residual symptoms can predict poorer course in some disorders.

But:

`residual symptom -> inevitable relapse`

is invalid.

## 68. Compensation can support recovery

Recovery can occur because vulnerability decreases, a trigger disappears, a maintaining loop breaks, environmental support increases, or compensatory skills increase.

Different routes can produce the same remission endpoint.

## 69. Clinical staging can organize course without proving biology

The 2026 Nature Reviews Psychology staging framework spans asymptomatic, mixed/subthreshold, full-threshold, recurrent, and treatment-resistant states.

Staging can improve monitoring.

It does not prove that all people follow one linear sequence.

---

# XI. Recurrence and relapse

## 70. Recurrence risk is dynamic

Risk after remission can depend on episode history, residual symptoms, treatment continuation, stress, sleep, comorbidity, age/development, and environment.

No universal recurrence probability exists.

## 71. Historical labels may hide continuous risk

Depression research has challenged fixed duration boundaries separating “relapse” from “recurrence”.

Prognosis may improve gradually with duration of remission rather than at one natural time point.

Course categories can therefore be operational conveniences.

## 72. Recurrence is not treatment failure by definition

Recurrence can occur after effective acute treatment because vulnerability persists, new exposures occur, protective conditions change, or treatment effect was time-limited.

Evaluate the causal question explicitly.

## 73. Fear of recurrence is a distinct process

Concern about recurrence can itself affect monitoring, avoidance, distress, and functioning.

A 2026 transdiagnostic model suggests this process may operate across mental health conditions.

Do not confuse:

`risk of recurrence`

with:

`fear of recurrence`.

---

# XII. Bidirectionality

## 74. Psychopathology can be both cause and consequence

Examples include poor sleep ↔ symptoms, isolation ↔ depression, stress ↔ psychopathology, and emotion dysregulation ↔ symptoms.

## 75. Cross-lagged evidence must separate stable differences

Traditional cross-lagged models can mix between-person differences and within-person change.

RI-CLPM and related models attempt separation.

No panel model automatically solves unmeasured confounding.

## 76. Reciprocal models can be more plausible than one-way models

If person and environment continuously influence each other, a single arrow may be incomplete.

Use transactional models when supported.

---

# XIII. Causal-model limits

## 77. Longitudinal precedence is necessary, not sufficient

`X at T1 -> Y at T2`

can still reflect shared causes, selection, stable traits, measurement error, prior Y affecting X, or time-varying confounding.

## 78. Mediation requires causal assumptions

A mediation model needs assumptions concerning exposure–outcome, exposure–mediator, and mediator–outcome confounding, post-exposure confounding, temporality, consistency, and positivity.

A significant indirect effect is not mechanism proof.

## 79. Statistical network edges are not causal arrows

Symptom-network models can characterize conditional associations.

Without causal identification:

`edge -> direct causal symptom interaction`

is too strong.

## 80. Dynamic-systems models are promising but not automatically identified

Complexity/dynamical approaches can model attractors, transitions, feedback, and early-warning signals.

Current work remains limited by dense-data requirements, measurement granularity, model specification, replication, and uncertain clinical prediction.

## 81. Critical slowing down is not yet a universal clinical warning signal

Some studies report early-warning patterns before transitions.

Evidence is insufficient for routine person-level forecasting across disorders.

## 82. Mendelian randomization is not causal proof by genetics

MR can strengthen causal inference under instrument relevance, independence, and exclusion-restriction assumptions.

Pleiotropy, population structure, weak instruments, selection, and phenotype definition can bias inference.

A 2026 Molecular Psychiatry review emphasizes critical evaluation rather than automatic causal interpretation.

## 83. Natural experiments and quasi-experiments remain assumption-bound

Strong causal inference from observational settings requires explicit estimand, identification strategy, defensible assumptions, and sensitivity analysis.

Adjusted regression alone is not enough.

---

# XIV. Person–environment systems

## 84. Environment is not an external constant

The experienced environment can be shaped by selection, evocation, behavior, social responses, and institutional constraints.

## 85. Symptoms can alter exposure

Withdrawal can reduce social contact, irritability can increase conflict, cognitive impairment can affect work stress, and avoidance can reduce corrective experiences.

These are hypotheses requiring evidence, not blame statements.

## 86. Environment can alter symptoms

Examples include chronic stress, discrimination, deprivation, trauma, role overload, instability, and social support.

Bidirectional models can be necessary.

## 87. Feedback loops can stabilize psychopathology

A self-reinforcing loop can persist after the initial trigger disappears.

Therefore:

`remove trigger -> immediate recovery`

is not guaranteed.

---

# XV. Resilience and protection

## 88. Resilience is not invulnerability

Resilience can be defined as relatively good adaptation despite meaningful adversity/risk.

It is context-dependent, outcome-dependent, and time-dependent.

## 89. Resilience is not a fixed trait by default

Protective adaptation can arise from personal capacities, relationships, resources, institutions, timing, and opportunity.

## 90. Apparent resilience can be domain-specific

Good work functioning does not imply low distress, good relationships, or absence of symptoms.

Outcome must be specified.

## 91. Protective processes can change over time

A factor protective at one developmental stage can be neutral or costly in another context.

Developmental timing routes to DEV.

---

# XVI. Course prediction

## 92. Group-level prognostic factors do not determine individual outcome

A factor associated with worse average course changes probability.

It does not establish destiny.

## 93. Calibration matters

A prognostic model should be evaluated for discrimination, calibration, external validation, and target-population transport.

## 94. Prediction and mechanism remain distinct

A strong prognostic predictor may be noncausal.

A causal mechanism may have limited predictive power when other factors dominate.

---

# XVII. Transdiagnostic process hierarchy

## 95. Level 0 — cross-sectional correlate

Process X is associated with symptoms across disorders.

No causal claim.

## 96. Level 1 — prospective predictor

X predicts later symptoms/course.

Temporal precedence established.

## 97. Level 2 — within-person temporal relation

Within-person deviations in X predict later within-person symptom change.

Stronger process evidence, still not full causality.

## 98. Level 3 — experimental perturbation

Manipulating X changes symptoms or process-relevant outcomes.

Supports causal effect of the manipulation under design assumptions.

## 99. Level 4 — causal mediation/process evidence

Change in X mediates effect on Y with correct temporal order, explicit assumptions, and competing pathways evaluated.

## 100. Level 5 — replicated mechanism

Converging evidence across samples, methods, disorders, contexts, and interventions.

Only here should strong general mechanistic language be used.

---

# XVIII. Course inference hierarchy

## 101. Level 0 — current symptom state

Current symptoms measured.

## 102. Level 1 — short-term fluctuation

Repeated measures establish change over days/weeks.

## 103. Level 2 — episode/course pattern

Longitudinal evidence establishes persistence, remission, recurrence, or diagnostic shift.

## 104. Level 3 — prognostic model

Predictors estimate future-course probability with validation.

## 105. Level 4 — causal course factor

A design identifies an effect of exposure/intervention on course.

## 106. Level 5 — mechanism of course

Independent process evidence explains how the causal factor alters onset, maintenance, remission, or recurrence.

# XIX. High-value failure modes

## Failure A — risk factor = cause

Incorrect. Association and prediction are not causal identification.

## Failure B — antecedent = cause

Incorrect. Temporal precedence is necessary but not sufficient.

## Failure C — transdiagnostic = universal

Incorrect. A process can span diagnoses without applying to every person.

## Failure D — transdiagnostic = mechanism

Incorrect. Cross-diagnostic association does not establish causation.

## Failure E — mediator = mechanism

Incorrect. Causal mediation requires strong assumptions and temporal evidence.

## Failure F — maintenance correlate = maintenance mechanism

Incorrect. Presence during illness can be cause, consequence, or confounded marker.

## Failure G — onset mechanism = maintenance mechanism

Incorrect. Different stages can involve different processes.

## Failure H — remission = mechanism fixed

Incorrect. Recovery can arise through compensation, context change, or multiple pathways.

## Failure I — recurrence = same mechanism returned

Incorrect. A similar syndrome can recur through different pathways.

## Failure J — relapse/recurrence distinction = natural boundary

Incorrect. Operational thresholds vary and can lack empirical discontinuity.

## Failure K — state = trait

Incorrect. Momentary elevation does not prove stable propensity.

## Failure L — trait = immutable

Incorrect. Relative stability does not mean fixed.

## Failure M — between-person = within-person

Incorrect. Nomothetic associations do not identify idiographic processes.

## Failure N — repeated measures = causality

Incorrect. Longitudinal models remain confounded unless identification is stronger.

## Failure O — cross-lagged coefficient = causal arrow

Incorrect. Stable differences and unmeasured confounding can remain.

## Failure P — network edge = symptom causation

Incorrect. Conditional dependence is not causal direction.

## Failure Q — protective factor = inverse risk factor

Incorrect. Protection can be nonlinear, context-specific, or interactive.

## Failure R — modifiable = causal

Incorrect. A modifiable correlate may not affect outcome when intervened upon.

## Failure S — strong predictor = mechanism

Incorrect. Prediction and explanation are different tasks.

## Failure T — response to mechanism-targeted therapy proves mechanism

Incorrect. Treatments can have multiple active ingredients and downstream pathways.

## Failure U — resilience = personality trait

Incorrect. Resilience is an adaptation outcome/process under specified adversity.

## Failure V — stress generation = blame

Incorrect. Person-linked stress exposure is a causal-process hypothesis, not moral responsibility.

## Failure W — staging = universal linear sequence

Incorrect. Stages are monitoring/organization tools, not mandatory trajectories.

# XX. 82-question Psychopathology Mechanisms / Course Inference Gate

## A. Target process

1. What exact process is proposed?
2. Cognitive, affective, behavioral, interpersonal, biological, or environmental?
3. At what timescale?
4. At what level of analysis?
5. Is it measured directly or inferred?
6. Does the measure overlap with the outcome?

## B. Stage specificity

7. Is the claim about onset?
8. Maintenance?
9. Persistence?
10. Remission?
11. Recovery?
12. Relapse?
13. Recurrence?
14. Is the same mechanism being assumed across stages?

## C. Risk/protection

15. Is the variable merely associated with risk?
16. Does it precede onset?
17. Is confounding addressed?
18. Is reverse causation plausible?
19. Is the variable modifiable?
20. Is modification causally linked to outcome?
21. Is protection being treated as inverse risk?
22. Is timing/dose/duration represented?
23. Is a population attributable fraction being misread as individual causation?

## D. Transdiagnosticity

24. Across which disorders/dimensions is the process observed?
25. Are methods comparable across disorders?
26. Is the process equally strong across disorders?
27. Is it present in nonclinical variation?
28. Is it universalized beyond the evidence?
29. Is cross-diagnostic presence being treated as causal proof?

## E. State–trait

30. Is the variable current state or stable propensity?
31. What proportion is between-person versus within-person?
32. Are repeated measurements available?
33. Is one cross-sectional score being treated as a trait?
34. Is a between-person association being applied within-person?
35. Is aggregation hiding dynamic states?
36. Does sampling frequency match process timescale?

## F. Longitudinal inference

37. Does X precede Y?
38. Could prior Y have caused X?
39. Are stable person differences separated?
40. Are time-varying confounders represented?
41. Is attrition selective?
42. Is measurement invariant across time?
43. Is lag length theoretically appropriate?
44. Does the model estimate within-person or between-person effects?

## G. Maintenance

45. Is the process elevated during active symptoms?
46. Does within-person change in process precede symptom change?
47. Has the process been manipulated?
48. Does intervention on the process alter symptoms?
49. Could symptom improvement cause process change?
50. Are competing maintenance pathways considered?

## H. Remission / recurrence

51. How is remission defined?
52. Is functional recovery measured separately?
53. How is relapse/recurrence defined?
54. Is the distinction empirical or conventional?
55. Are residual symptoms present?
56. Is recurrence being assumed to reproduce the same mechanism?
57. Are new exposures considered?
58. Is course probability calibrated to the relevant population?

## I. Feedback / environment

59. Could symptoms alter environmental exposure?
60. Could the environment alter symptoms?
61. Are dependent and independent stressors distinguished?
62. Is stress generation measured without stigmatizing inference?
63. Are feedback loops tested longitudinally?
64. Could social responses maintain the pattern?

## J. Causal claims

65. What causal estimand is implied?
66. What design identifies it?
67. Is exchangeability plausible?
68. Is positivity plausible?
69. Is consistency plausible?
70. Is interference relevant?
71. Are mediators/colliders/confounders distinguished?
72. Is mediation being interpreted causally without assumptions?
73. Is MR/quasi-experimental evidence being overclaimed?

## K. Final inference

74. What evidence supports correlation?
75. What supports prediction?
76. What supports temporality?
77. What supports within-person process?
78. What supports causal effect?
79. What supports mechanism?
80. Which alternative mechanisms remain?
81. What is the narrowest defensible conclusion?
82. What claim would exceed the evidence?

# XXI. Evidence-strength map

## High-confidence working rules

1. onset, maintenance, persistence, remission, recovery, relapse, and recurrence are distinct course targets;
2. risk marker, causal factor, mediator, moderator, and mechanism are distinct;
3. temporal precedence does not establish causality;

# Canonical working rule

**A clinical mechanism claim must identify the stage it explains, distinguish within-person process from between-person liability, survive alternative causal models, and remain separate from the diagnosis used to describe the syndrome.**

Use:

`course target -> candidate process -> temporal evidence -> within/between separation -> alternative causes -> causal identification/perturbation -> replication -> calibrated mechanism claim`.

Never:

`risk marker -> cause`

`transdiagnostic -> universal`

`mediator -> mechanism`

`longitudinal -> causal`

`between-person -> within-person`

`remission -> mechanism gone`

`recurrence -> same mechanism returned`

`network edge -> causal arrow`

`modifiable -> causal`

`resilience -> fixed trait`.

## Source-boundary note

Transdiagnostic reviews establish breadth of association and, where interventions are involved, some evidence of modifiability. They do not make every transdiagnostic process a universal causal mechanism.

Longitudinal cohort studies establish population course and temporal prediction, not individual destiny or causal identification.

Stress-generation research is treated as a reciprocal person–environment process and never as blame.

Relapse/recovery terminology is treated as disorder- and study-specific; depression evidence cautions against assuming fixed duration thresholds represent natural state boundaries.

Phase A causal-inference rules remain controlling: prediction, longitudinal precedence, mediation, network association, Mendelian randomization, and quasi-experimental estimates each require claim-matched assumptions before causal language is used.
