## Question card

### E1 — Fleeson (2001), foundational empirical studies
Toward a structure- and process-integrated view of personality: traits as density distributions of states. JPSP 80, 1011–1027.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.80.6.1011 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/
Access: abstract/search extract, not full article. Three experience-sampling studies over two to three weeks support coexistence of between-person tendencies and considerable intraindividual variation. The distributional account motivates describing average expression and variability separately. It does not identify a specific person's mechanisms from isolated acts.

### E2 — Four interaction types (2024 issue)
Distinguishing four types of Person × Situation interactions: An integrative framework and empirical examination. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37668571/ ; https://cris.uni-muenster.de/portal/en/publication/85739722
Access: abstract/search extract; direct PubMed opening blocked. Broad interaction variance, individual differences in situational associations, variation of trait associations across situations and specific trait-by-situation interactions are distinct estimates. Evidence reported relatively large broad interaction variation but generally small specific measured interactions. Broad variation is not evidence for any particular hypothesized mechanism.

### E3 — Bauditz et al. (2026), Construal and Contact in Person–Situation Relations: Evidence from Controlled and Naturalistic Designs
European Journal of Personality, online May 22, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1177/08902070261449352
Access: publisher article abstract, design, selected results, discussion, limitations and conclusion inspected; full end-to-end reading not established. Study 1: standardized online group conversations; methods report 186 retained participants and 3,094 assessments, while abstract reports 189 (unresolved discrepancy). Study 2: 1,596 participants and 72,211 assessments, German-speaking adults, mean age 28.67. State/personality and situational self-ratings; contextual reports of partner, place and activity. Study 2 trait–aggregated-state correlations .43–.63; state ICCs .48–.58. Some contextual moderation contrasts reversed direction; random slopes favored in all 45 naturalistic associations. Interpret as conditional associations, not deterministic laws. Constraints: self-reported context, differing scales, coarse categories, selection and imperfect laboratory standardization; no strict causal separation of contact and construal. Numerical discrepancy in Study 1 retained rather than silently reconciled. Exploratory analyses and multiplicity matter.

### E4 — Kritzler et al. (2024), Are state-trait fit and state-situation fit relevant for within-person dynamics of personality states?
JPSP 127(4), 880–900. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000519 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39570691/
Access: primary abstract and metadata inspected; full text not established. Three intensive longitudinal samples (194, 254, 374; 4,244, 7,667, 16,418 observations). Response-surface and multilevel analyses did not yield consistent evidence that state–trait or state–situation fit predicts momentary affect. Significant isolated findings failed to replicate across samples. This is evidence against a simple general fit account, not proof that all context-dependent affective processes are absent.

### E5 — Idiographic momentary profiles of personality facets (online 2025)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40658546/ ; https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000568
Access: indexed abstract/search excerpts, full-text access blocked. Two experience-sampling datasets, person-specific finite-mixture profiles. Multiple state profiles and situational associations suggest utility of representing within-person heterogeneity. Clusters are descriptive model outputs, not proof of separate personalities or intrinsic types. Independent model validity and generalization require checks.

### E6 — Target–observer momentary behavior agreement (2022 online)
Do People Agree on How They and Others are Acting? Examining the Degree of Target-Observer and Observer-Observer Agreement about Current Behavior as it Changes across Situations. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10921981/
Access: indexed primary article excerpt, opening blocked. 97 targets, 183 observers, 22 activities across 20 lab sessions; 3,493 target reports, 2,973 with corresponding observer data. Design provides a route for comparing momentary ratings and observable behavior. Agreement on observable variation cannot validate inferred private intentions. No quantitative agreement coefficient extracted.

Sources do not contradict the general existence of traits; the critical limitation concerns explanatory and predictive specificity. Full-text and systematic coverage remain incomplete.

## Synthesis: distinct analytical objects

- Trait: relatively enduring probabilistic tendency; descriptive construct, not a motive or a causal organ.
- State: transient manifestation of relevant thoughts, feeling or behavior; a rating is a measurement, not direct access to the latent state.
- Contact: the situation encountered, including opportunities, partners and constraints; reported contact is not necessarily objectively verified.
- Construal: the meaning a person assigns to the situation; distinct from both analyst interpretation and the observable situation.
- Behavior: the recorded action or omission with channel, timing and opportunity specified.
- Conditional regularity: repeatable association between context and behavior. Can coexist with broad trait tendencies. A stable conditional pattern need not imply one invariant underlying motive.
- Developmental change: enduring shift in a distribution or conditional pattern, distinguished from new environments, altered sampling, transient states and measurement changes. Full investigation deferred to IND-01.2.

An original conceptual measurement map, not an empirically fitted structural model:
observed behavior at time t = function of stable tendencies, transient state, encountered context, construal, goals, opportunities and prior interaction history, plus measurement/recording error.
These components may interact; construal and state may influence one another and context selection may depend on prior behavior. An observed outcome cannot uniquely identify all unobserved inputs. Avoid fitting an elaborate narrative simply because it can explain existing observations.

## Specialist application: context-conditioned inference

1. Specify the proposition and temporal scale: action, state, situation-specific tendency, broad trait or durable change.
2. Separate the data channels: direct behavior, direct report, third-party account, contextual fact and analyst inference. Never infer internal motivation just because the external behavior is recorded.
3. Specify baseline and opportunity: comparable situations, repeated occasions, observation coverage, partner/activity/location, constraints and missing data. No fixed universal minimum number of observations; adequacy depends on variability, precision, sampling and stakes.
4. Compare plausible models: enduring trait, transient state, changed opportunity, construal, context selection or combinations. Distinguish the model from its evidence.
5. Check direction and level: does the association within one person equal an across-person association? Not necessarily. Does the association reverse across contexts? Examine before pooling.
6. Derive discriminating, nonintrusive observations prospectively; compare competing predictions with subsequent independent evidence when available. No deception, coercion or engineered distress.
7. Report narrowest justified proposition with uncertainty and revision condition. A stable pattern is not the same as a proven psychological mechanism.

## Synthetic regression exercises

These invented examples are unrelated to private material. Rubric fixed for this note before evaluating the following answers: one point each for correct construct separation, at least one competing explanation, and correct evidential limit/revision condition, per exercise. Critical errors (unsupported motive/diagnosis, causal inflation, group-to-person certainty or privacy breach) block the exercise. These are transparent self-checks, not independent validation.

A. A museum guide speaks confidently during tours but rarely speaks in a technical staff meeting. What is justified?
Answer: context-linked difference in speaking behavior; possible differences in expertise, role expectations or perceived evaluation. Neither global extraversion nor fear is established. Compare comparable meetings and tours with verified opportunity to speak. Self-score 3/3.

B. A volunteer's average organization rating rises after a new role introduces mandatory checklists. Does personality change follow?
Answer: improved measured organization under the new conditions, not necessarily enduring trait change. External scaffolding and changed sampling compete with trait change. Compare similar tasks with and without scaffolding across time and use stable measures. Self-score 3/3.

C. In a hypothetical workshop dataset, higher reported difficulty accompanies greater assertiveness during collaborative tasks but lower assertiveness during solo practice. What may pooling conceal?
Answer: contextual moderation, potentially a sign reversal. Task affordance, perception and selection are alternative explanations. Stratify by task and collect comparable repeated observations; no causal claim or global trait label. Self-score 3/3.

D. A group's mean personality rating predicts a group's mean outcome, but a particular person's repeated ratings show a different pattern. Which estimate governs an individual forecast?
Answer: distinguish between-person and within-person estimands; individual repeated evidence may support a contextual forecast if measured reliably, while group estimate may serve only as a qualified prior. Check out-of-sample prediction; no exact forecast probability from group association alone. Self-score 3/3.
