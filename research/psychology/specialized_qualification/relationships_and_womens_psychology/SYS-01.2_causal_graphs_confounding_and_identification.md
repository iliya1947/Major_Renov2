## 3. Causal construct map and identification
A causal estimand specifies the contrast, unit/population, exposure or intervention versions, comparator, outcome and horizon. For a well-defined binary exposure A and outcome Y, an average total effect can be written E[Y(1)-Y(0)]. Identification asks whether this quantity is uniquely determined by the observed distribution under stated assumptions; estimation asks how to estimate it from finite data. An association may predict an outcome without identifying the effect of altering its predictor.

For simple adjustment by pre-exposure covariates L, the identification formula E[Y(a)] = sum_l E[Y|A=a,L=l]P(L=l) applies for discrete L if conditional exchangeability, positivity and consistency hold, with relevant interference addressed and measurement/selection assumptions defensible. Consistency links observed outcomes to the actually received well-defined exposure; positivity requires exposure support within relevant covariate strata; exchangeability is the absence of confounding conditional on the specified adjustment set, not an automatic consequence of including many variables. In a dyad, one person's outcome may depend on both people's exposures: Y_A(a_A,a_B). Define own and cross-person causal contrasts under specified joint assignments rather than assume no interference. An APIM partner coefficient is not automatically a causal spillover estimate.

Three illustrative structures:
- A <- U -> Y: confounding path; an adequate pre-exposure U adjustment can block it if the assumed graph is correct.
- A -> M -> Y: mediator; conditioning on M changes the question and generally does not estimate the total effect; direct/indirect effects require additional assumptions.
- A -> S <- Y: collider; conditioning on S can create association where none exists. Retention or observation may depend on both variables; selecting observed episodes can distort the inferred relation.
These are minimal examples, not universal instructions to adjust or exclude a variable based on its name.

Synthetic demonstration (not empirical): let A and Y be independent fair binary variables, and define S=1 if either A=1 or Y=1. In the population P(Y=1|A=1)=P(Y=1|A=0)=1/2. In the selected S=1 sample, P(Y=1|A=1,S=1)=1/2 and P(Y=1|A=0,S=1)=1. Selection alone induces a negative association. Conversely, observational equivalence is possible: one model has a fair binary A causing Y=A; another has a fair binary U causing both A=U and Y=U, with no A->Y arrow. Both yield the same joint distribution of A and Y, while their causal contrasts differ (1 and 0 respectively). In the latter deterministic example positivity given U fails. Perfect predictive association does not distinguish the two mechanisms.

Feedback is compatible with an acyclic graph when unfolded into time-indexed nodes: A_t -> B_(t+1) -> A_(t+2). Include prior states, common inputs and relevant measurement/selection nodes. Within-wave temporal ordering cannot be recovered merely by choosing an arrow. A time-dependent variable can mediate earlier exposure and confound later exposure. Example A0 -> L1 -> A1, L1 -> Y2, with earlier A0 also potentially influencing Y2. Simple conditioning on L1 may remove part of earlier exposure's total effect, while failing to account for L1 may confound later exposure. Define whether a sustained exposure regime or a single-time contrast is targeted; g-methods can be appropriate under explicit sequential exchangeability, consistency, positivity and correct relevant model assumptions. No fitted model or estimation attempted here.

CLPM, RI-CLPM, latent growth/residual and change-score models encode different decompositions and dynamic estimands. RI-CLPM can represent stable between-person differences separately from within-person deviations, but does not automatically resolve time-varying confounding, measurement error, unequal lags, selection, interference or misspecification. Model fit is insufficient to identify a graph; alternative graphs may imply the same observable distribution. A single dyad cannot reliably estimate population-level random effects or unobserved causal parameters simply by accumulating unstructured observations.

## 4. Operational procedure
1. State the exact question and whether it is descriptive, predictive or causal. Define a plausible causal contrast only when meaningful; distinguish naturally observed choices from hypothetical interventions and their feasibility.
2. Construct competing time-indexed causal graphs with explicit assumptions. Include common causes, prior states, mediator candidates, selection, measurement and partner/shared inputs where relevant. Do not promote a preferred psychological story into a known graph.
3. Identify candidate adjustment sets by causal roles and paths, not by correlations or statistical significance. Distinguish pre-exposure confounding from post-exposure mediation and collider selection. Retain unobserved confounders as unresolved.
4. Evaluate timing, consistency of constructs, positivity, measurement quality, missingness, interference and identification strategy. Compare different model specifications and note which estimands change. An observational lag alone is not identification.
5. For sequential exposures, specify the causal regime and investigate treatment-confounder feedback before choosing methods. Do not apply advanced estimators to insufficient data or invent weights or effects.
6. Check model predictions against appropriate observations, including contrary evidence. Better prediction or fit does not establish causation. Separate uncertainty about effects from uncertainty about the assumed graph.
7. Make decisions under uncertainty without requiring certainty about another person's motives. Use only legitimate information and voluntary communication; no engineered perturbations, manipulation or monitoring. Explicit choices, safety and autonomy remain independent constraints.
