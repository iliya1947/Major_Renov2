## 2. “Core” — project definition

“Core” is useful training shorthand but is **not one anatomical organ**.

For this project, trunk/core-related anatomy can include:

- vertebral column;
- intrinsic back musculature;
- abdominal wall;
- quadratus lumborum;
- diaphragm;
- pelvic floor;
- thoracolumbar and abdominal aponeurotic/fascial structures;
- rib cage;
- pelvis;
- hip-linked muscles depending task.

Project rule:

**Whenever “core” is used, translate it into the actual structure/function being discussed.**

No single muscle equals “the core”.

---

## 3. Intrinsic vs extrinsic back-muscle boundary

The project distinguishes:

### Extrinsic back-related muscles

These connect the trunk with upper limb/ribs or serve shoulder/respiratory functions.

Examples:
- trapezius;
- latissimus dorsi;
- rhomboids;
- levator scapulae;
- serratus posterior.

These are not the main target of ANA-07 because shoulder roles were handled in ANA-04.

### Intrinsic / true back muscles

Epaxial musculature associated with the vertebral column and supplied predominantly by dorsal rami.

Main structural groups for project use:

1. splenius;
2. erector spinae;
3. transversospinalis;
4. small segmental muscles.

### Terminology caution

Current reference sources differ somewhat in how they label “superficial/intermediate/deep” layers within the intrinsic back musculature.

Project rule:

**prioritize named muscle groups over one rigid layer-label taxonomy.**

This avoids a classification dispute that does not change training decisions.

---

## 4. Erector spinae

Canonical columns:

1. **iliocostalis** — lateral;
2. **longissimus** — intermediate;
3. **spinalis** — medial.

These longitudinal systems extend from broad caudal/common attachments toward:

- ribs;
- transverse processes;
- spinous processes;
- skull depending subdivision.

Project rule:

**erector spinae ≠ one homogeneous “lower-back muscle”.**

---

## 5. Erector-spinae subdivisions

### Iliocostalis
- lumborum;
- thoracis;
- cervicis.

### Longissimus
- thoracis;
- cervicis;
- capitis.

### Spinalis
- thoracis;
- cervicis/capitis components may be poorly developed or variable.

Broad anatomical roles:

- bilateral extension / control of flexion;
- unilateral lateral-flexion contribution;
- regional rotation contributions depending fascicle orientation;
- postural/load-control roles.

Exact lift-specific force and activation → MEC/PHY.

## 6. Transversospinalis group

Core group:

1. semispinalis;
2. multifidus;
3. rotatores.

Located deep to erector spinae and organized around transverse-to-spinous-process relationships.

Broad roles include:

- extension;
- rotational contribution;
- segmental control;
- posture/proprioceptive function.

Innervation:
- dorsal rami.

Project rule:

**transversospinalis is a family of muscles with region-specific geometry, not one generic “deep stabilizer”.**

---

## 7. Lumbar multifidus — current update

Legacy anatomy commonly summarizes multifidus fascicles as spanning several vertebral segments.

That remains a useful orientation mnemonic, but current 2026 anatomical work shows that lumbar multifidus bundle architecture is more complex than one uniform “2–4 levels” model.

Harano et al. 2026 found:

- distinct bundle patterns when dissected from insertion;
- one bundle associated with inferior lumbar mammillary / lateral sacral origins;
- another pennate bundle with contributions including thoracolumbar fascial/aponeurotic origin;
- prior descriptions of origin/insertion and bundle structure remain inconsistent enough to justify anatomical reappraisal.

Project rule:

**do not encode one universal lumbar-multifidus fascicle-span formula as exact anatomy.**

High-confidence project claims:

1. lumbar multifidus is a major deep paraspinal/transversospinalis structure;
2. it has segmentally organized, nonuniform fascicle architecture;
3. it participates in spinal motion/control;
4. exact bundle-specific training claims require anatomy/biomechanics evidence beyond a textbook mnemonic.

## 8. Semispinalis / rotatores / small segmental muscles

### Semispinalis

Regional parts:
- thoracis;
- cervicis;
- capitis.

Broad roles:
- extension;
- contralateral rotation.

### Rotatores

Small deep transversospinalis muscles, prominent in thoracic region.

Potential roles:
- local motion/control;
- proprioceptive function.

### Small segmental muscles

Include:
- interspinales;
- intertransversarii;
- levatores costarum.

Project use:

Recognize these structures, but do not inflate low-priority details into exercise-program taxonomy.

## 9. Abdominal wall — structural organization

Major anterolateral muscles:

1. rectus abdominis;
2. external oblique;
3. internal oblique;
4. transversus abdominis.

Pyramidalis is variable and lower priority.

Lateral wall layers from superficial to deep:

1. external oblique;
2. internal oblique;
3. transversus abdominis.

These continue into broad aponeuroses.

Project rule:

**the abdominal wall is a layered musculofascial/aponeurotic system, not four independent cylinders.**

---

## 10. Rectus abdominis

Paired vertical muscle.

Broad attachments:

- pubic crest / symphysis region;
- xiphoid process;
- costal-cartilage region of upper abdominal wall.

Interrupted by tendinous intersections.

Broad roles:

- trunk flexion;
- abdominal-wall tension/compression;
- pelvic-rib-cage force relationship;
- forceful expiration contribution.

Project rule:

There is no separate anatomical “lower abs muscle”.

Regional architecture/loading questions → ANA-11/PHY/TEC.

---

## 11. Rectus sheath / linea alba

Rectus sheath is formed by abdominal-muscle aponeuroses.

Its arrangement changes above vs below the arcuate line.

Linea alba:
- midline fibrous/aponeurotic raphe;
- connects left/right abdominal-wall aponeuroses;
- extends from xiphoid region toward pubic symphysis.

Project implication:

**abdominal force transmission is distributed through aponeurotic sheets and midline connective tissue, not only contractile muscle bellies.**

---

## 12. External oblique

Broad architecture:

- arises from lower ribs;
- fibers generally inferomedial;
- contributes aponeurosis to linea alba / abdominal wall;
- connects with iliac/inguinal structures.

Broad roles:

- bilateral trunk flexion/compression;
- unilateral contralateral-rotation contribution;
- lateral-flexion contribution.

Exact task recruitment → MEC/PHY.

---

## 13. Internal oblique

Broad attachments:

- thoracolumbar-fascial / iliac / inguinal regions;
- lower ribs;
- linea alba / pubic aponeurotic structures.

Fiber direction varies regionally.

Broad roles:

- abdominal-wall tension/compression;
- ipsilateral-rotation contribution;
- lateral flexion;
- trunk flexion contribution.

Project rule:

Do not infer fixed activation ratios from fiber orientation alone.

---

## 14. Transversus abdominis

Deepest principal lateral abdominal muscle.

Broad attachments include:

- lower costal-cartilage region;
- thoracolumbar-fascial / posterior abdominal-wall relationships;
- iliac crest;
- inguinal structures;
- linea alba/aponeurosis.

Fibers predominantly transverse.

Broad role:

- abdominal-wall tension/compression;
- contribution to pressure/stiffness system.

Project rule:

**TrA is not anatomically “the deep core” or the only spinal stabilizer.**

“TrA must activate first” is not an anatomical conclusion.

---

## 15. Oblique / abdominal-wall integration

EO, IO, TrA and rectus integrate through:

- aponeuroses;
- rectus sheath;
- linea alba;
- thoracolumbar / posterior connective tissues;
- rib and pelvic attachments.

Classical rotation pairings remain useful orientation examples, but task-specific recruitment is not fixed.

Project rule:

**fiber direction supports action possibilities; it does not supply an EMG prescription.**

---

## 16. Quadratus lumborum

Broad attachment regions:

- iliac crest;
- 12th rib;
- lumbar transverse processes.

QL lies in the posterior abdominal/trunk region and interfaces with thoracolumbar fascial and diaphragmatic structures.

### 2026 update

Lee et al. 2026 examined 10 embalmed older-adult cadavers and found reproducible QL layer organization with:

- lumbocostal;
- iliocostal;
- iliolumbar layers;

plus layer-specific morphometric and neural heterogeneity.

Project conclusion:

**QL is anatomically nonuniform and should not be reduced to one simple force vector.**

But:

- study population was small and cadaveric;
- layer-specific neural findings do not by themselves prove exercise-specific functional specialization.

Broad action descriptions such as lateral-flexion / rib / lumbopelvic contributions remain orientation-level only.

---

## 17. Thoracolumbar fascia

TLF is a named regional connective-tissue structure in the posterior trunk.

It relates structurally to:

- erector spinae;
- multifidus;
- QL;
- latissimus dorsi;
- abdominal aponeuroses;
- pelvis / sacral region.

### Current layer-model nuance

Current anatomical literature uses more than one TLF layer model.

A 2025 systematic microstructural review describes both:

- three-layer models;
- simplified two-layer models;

and reports multilaminar collagen-rich organization.

A 2026 Journal of Anatomy study further refines the posterior-layer architecture.

Project rule:

**TLF is real regional anatomy, but one exact layer taxonomy should not be treated as universally settled.**

And, consistent with ANA-02:

**TLF does not justify generic whole-body “fascial chain” training claims.**

Detailed force transmission → MEC.

## 18. Diaphragm

Diaphragm = dome-shaped musculotendinous partition between thoracic and abdominal cavities.

It is the principal inspiratory muscle.

Main origin/attachment regions:

1. sternal;
2. costal;
3. lumbar crura / arcuate structures.

Fibers converge on central tendon.

Innervation:
- motor: right/left phrenic nerves, C3–C5.

---

## 19. Diaphragm and pressure anatomy

Current NCBI reference explicitly identifies diaphragm roles in:

- inspiration;
- assisting abdominal muscles in raising intra-abdominal pressure during straining;
- pressure changes between thorax and abdomen.

Project structural model:

Diaphragm forms the **superior muscular boundary** of the abdominal pressure-containing region.

But anatomy alone does not determine:

- exact bracing strategy;
- exact IAP needed;
- how much spinal unloading occurs;
- one breathing technique for lifting.

Those are PHY/MEC/TEC questions.

---

## 20. Diaphragm openings

Major openings:

1. caval;
2. esophageal;
3. aortic.

Project implication:

Diaphragm is a perforated musculotendinous partition, not a sealed piston.

This matters when using “pressure cylinder” as a teaching model.

## 21. Intercostal boundary

Intercostal muscle layers support and move the thoracic cage.

They are relevant because trunk function and breathing involve:

- diaphragm;
- rib cage;
- intercostals;
- abdominal wall.

Detailed respiratory mechanics belongs to PHY/MEC.

ANA-07 only preserves this structural dependency.

## 22. Pelvic floor — current structural model

Current NCBI pelvic-floor reference, updated 2026-05-13, defines pelvic floor as a musculofascial complex forming the inferior boundary of the pelvis.

Major muscular components:

- levator ani;
- coccygeus.

Levator ani commonly includes:

1. puborectalis;
2. pubococcygeus / pubovisceral components;
3. iliococcygeus.

Supported by:
- endopelvic fascia;
- perineal membrane and associated tissues.

---

## 23. Pelvic-floor broad functions

Current 2026 reference supports roles in:

1. pelvic-viscera support;
2. continence;
3. defecation / micturition coordination;
4. contribution to respiration;
5. contribution to lumbopelvic stability.

Project rule:

**pelvic floor is part of trunk pressure/support anatomy, but that does not mean every lifter needs isolated pelvic-floor training.**

Training/clinical need → TEC/MED/IND.

## 24. Structural pressure-system model

Useful project boundaries:

### Superior
- diaphragm.

### Anterolateral
- abdominal wall.

### Posterior
- vertebral column;
- intrinsic paraspinals;
- QL;
- thoracolumbar connective tissues.

### Inferior
- pelvic floor.

This is an anatomical **boundary model** for later intra-abdominal-pressure research.

Project caution:

**“canister/cylinder” is a teaching model, not a literal rigid sealed container.**

Reasons include:
- diaphragm openings;
- compliant abdominal wall;

## 25. Rib-cage / pelvis relationship

Abdominal wall spans/connects structures of:

- lower ribs/thorax;
- pelvis;
- linea alba;
- posterior aponeurotic/fascial structures.

Therefore rib-cage and pelvic orientation can change muscle geometry.

Project rule:

**anatomy does not identify one universally optimal ribcage–pelvis position.**

Exact lifting implications → MEC/TEC.

---

## 26. Trunk movement families

Anatomical movement families include:

1. flexion;
2. extension;
3. lateral flexion;
4. rotation.

Real tasks can combine these and can also involve:

- resisting motion;
- maintaining stiffness;
- breathing while generating external force.

Visible ROM is therefore not required for trunk muscles to produce force.

Exact isometric / anti-motion mechanics → MEC.

---

## 27. “Bracing” / “hollowing” boundary

Anatomy can identify muscles and structural relationships.

It cannot by itself establish:

- draw-in/hollowing as superior;
- “360° brace” as universally superior;
- TrA-first timing;
- one universal safe spine strategy.

These require:
- neurophysiology;
- biomechanics;
- outcome evidence.

---

## 28. Multifidus / “inner core” myth boundary

Multifidus is important deep paraspinal anatomy.

TrA, diaphragm and pelvic floor are also important structures.

But anatomical depth does **not** imply:

- universal weakness;
- universal delayed activation;
- mandatory isolated pre-activation;
- superiority over larger trunk muscles.

Project rule:

**depth is anatomy, not a hierarchy of training importance.**

## 31. What changed versus legacy ANA-07

### Confirmed

Legacy core remains valid:

- “core” = functional shorthand, not organ;
- erector spinae = iliocostalis + longissimus + spinalis;
- transversospinalis = semispinalis + multifidus + rotatores;
- abdominal wall = rectus + EO + IO + TrA plus aponeurotic integration;
- QL links pelvic/lumbar/12th-rib region;
- diaphragm = principal inspiratory muscle and superior pressure boundary;
- pelvic floor = inferior musculofascial support boundary;
- TLF = named regional connective-tissue structure;
- no one muscle is “the core stabilizer”.

### Strengthened / updated

1. **Lumbar multifidus:** 2026 anatomical reappraisal shows more complex bundle architecture and continued disagreement in exact origin/insertion descriptions; the project drops rigid “always 2–4 vertebral levels” precision.
2. **QL:** 2026 3D micro-CT/immunofluorescence study supports reproducible three-layer internal organization and layer-specific neural heterogeneity, while functional specialization remains unproven.
3. **Thoracolumbar fascia:** current literature explicitly contains competing two-layer vs three-layer models; project keeps the exact layer taxonomy flexible.
4. **Pelvic floor:** current NCBI source updated 2026 explicitly retains respiration and lumbopelvic-stability contributions in addition to continence/support functions.
5. **Diaphragm:** current reference preserves both respiratory and abdominal-pressure roles; project retains it as a pressure-system boundary without prescribing lifting technique.

### Tightened

1. No “inner core” hierarchy.
2. No TrA-first rule.
3. No fixed multifidus fascicle-span rule.
4. No mystical whole-body TLF chain.
5. No single universally optimal ribcage–pelvis or bracing position.

## 32. Established findings

### HIGH

1. Erector spinae comprises iliocostalis, longissimus and spinalis systems.
2. Transversospinalis comprises semispinalis, multifidus and rotatores.
3. Intrinsic back muscles are supplied predominantly by dorsal rami.
4. Abdominal wall is a layered/aponeurotic system integrating rectus, EO, IO and TrA.
5. Diaphragm is the principal inspiratory muscle and participates in abdominal-pressure generation.
6. Pelvic floor is an inferior musculofascial support/pressure-boundary complex.
7. QL is anatomically nonuniform rather than a simple single-vector muscle.
8. TLF is a multilayer regional connective-tissue structure with muscle/aponeurosis relationships.
9. “Core” is not one anatomical organ.

### MODERATE / MODEL-DEPENDENT

1. Exact lumbar-multifidus bundle boundaries/origin-insertion taxonomy remain under active anatomical refinement.
2. Exact TLF layer nomenclature depends on the anatomical model used.
3. Exact functional significance of QL layer-specific neural architecture remains uncertain.
4. Task-specific contribution of each trunk muscle requires biomechanics/neurophysiology rather than anatomy alone.
