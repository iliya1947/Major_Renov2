# SAF-01 — Safety and crisis foundations: acute-risk recognition, suicide/self-harm assessment boundaries, emergency/urgent/routine escalation, medical/psychiatric red flags, remote-assessment limits, and referral logic

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Establish the generic safety-triage architecture required before any specialized crisis-management block.

The purpose is not to predict rare catastrophic events with false precision.

The purpose is to answer:

> When does available evidence justify emergency escalation, urgent specialist assessment, routine follow-up, or continued observation — and what can *not* be safely inferred from screens, symptoms, labels, or remote conversation alone?

This block integrates:

1. direct versus inferred safety evidence;
2. self-harm versus suicidal intent;
3. current suicidal thoughts, plan/access, past behavior, and changing intent;
4. screening versus assessment versus disposition;
5. limitations of risk tools and global low/medium/high categories;
6. emergency versus urgent versus routine referral logic;
7. psychiatric red flags;
8. medical red flags;
9. violence/coercion and safeguarding boundaries;
10. remote/telehealth safety limitations;
11. safety planning versus safety contracts;
12. means-safety principles;
13. uncertainty/documentation;
14. limits of AI-assisted psychological safety inference.

Canonical rule:

`direct safety evidence -> current state -> immediacy -> intent/plan/access/capability -> recent behavior/change -> medical/psychiatric state -> support/environment -> remote-assessment limitations -> least-delaying appropriate escalation -> uncertainty documented`.

Never:

`screen score -> future suicide prediction`

or:

`no disclosed intent -> safe`.

# I. Question Card

## 1. Concrete dependency

The current qualification can already:

- recognize psychopathology patterns without overdiagnosis;
- distinguish intrusive thoughts from intent;
- distinguish coercive control from ordinary relationship conflict;
- distinguish acute stress from pathology;
- reason about uncertainty and decision thresholds.

It still requires an explicit safety layer because high-stakes failure can occur if:

- a screening result is mistaken for a prediction;
- absence of disclosure is mistaken for absence of risk;
- self-harm is assumed to have one motive;
- intrusive thoughts are mistaken for intent;
- suicidal ideation is assumed to imply one exact disposition;
- severe confusion is psychologized instead of medically assessed;
- mania/psychosis/intoxication/withdrawal are treated as routine outpatient problems despite acute danger;
- remote assessment overstates what can be verified;
- a "safety contract" is treated as protection;
- support/referral is delayed while trying to perfect psychological explanation.

# II. Safety reasoning is threshold reasoning, not prophecy

## 12. The core task is not exact prediction

Rare catastrophic outcomes are difficult to predict accurately at the individual level.

Safety reasoning asks:

- Is there enough evidence of current danger or instability to justify escalation now?
- What delay cost would be unacceptable?
- What can be safely managed at the current level of care?

## 13. Prediction and disposition are different

A person may have:

- uncertain long-term suicide probability;
- but clear current need for urgent evaluation.

Conversely, a historical risk factor may increase long-term risk without requiring emergency intervention today.

## 14. Risk factor != acute danger

Examples of risk factors can include:

- prior suicidal behavior;
- psychiatric disorder;
- substance problems;
- chronic pain;
- social adversity.

They alter concern.

They do not by themselves determine current disposition.

## 15. Warning sign != deterministic prediction

Current change in:

- thoughts;
- behavior;
- distress;
- planning;
- access;
- functioning

can matter for immediate safety.

Still, no single sign guarantees an outcome.

# III. Screening, assessment, formulation, and disposition are distinct

## 16. Screening

Purpose:

> Identify who may need further assessment.

A screen is not a full risk formulation.

## 17. Brief safety assessment

Purpose:

> Clarify current thoughts/behavior, immediacy, plan/access, history, symptoms, stressors, supports, and whether comprehensive evaluation is needed.

## 18. Comprehensive assessment

May integrate:

- direct interview;
- psychiatric symptoms;
- past behavior;
- current stressors;
- physical health;
- substance use;
- supports;
- access to lethal means;
- clinical history;
- safeguarding.

## 19. Risk formulation

NICE recommends risk formulation as part of psychosocial assessment rather than global numerical/category prediction.

Formulation asks:

- what is happening now;
- what is changing;
- what vulnerabilities/resources matter;
- what support/safety response is needed.

## 20. Disposition

Disposition is an action decision:

- emergency evaluation;
- urgent follow-up;
- routine follow-up;
- no additional intervention at present.

It is not a pure risk score.

# IV. Risk tools do not produce reliable future-suicide categories

## 21. NICE NG225

Current NICE self-harm guidance states:

- do not use risk scales to predict future suicide or repetition of self-harm;
- do not use them to decide who receives treatment or discharge;
- do not use global low/medium/high stratification for those purposes.

## 22. The alternative is needs/safety-focused assessment

NICE directs assessment toward:

- immediate safety;
- long-term safety;
- needs;
- risk formulation.

## 23. VA/DoD 2024 adds nuance

The VA/DoD guideline:

- supports use of validated screening measures for identifying possible suicide-related risk;
- recommends broader assessment domains;
- states there is insufficient evidence to recommend one specific risk-stratification tool/method.

## 24. Therefore

`validated screen` can be useful.

But:

`screen -> exact risk tier -> future outcome`

is not justified.

## 25. Screening programs and prediction are also different questions

A screening program can help identify people needing evaluation even if screening alone has not been proven to reduce attempts/deaths.

---

# V. Negative screen does not prove safety

## 26. NIMH explicitly preserves clinical override

The current ASQ toolkit notes that clinical judgment can override a negative screen.

## 27. Reasons a screen can miss risk

Possible reasons:

- non-disclosure;
- misunderstanding;
- rapid state change;
- item coverage limits;
- setting/privacy;
- fear of consequences;
- communication/language barriers.

## 28. Therefore

`negative screen -> zero concern`

is invalid.

## 29. Positive screen also does not equal one disposition

NIMH distinguishes:

- acute positive;
- non-acute positive.

The latter requires further assessment rather than automatic emergency disposition.

---

# VI. Direct asking about suicide is appropriate

## 30. WHO 2026

WHO states that asking someone whether they are thinking about suicide does not make them act on those feelings and may reduce anxiety/help the person feel understood.

## 31. Direct questions reduce inference error

If the safety question is material, do not rely only on:

- tone;
- metaphor;
- personality theory;
- indirect linguistic cues.

## 32. Direct disclosure outranks stereotype

A direct statement of:

- current suicidal thoughts;
- intent;
- plan;
- recent attempt

is stronger safety evidence than a generic demographic/profile inference.

## 33. No-disclosure does not prove absence

Direct questions improve evidence.

They do not create perfect verification.

---

# VII. Self-harm and suicide intent are not interchangeable

## 34. NICE definition

NICE defines self-harm as intentional self-poisoning or injury irrespective of apparent purpose.

Thus self-harm is broader than suicidal behavior.

## 35. Same behavior can have different functions

Possible functions include:

- affect regulation;
- communication;
- self-punishment;
- dissociation management;
- suicidal intent.

Function must be assessed.

## 36. Suicide attempt requires intent-to-die assessment

Physical severity alone does not perfectly reveal intent.

## 37. Low apparent lethality does not mean no distress

Psychological/safety assessment remains relevant.

## 38. High medical lethality requires medical response regardless of inferred motive

Medical stabilization and psychological assessment are parallel priorities.

---

# VIII. Intrusive thoughts are not automatically intent

## 39. CLN-03 boundary

The existing psychopathology architecture already established:

`intrusive thought != intent`

and:

`obsession != delusion`.

## 40. Harm-related intrusive thoughts can be ego-dystonic

They may be:

- unwanted;
- frightening;
- resisted.

## 41. Intent requires separate evidence

Assess:

- desire;
- intention;
- planning;
- behavior;
- capability/access;
- temporal immediacy.

## 42. Safety uncertainty still matters

If phenomenology cannot be clarified or behavior suggests danger, escalate rather than assume benignity.

---

# IX. Current suicidal thoughts are a major acute signal

## 43. NIMH ASQ pathway

For adults in its medical-setting pathway:

- current suicidal thoughts after a positive screen are treated as acute/imminent concern requiring urgent/STAT mental-health evaluation.

## 44. This is a pathway rule, not a universal mathematical definition

Different health systems may operationalize acute risk differently.

The general transferable principle is:

**current active suicidal thinking can materially elevate immediacy and warrants direct safety evaluation.**

## 45. Current thoughts should not be assessed alone

NIMH's brief assessment also includes:

- frequency/recency;
- plan;
- access to means;
- past behavior;
- symptoms;
- substance use;
- stressors;
- supports.

---

# X. Plan and access matter

## 46. Plan is not binary

A vague idea and a detailed feasible plan carry different implications.

## 47. Access matters

A plan with immediate access to a highly lethal method raises concern.

## 48. Access alone is not intent

Many people have access to potentially dangerous means without suicidal intent.

## 49. Intent, plan, access, and capability should be separated

Do not compress them into one variable.

---

# XI. Past behavior matters but does not determine the present

## 50. Prior attempt is an important risk factor

WHO and NIMH both identify past suicidal behavior as highly relevant.

## 51. Past attempt != current imminent risk

Time, current state, treatment, environment, and current intent matter.

## 52. Recent escalation is particularly important

Changes in:

- frequency;
- intent;
- planning;
- distress;
- self-harm

can increase referral priority.

NICE explicitly prioritizes referral when self-harm or suicidal intent is increasing.

---

# XII. Emergency, urgent, and routine are action tiers

## 53. Emergency

Use when delay can create unacceptable immediate danger.

Examples include:

- current suicidal intent/thoughts with inability to maintain safety;
- recent serious self-harm/poisoning requiring medical care;
- severe acute behavioral/mental-state disturbance with immediate danger;
- medical instability;
- severe confusion suggestive of delirium;
- violence/threat where immediate safety cannot be maintained.

## 54. Urgent

Use when:

- danger is not clearly immediate;
- but deterioration, increasing intent/self-harm, suspected mania/severe depression, emerging psychosis, or other serious instability requires rapid specialist assessment.

## 55. Routine/non-urgent

Use when:

- no immediate safety concern is identified;
- person can maintain safety;
- symptoms/needs still merit planned mental-health follow-up.

## 56. These tiers are not fixed numerical risk categories

They are **service/action thresholds**.

That distinction is crucial.

## 57. Context changes thresholds

Lower tolerance for delay may be appropriate when:

- information is incomplete;
- person cannot reliably engage;
- supports are absent;
- medical condition is unclear;
- access to emergency care is difficult.

---

# XIII. NIMH adult outpatient disposition illustrates tiering

## 58. Emergency psychiatric evaluation

NIMH recommends emergency evaluation when imminent risk/current suicidal thoughts are identified in its pathway.

## 59. Further evaluation

When more risk evaluation is needed, NIMH recommends expedited mental-health referral, preferably within 72 hours in the outpatient guide.

## 60. Non-urgent follow-up

Some positive screens may ultimately warrant routine follow-up rather than emergency care.

## 61. No further intervention

A structured assessment can conclude no additional intervention is required at that time.

## 62. Important boundary

These are NIMH medical-setting pathways.

They should not be copied mechanically into every country/service/AI interaction.

---

# XIV. Safety planning is not a no-suicide contract

## 63. Safety plan

NICE defines a safety plan as a written, prioritized list of:

- warning signs;
- coping strategies;
- support people;
- professional contacts;
- steps to reduce access to self-harm methods.

## 64. NIMH explicitly distinguishes safety planning from safety contracts

NIMH states that asking a person to "contract for safety" is not effective and can create false reassurance.

## 65. Therefore

`promised not to self-harm -> safe`

is invalid.

## 66. Safety planning is collaborative

It should improve:

- recognition;
- coping;
- connection;
- access to help;
- means safety.

## 67. Evidence nuance

VA/DoD 2024 concluded evidence was insufficient to recommend for or against safety planning/crisis response plans specifically for reducing suicide attempts in certain defined risk groups.

## 68. Operational use and outcome evidence are different

A practice can be used as a care/safety organization tool while evidence for reducing a particular hard endpoint remains uncertain.

This distinction prevents evidence inflation.

---

# XV. Means safety

## 69. General principle

Increasing time/distance between suicidal crisis and highly lethal means can reduce opportunity for impulsive fatal action.

## 70. NIMH includes means restriction in safety planning

The practical principle is:

- secure/remove dangerous means where feasible;
- involve appropriate support when safe and lawful.

## 71. Means safety is not punishment

It should be:

- collaborative where possible;
- proportionate;
- safety-focused.

## 72. Do not infer safety merely from one inaccessible method

Suicidal intent and overall environment still require assessment.

---

# XVI. Physical and psychological safety must run in parallel

## 73. NICE self-harm guidance

After self-harm:

- physical healthcare;
- psychosocial assessment

should be carried out concurrently as soon as possible.

## 74. Medical severity cannot be delayed for psychological formulation

Examples:

- self-poisoning;
- significant injury;
- altered consciousness;
- physiological instability.

## 75. Psychological safety cannot be ignored after physical stabilization

A medically minor injury can still indicate serious psychological distress.

## 76. Parallel-care rule

`physical urgency` and `mental-health urgency` are separate axes.

---

# XVII. Acute confusion/delirium is a medical red flag

## 77. NICE delirium guidance

Recent changes over hours/days in:

- concentration;
- response speed;
- confusion;
- perception;
- mobility;
- agitation;
- sleep;
- communication

can indicate delirium and require assessment.

## 78. Delirium is not a primary psychological explanation

Potential causes include medical illness, medication, intoxication/withdrawal, metabolic/neurological factors.

## 79. Hypoactive delirium can be missed

Withdrawal, slow response, reduced mobility, low engagement can look like:

- depression;
- fatigue;
- disengagement.

## 80. Therefore

`new acute confusion -> psychological interpretation first`

is unsafe.

---

# XVIII. Mania and severe depression require urgent specialist assessment

## 81. NICE bipolar guidance

NICE recommends urgent specialist mental-health assessment when:

- mania is suspected;
- severe depression is suspected;
- person is a danger to self or others.

## 82. Mania is not merely high energy

The safety issue can involve:

- severe behavioral dysregulation;
- impaired judgment;
- psychosis;
- reduced capacity to manage risk.

## 83. Do not use a questionnaire as diagnosis

NICE also advises against questionnaire-based identification of bipolar disorder in adult primary care.

## 84. Safety action can precede diagnostic certainty

You do not need complete bipolar classification before urgent specialist evaluation when acute severity/danger is present.

---

# XIX. Psychosis requires prompt assessment, not automatic dangerousness assumptions

## 85. Psychosis is a symptom domain

CLN-03 already established:

`psychosis != schizophrenia`.

## 86. NICE recommends referral without delay for possible psychosis with distress/functional decline

Early specialist assessment matters.

## 87. Acute psychosis becomes a crisis issue when severity/risk exceeds routine service capacity

NICE crisis guidance uses severity and risk to self/others as service-level escalation factors.

## 88. Psychosis does not equal violence

Do not stigmatize or infer dangerousness from diagnosis/symptom label alone.

## 89. Safety-relevant features require direct evidence

Examples:

- dangerous commands/behavior;
- inability to care for basic safety;
- severe disorganization;
- immediate risk behavior;
- substance/medical cause.

---

# XX. Substance intoxication and withdrawal can change urgency

## 90. Substance effects can alter risk assessment reliability

Intoxication can affect:

- impulse control;
- cognition;
- reliability of report;
- behavior.

## 91. Withdrawal can be medically dangerous

NICE recommends hospital admission for acute alcohol withdrawal with:

- withdrawal seizures;
- delirium tremens;
- high risk of developing those complications.

## 92. Severe withdrawal is not a psychotherapy problem

It requires medical management.

## 93. Substance-induced psychiatric symptoms require temporal/exposure assessment

CLN-03 already established:

- intoxication/withdrawal-linked;
- medication/substance-induced;
- independent psychiatric disorder

must be separated.

## 94. Safety first, etiology second when delay is dangerous

A full etiological formulation can follow stabilization.

---

# XXI. Violence, coercion, and risk to others

## 95. REL-02 boundary remains controlling

Coercive control, threats, stalking, sexual coercion, and violence cannot be reduced to a symmetric communication problem.

## 96. Direct threat/behavior outranks attachment interpretation

Do not explain a credible safety threat away as:

- anxious attachment;
- avoidant attachment;
- conflict style.

## 97. Risk to others requires its own evidence

Do not infer violence risk from:

- diagnosis;
- psychosis alone;
- anger alone;
- personality stereotype.

## 98. Immediate danger requires emergency/safeguarding response

Exact legal route depends on jurisdiction.

---

# XXII. Safeguarding

## 99. Safeguarding concern is not identical to psychiatric crisis

Potential concerns can involve:

- domestic abuse;
- exploitation;
- child/vulnerable-person safety;
- neglect;
- coercion.

## 100. NICE self-harm guidance explicitly requires safeguarding consideration

Assessment should ask whether safeguarding concerns are present.

## 101. Consent/confidentiality can have limits

When serious danger or safeguarding duties apply, information-sharing rules can change.

## 102. Legal specifics are jurisdiction dependent

Do not invent:

- mandatory reporting rules;
- detention authority;
- confidentiality exceptions.

ETH will own detailed ethical/legal reasoning.

---

# XXIII. Remote/telehealth safety has structural limits

## 103. Remote assessment cannot verify everything

Limitations can include:

- uncertain physical location;
- limited view of environment;
- inability to perform physical exam;
- inability to directly secure dangerous means;
- disconnection;
- uncertain identity;
- limited local-service knowledge.

## 104. HHS telebehavioral-health guidance requires an emergency plan

Before/for remote care it recommends documenting:

- current patient location/address;
- local emergency services;
- local emergency contact/support;
- other clinician contacts;
- what to do if connection fails during crisis.

## 105. Location is operational safety information

In an actual clinical telehealth service, knowing where the person is can determine whether emergency help can reach them.

## 106. This does not mean all remote conversations should collect location

Privacy/proportionality matter.

For general AI conversation, do not collect unnecessary sensitive location data.

## 107. Remote uncertainty should lower confidence

If immediate risk cannot be ruled out remotely, do not manufacture reassurance.

---

# XXIV. AI-assisted safety inference has stricter limits

## 108. AI conversation is not a formal clinical risk assessment

AI cannot reliably provide:

- physical examination;
- verified identity;
- verified environment;
- direct emergency intervention;
- legal capacity determination;
- full collateral information.

## 109. Text pattern recognition is not suicide prediction

Language can be informative.

But:

`wording/tone -> exact acute risk`

is not validated as a stand-alone inference.

## 110. AI should fail safely

When evidence suggests immediate danger:

- prioritize connection to real-world emergency/crisis support;
- avoid prolonged theory;
- do not offer false certainty.

## 111. AI should also avoid false escalation from weak pattern matching

A metaphor, intrusive thought, or distress statement is not automatically suicidal intent.

Direct clarification matters.

---

# XXV. Distress level and risk are related but not identical

## 112. Severe distress can occur without suicidal intent

## 113. Suicidal intent can sometimes be present without dramatic visible distress

Therefore:

`looks calm -> safe`

and:

`looks distressed -> imminent suicide`

are both invalid.

## 114. NIMH/NICE pathways include distress as one part of assessment

Not the sole determinant.

---

# XXVI. Protective factors are not immunity

## 115. Reasons for living/support matter

They can reduce concern or guide safety planning.

## 116. Protective factor != guarantee

A person can have:

- family;
- employment;
- treatment;
- future plans

and still be at risk.

## 117. Avoid arithmetic scoring

Do not subtract:

`2 protective factors - 3 risks = medium risk`.

Safety formulation is not a point system.

---

# XXVII. Current change matters more than static profile in acute triage

## 118. Static variables

Examples:

- diagnosis history;
- demographic factor;
- prior attempt.

## 119. Dynamic variables

Examples:

- current suicidal thoughts;
- recent escalation;
- acute intoxication;
- new psychosis/mania;
- acute loss/stressor;
- access change;
- support breakdown.

## 120. Acute triage prioritizes dynamic/current evidence

This does not erase historical risk.

It changes immediacy.

---

# XXVIII. Referral priority can be triggered by assessor uncertainty

## 121. NICE explicitly includes clinician concern

Priority referral is appropriate when the assessor is concerned, alongside rising/high distress or increasing self-harm/intent.

## 122. This is not license for arbitrary fear

Concern should be grounded in:

- observed change;
- incomplete safety information;
- concerning behavior;
- inability to establish safety.

## 123. Uncertainty can itself matter when consequences of delay are severe

DEC threshold reasoning applies:

- false reassurance cost can be high;
- false-positive escalation also has costs.

The goal is proportionate safety, not maximal intervention.

---

# XXIX. Avoid binary safe/unsafe language

## 124. "Safe" is often too strong

Prefer:

- no immediate safety concern identified;
- current evidence does not indicate emergency escalation;
- further assessment is needed;
- uncertainty remains.

## 125. Safety status is time-bound

A reasonable assessment now does not guarantee future safety.

## 126. Reassessment is appropriate after material change

Examples:

- new disclosure;
- worsening intent;
- intoxication;
- major stressor;
- new psychosis/mania;
- failed support plan.

# XXX. Documentation logic

## 127. Record evidence, not just label

Useful documentation structure:

1. direct statements;
2. observed behavior/state;
3. recent behavior/history;
4. plan/access;
5. medical/psychiatric concerns;
6. supports;
7. uncertainty;
8. disposition/rationale.

## 128. Avoid unsupported "low risk"

A label without formulation can create false reassurance.

## 129. Distinguish unknown from absent

`not assessed`

is not:

`no`.

## 130. Distinguish denied from verified absent

`denies current intent`

is not:

`intent impossible`.

NIMH and NICE both include safety planning in care pathways.

## 133. Therefore

Allowed:

`safety planning is a recommended/used clinical safety process in several current pathways`.

Not allowed:

`safety plan proven to prevent suicide in every patient`.

- underlying disorder;
- chronic risk;
- social stressor;

Care transition is a separate safety problem.

## 138. AI responses should use current local resources when available

But location should not be inferred or exposed unnecessarily.

# XXXVII. Evidence-strength map

## High-confidence practical boundaries

1. self-harm and suicidal intent are not interchangeable;
2. direct questioning about suicide is appropriate and does not cause suicidal behavior;
3. screening is not prediction;
4. negative screening does not prove safety;
5. positive screening requires acuity/assessment rather than one automatic disposition;
6. global low/medium/high risk categories should not be used to predict future suicide or determine discharge under NICE NG225;
7. current suicidal thoughts are a major acute signal in NIMH ASQ pathways;
8. plan feasibility/access and past behavior add important information;
9. no single risk factor determines current disposition;
10. physical and psychosocial care should proceed concurrently after self-harm;
11. acute confusion/delirium is a medical-assessment problem;
12. suspected mania/severe depression warrants urgent specialist mental-health assessment under NICE guidance;
13. possible psychosis with distress/functional decline warrants specialist referral without delay;
14. severe alcohol withdrawal with seizure/delirium risk requires hospital-level medical care;
15. safety contracts should not be treated as protection;
16. safety planning is distinct from a safety contract;
17. remote crisis care requires emergency-planning infrastructure;
18. AI conversation is not a formal clinical risk assessment.

## Supported but context-sensitive

1. validated suicide-risk screening tools;
2. structured brief safety assessment;
3. safety planning;
4. means-safety counseling;
5. post-screen follow-up;
6. telebehavioral emergency planning;
7. risk formulation.

## Not established as universal

1. exact individual suicide probability from a scale;
2. one universal acute-risk classification system;
3. one global low/medium/high threshold;
4. one screening tool for every population/setting;
5. hospitalization for every positive screen;
6. safety planning as a guaranteed suicide-prevention intervention;
7. psychosis/mania diagnosis as automatic dangerousness;
8. remote assessment as equivalent to in-person assessment;
9. AI as substitute for crisis/emergency services;
10. one jurisdiction's emergency/legal rules as universal.

# XL. Canonical working rule

**In safety work, the required action threshold can be clearer than the exact probability of harm. Escalate based on current direct evidence, immediacy, medical/psychiatric instability, environment, and uncertainty — not on a pseudo-precise risk score.**

Use:

`direct evidence -> current state -> intent/plan/access -> recent behavior/change -> medical/psychiatric red flags -> supports/environment -> remote limitations -> emergency/urgent/routine action -> uncertainty/follow-up`.

Never:

`screen -> prediction`

`negative screen -> safe`

`self-harm -> suicide attempt`

`intrusion -> intent`

`protective factor -> immunity`

`psychosis -> dangerous`

`acute confusion -> psychological explanation`

`safety contract -> safety`

`remote chat -> formal assessment`

`AI -> emergency service`.

## Source-boundary note

NICE NG225 is used for self-harm assessment, risk-tool limits, risk formulation, parallel physical/psychosocial care, and referral priority. The current NICE PDF was opened and relevant pages were visually inspected in addition to extracted text.

NIMH ASQ/BSSA is used as a **medical-setting screening and disposition pathway**, not as a universal risk algorithm and not as an AI assessment protocol.

WHO 2026 is used for the public-health boundary that asking directly about suicide does not cause suicidal behavior and for general immediate-danger guidance.

The VA/DoD 2024 CPG is used to show current uncertainty around specific suicide-risk stratification methods and hard-outcome evidence for safety-planning/crisis-response interventions. The full guideline PDF was opened and the recommendations table was visually inspected.

HHS telebehavioral guidance is U.S.-specific operational guidance; only the generic remote-emergency-planning principles are transported.

NICE bipolar/psychosis/delirium/alcohol-withdrawal guidance is used only to establish escalation/red-flag boundaries, not to provide diagnosis or treatment instructions.

No source is used to infer any individual's suicide risk, self-harm intent, dangerousness, emergency status, diagnosis, legal capacity, or need for involuntary treatment.
