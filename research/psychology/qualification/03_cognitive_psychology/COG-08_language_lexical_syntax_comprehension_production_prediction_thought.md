# COG-08 — Language: lexical, semantic, syntactic, comprehension/production, prediction, and language–thought boundaries

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0**

## Purpose

Establish a current working architecture of language processing that is precise enough for psychological inference without collapsing language into:

- thought;
- semantic cognition;
- social communication;
- working memory;
- cognitive control;
- speech motor control;
- reading/writing;
- prediction.

The block separates:

- linguistic form from meaning;
- lexical access from semantic knowledge;
- morphology from syntax;
- syntax from semantics;
- sentence meaning from pragmatic inference;
- comprehension from production;
- linguistic prediction from comprehension itself;
- core linguistic processing from perception/motor systems;
- language from general reasoning and conceptual thought;
- language-specific control from domain-general control;
- monolingual assumptions from multilingual reality;
- task performance from a pure measure of language ability.

## Core rule

**Language is a structured symbolic system whose comprehension and production require mapping between linguistic forms and meanings, combining elements into larger structures, and integrating those structures with context and knowledge. Language interacts strongly with perception, semantics, memory, control, social cognition, and reasoning, but is not reducible to any one of them.**

A generic comprehension pipeline is:

`signal / text / sign`
-> `perceptual form analysis`
-> `word/morpheme identification`
-> `lexical + morphosyntactic access`
-> `combinatorial structure`
-> `sentence/discourse interpretation`
↔ `semantic knowledge + context + prediction + pragmatics`

A generic production pipeline is:

`communicative / conceptual intention`
-> `message planning`
-> `lexical selection`
-> `morphosyntactic / constituent planning`
-> `phonological / orthographic / sign-form encoding`
-> `motor output`
↔ `monitoring / control / repair`

These are analytical stages, not rigidly serial boxes.

## 1. Current broad definition

NIMH RDoC currently defines language as:

> a system of shared symbolic representations of the world, the self, and abstract concepts that supports thought and communication.

For cognitive analysis, this broad definition is useful but insufficiently granular.

Language processing additionally requires mechanisms for:

- form recognition;
- lexical access;
- morphosyntax;
- combinatorial interpretation;
- production planning;
- mapping between form and meaning.

## 2. Language is not one process

"Language ability" can refer to partially separable functions such as:

- speech perception;
- spoken-word recognition;
- lexical retrieval;
- morphology;
- syntax;
- semantic composition;
- discourse integration;
- pragmatic interpretation;
- sentence production;
- speech planning;
- reading;
- writing;
- sign-language comprehension/production.

A failure in one domain cannot automatically be generalized to all language.

## 3. Form and meaning

Language links symbolic forms to meaning.

Forms can be:

- phonological;
- orthographic;
- signed/gestural;
- morphological;
- syntactic.

Meanings can be expressed at multiple scales:

- morpheme;
- word;
- phrase;
- sentence;
- discourse.

Canonical rule:

**linguistic form != meaning, although language processing links them.**

## 4. Lexical representation

A lexical representation can include information about:

- word form;
- pronunciation;
- spelling;
- morphological structure;
- syntactic distribution;
- mappings to meaning.

The lexicon should not be treated as a simple dictionary of independent word entries.

Current spoken-word recognition research emphasizes:

- competition among candidate forms;
- adaptation/plasticity;
- influence of linguistic experience;
- multilingual experience;
- time-varying lexical processing.

## 5. Spoken-word recognition is not pure hearing

Spoken-word recognition lies between:

- auditory perception;
- lexical knowledge;
- sentence/context processing.

It requires mapping temporally unfolding acoustic information onto candidate word forms.

Performance can depend on:

- hearing quality;
- accent;
- speaker variability;
- phonological expectations;
- lexical frequency;
- neighborhood competition;
- language experience;
- context.

Therefore:

`failed spoken-word recognition -> lexical deficit`

is unsafe without perceptual alternatives being tested.

## 6. Word recognition is plastic

A 2024 Annual Review emphasizes that spoken-word recognition changes across:

- development;
- aging;
- training;
- sensory changes;
- multilingual experience.

Therefore the "ideal monolingual adult lexicon" should not be treated as the universal baseline for human word recognition.

Canonical rule:

**lexical processing reflects learning history and current sensory/cognitive context.**

## 7. Lexical access is not semantic knowledge itself

A person can know a concept while failing to retrieve its word form.

Conversely, a word form can be produced with incomplete semantic access.

This preserves the COG-06 distinction:

**conceptual knowledge != lexical retrieval.**

Naming failures must be localized across:

- conceptual access;
- lexical selection;
- phonological encoding;
- articulation.

## 8. Lexical competition

When producing or recognizing a word, related candidate forms can become activated.

Competition may reflect:

- phonological overlap;
- semantic relatedness;
- orthographic similarity;
- language membership in multilingual speakers.

But behavioral interference/facilitation does not uniquely identify the architecture of lexical selection.

Different models explain competition through:

- activation;
- selection thresholds;
- inhibition;
- learning/adaptation;
- decision processes.

## 9. Cross-modal lexical codes interact

A 2025 systematic/meta-analytic review found robust:

- orthographic influences on spoken-word recognition and production;
- phonological influences on written-word recognition and production.

Thus spoken and written language systems are not fully isolated.

This does not mean orthography and phonology are the same representation.

Canonical rule:

**cross-modal activation != representational identity.**

## 10. Morphology

Morphology concerns structured relations within words, including:

- roots/stems;
- inflection;
- derivation;
- agreement-relevant features.

Morphological processing can interact with:

- lexical frequency;
- semantics;
- phonology;
- syntax;
- language-specific structure.

Do not assume every morphologically complex word is processed by one universal decomposition mechanism.

## 11. Syntax

Syntax concerns structured relations among words/constituents that constrain how linguistic elements combine.

Syntactic knowledge supports:

- dependency relations;
- argument structure;
- word-order relations;
- agreement;
- constituency;
- hierarchical interpretation.

Canonical boundary:

**syntax != word sequence alone.**

Linear order carries information, but syntactic structure can encode relations that are not reducible to adjacency.

## 12. Syntax and semantics interact

Sentence interpretation depends jointly on:

- syntactic structure;
- lexical semantics;
- world knowledge;
- context.

A syntactically well-formed sentence can be semantically implausible.

A semantically plausible interpretation can conflict with a rare or difficult syntactic structure.

Current work shows that comprehension depends on priors over both:

- meaning;
- structure.

Therefore:

`semantic plausibility = syntactic parse`

is false.

## 13. Structure is not settled as one neural/computational mechanism

Current neuroscience contains active disagreement over:

- how specialized the core language network is;
- how topographically homogeneous it is;
- how widely linguistic information is distributed;
- which regions support syntax versus broader language functions.

Fedorenko et al. (2024) argue for a strongly interconnected, language-selective core network.

Published 2024–2025 correspondence challenges parts of this account, especially claims of monolithic localization or limited extra-network linguistic sensitivity.

Canonical rule:

**functional language selectivity is well supported; the strongest claims about one fixed, monolithic neural language architecture remain contested.**

## 14. Core language network and interfaces

Fedorenko et al. (2024) provide strong evidence that a core frontotemporal language network is:

- strongly interconnected;
- responsive across input/output modalities;
- language-selective relative to many nonlinguistic tasks;
- distinct from lower-level perceptual/motor systems;
- distinct from broader knowledge/reasoning systems.

This supports a useful cognitive boundary:

**core linguistic computation != speech perception/motor execution != general reasoning.**

Detailed neural implementation is deferred to NEU.

## 15. Modality independence does not mean modality irrelevance

Language can be:

- spoken;
- written;
- signed.

Core linguistic computations can generalize across modalities.

But each modality also recruits specialized systems:

- audition for speech;
- vision for reading/sign;
- motor speech systems for speaking;
- hand/body motor systems for sign;
- orthographic/motor systems for writing/typing.

Therefore:

`language network modality-independent -> language performance modality-independent`

is false.

## 16. Comprehension

Comprehension transforms an incoming linguistic signal into an interpreted message.

It can involve:

- perceptual decoding;
- lexical recognition;
- morphological analysis;
- syntactic composition;
- semantic integration;
- discourse context;
- pragmatic inference;
- world knowledge.

Failure at a late interpretive stage does not imply failed lexical recognition.

## 17. Incremental processing

Language comprehension typically unfolds incrementally.

Listeners/readers begin interpreting before an utterance or sentence is complete.

Incrementality allows rapid comprehension but creates temporary ambiguity.

Interpretations can be:

- revised;
- reweighted;
- abandoned

as new information arrives.

Canonical rule:

**early interpretation != final representation.**

## 18. Ambiguity resolution

Language is ambiguous at multiple levels:

- phonological;
- lexical;
- syntactic;
- semantic;
- referential;
- pragmatic.

Resolution can use:

- frequency;
- context;
- structure;
- world knowledge;
- speaker information;
- prosody;
- visual context.

A successful final interpretation does not reveal which cue dominated without targeted evidence.

## 19. Prediction in comprehension

Comprehenders can anticipate upcoming information.

Prediction can concern:

- semantic features;
- lexical items;
- phonology;
- morphology;
- syntax/constituent structure;
- referents;
- discourse events.

Prediction can increase processing efficiency under suitable conditions.

However, 2026 review evidence emphasizes that prediction:

- is not one unitary mechanism;
- varies across individuals;
- varies across development;
- depends on experience/resources;
- is not obligatorily used in every comprehension situation.

Canonical rule:

**prediction is one adaptive comprehension strategy/process family, not the definition of comprehension itself.**

## 20. Prediction versus integration

A reduced response to expected language can reflect:

- preactivation/prediction;
- easier post-input integration;
- both.

This distinction remains active in psycholinguistics.

Even measures such as:

- N400;
- reading time;
- eye movements;
- surprisal effects

do not automatically isolate prediction from integration.

## 21. Surprisal is a model of predictability, not the whole comprehension process

Language-model surprisal quantifies how unexpected an input is relative to a probabilistic model.

Human processing often correlates with surprisal.

But:

`LLM surprisal predicts reading time -> humans comprehend by the same mechanism as the LLM`

does not follow.

A 2026 Nature Neuroscience study shows word-level predictive responses constrained by constituent structure, illustrating that prediction interacts with linguistic structure rather than simply minimizing next-word uncertainty uniformly.

## 22. Language models are comparison models, not human-language identities

LLMs can be useful for:

- estimating contextual predictability;
- generating representational hypotheses;
- comparing sentence-processing models.

But human language differs in:

- embodiment;
- social interaction;
- multimodal grounding;
- learning history;
- real-time biological constraints;
- communicative goals.

Model fit requires psychological interpretation.

## 23. Production begins before articulation

Language production can require:

1. conceptual/message planning;
2. lexical selection;
3. morphosyntactic structuring;
4. phonological/orthographic/sign-form planning;
5. motor execution.

A speech error can originate at different stages.

Canonical rule:

**speech output error != linguistic knowledge deficit by default.**

## 24. Word production is monitored and controlled

Nozari's 2025 perspective integrates language production with broader action-control frameworks.

Speakers can:

- monitor emerging output;
- detect conflict/error;
- adjust selection;
- repair output.

Monitoring can occur before and after articulation.

Therefore self-correction is evidence of monitoring/control, not automatically evidence that initial lexical knowledge was absent.

## 25. Production is adaptive

Lexical production history can change future accessibility/competition.

Recent work supports adaptive lexical-processing effects after use.

Thus:

`word retrieval performance = fixed lexical property`

is too static.

Production history matters.

## 26. Sentence production

Sentence production requires coordinating:

- message structure;
- lexical choices;
- grammatical roles;
- constituent structure;
- morphology;
- word order;
- articulation.

The sentence-production literature is less extensive than comprehension literature in several areas, and 2025 review/meta-analysis work explicitly notes gaps.

Do not treat comprehension architectures as automatically identical to production architectures.

## 27. Macroplanning

Longer utterances/discourse require deciding:

- what information to mention;
- order of ideas;
- referential structure;
- discourse coherence.

Macroplanning is not reducible to lexical retrieval or syntax.

It interfaces with:

- memory;
- executive control;
- social/pragmatic goals.

## 28. Comprehension and production are distinct but coupled

Traditional psycholinguistics often studies them separately.

Current conversation research emphasizes tight coupling in actual interaction.

During conversation, a participant can simultaneously:

- comprehend the partner;
- plan a response;
- monitor turn timing;
- track common ground;
- produce speech/gesture.

Therefore isolated laboratory comprehension/production tasks sample only part of natural language use.

## 29. Conversation is joint action

A 2026 Nature Reviews Psychology review emphasizes language use in face-to-face interaction as situated joint action.

Conversation involves:

- turn coordination;
- multimodal signals;
- recipient design;
- shared/common ground;
- adaptation.

This introduces social-cognitive processes beyond core linguistic decoding/encoding.

Canonical rule:

**language processing != social communication in full.**

## 30. Pragmatics

Pragmatics concerns how interpreted meaning depends on:

- speaker intention;
- context;
- common ground;
- implicature;
- presupposition;
- discourse goals;
- social norms.

Literal sentence meaning can differ from intended communicative meaning.

Pragmatic inference therefore recruits:

- language;
- semantic knowledge;
- reasoning;
- social cognition.

It should not be collapsed into syntax or lexical semantics.

## 31. Common ground is dynamic

Common ground refers broadly to information participants treat as mutually available/relevant in communication.

Current formal-pragmatics/psycholinguistic work emphasizes that common-ground use is:

- context-dependent;
- graded;
- cognitively constrained;
- interaction-sensitive.

Do not assume that speakers perfectly model everything their partner knows.

## 32. Language and thought are distinguishable

The strongest current neurocognitive evidence supports substantial functional dissociation between:

- core language processing;
- general reasoning;
- mathematics;
- other knowledge systems.

People with severe language impairment can retain substantial nonlinguistic reasoning.

Conversely, broader cognitive deficits can coexist with relatively preserved language.

Canonical rule:

**language is not identical to thought.**

## 33. "Language is for communication, not thought" is a perspective, not settled final fact

Fedorenko, Piantadosi, and Gibson (2024) argue that language is primarily optimized for communication rather than thought.

The evidence for **dissociation between core language and many forms of thought** is strong and highly relevant.

The stronger evolutionary/functional claim that language's primary function is communication remains a theoretical position.

Therefore this qualification adopts the narrower conclusion:

**language can shape and support thought, but general thought/reasoning is not constituted by the core language system.**

## 34. Language can influence cognition without determining it

Language can influence:

- memory strategies;
- categorization;
- attention;
- numerical representation;
- social framing;
- abstraction.

But evidence for language effects does not establish strong linguistic determinism.

Canonical boundary:

**language influences some cognitive representations/processes; language does not rigidly determine all possible thought.**

## 35. Inner speech is not equivalent to thought

People can use inner speech for:

- rehearsal;
- planning;
- self-regulation;
- problem solving.

But thought also occurs through:

- visual/spatial representation;
- affective representation;
- motor imagery;
- relational models;
- nonverbal concepts.

Therefore:

`no inner speech -> no thought`

is invalid.

Detailed inner speech/self-regulation belongs partly in EXE/MET.

## 36. Language and semantic cognition are distinct but interdependent

Language maps forms to meanings.

Semantic cognition represents/use conceptual knowledge.

A word can activate a concept.

A concept can be accessed through:

- pictures;
- sounds;
- actions;
- nonverbal cues

without linguistic form.

Thus COG-06 remains necessary as a separate domain.

## 37. Language and working memory

Language tasks can impose working-memory demands through:

- maintaining unresolved dependencies;
- tracking referents;
- holding partial parses;
- planning utterances;
- following long instructions.

But language competence and general working-memory capacity are not identical.

A complex sentence can fail because of:

- linguistic structure;
- working memory;
- attention;
- knowledge;
- task design.

## 38. Language and cognitive control

Control can be needed for:

- ambiguity resolution;
- conflict monitoring;
- word selection;
- error repair;
- switching languages;
- suppressing irrelevant alternatives.

But the existence of control demands does not make language a domain-general control task.

Core linguistic computation and control remain distinct interacting processes.

## 39. Multilingualism is not an exception case

A large proportion of humans use more than one language.

Multilingual processing can involve:

- cross-language activation;
- shared/distributed conceptual representations;
- lexical competition;
- language selection;
- code switching;
- transfer from prior language experience.

Therefore monolingual performance should not be treated as the universal human norm.

## 40. Bilingual language control

Current review work distinguishes language-specific control from domain-general cognitive control while acknowledging overlap and open questions.

Bilingual production can require:

- selecting target-language representations;
- managing competition;
- proactive control;
- reactive control.

But:

`bilingual language switching = generic executive-function test`

is too strong.

## 41. Bilingualism is heterogeneous

"Bilingual" does not define one population.

Relevant dimensions include:

- age of acquisition;
- proficiency;
- dominance;
- literacy;
- frequency of use;
- switching ecology;
- language similarity;
- immersion;
- social context.

Group labels that ignore these variables can hide meaningful effects.

## 42. Do not assume a universal bilingual cognitive advantage

The literature on broad executive advantages from bilingualism remains controversial and heterogeneous.

Language-control demands are real.

They do not automatically imply a global domain-general executive advantage for every bilingual person.

Canonical rule:

**bilingual language control != universal cognitive advantage.**

## 43. L1/L2 processing is shaped by learning history

Current 2025 review work argues that apparent online cross-language activation can sometimes partly reflect long-term transfer from L1 during L2 learning.

Therefore bilingual processing effects should distinguish:

- current activation/control;
- long-term learned representation;
- proficiency;
- acquisition history.

## 44. Reading is not spoken language on paper

Reading adds:

- orthographic decoding;
- visual word recognition;
- learned mappings between writing and language.

Writing/typing adds:

- orthographic retrieval;
- motor sequencing;
- spelling.

Literacy is culturally learned and can reshape language processing.

Therefore spoken, written, and signed tasks should not be treated as interchangeable.

## 45. Typing is a useful production modality

A 2026 Annual Review argues that typing can reveal language-production processes while avoiding some speech-specific motor constraints.

But typing introduces its own:

- orthographic;
- motor;
- technological

demands.

Cross-modality convergence is more informative than assuming one output modality is pure.

## 46. Signed languages are full languages

Signed languages possess:

- lexical structure;
- morphology;
- syntax;
- discourse;
- productive combinatorial systems.

They recruit visual-manual rather than auditory-vocal channels while engaging core language mechanisms.

This strongly supports separation of:

**language computation** from **speech**.

## 47. Language task impurity

Language tasks often recruit nonlanguage processes.

Examples:

### Lexical decision

Requires:
- visual/auditory perception;
- lexical familiarity;
- decision criterion;
- motor response.

### Picture naming

Requires:
- visual recognition;
- semantic access;
- lexical selection;
- phonological planning;
- articulation.

### Sentence comprehension

Requires:
- perception/reading;
- lexical access;
- syntax;
- semantics;
- memory;
- task response.

### Verbal fluency

Requires:
- semantic/lexical knowledge;
- search strategy;
- switching;
- inhibition;
- working memory;
- speech output.

Canonical rule:

**language task score != pure language process.**

## 48. Error localization

Language errors should be classified before mechanism inference.

Possible loci include:

- perceptual input;
- phonological representation;
- orthographic representation;
- lexical selection;
- semantic access;
- morphology;
- syntax;
- discourse/pragmatics;
- motor planning;
- monitoring/control.

One surface error can arise from multiple loci.

## 49. Development and experience matter

Language processing changes with:

- development;
- education/literacy;
- aging;
- sensory experience;
- proficiency;
- language exposure;
- multilingualism.

Prediction itself changes across the lifespan according to current 2026 review evidence.

Therefore:

`adult monolingual laboratory effect = universal language mechanism`

requires explicit generalization evidence.

## 50. Language Inference Gate

Before interpreting a language result, ask:

### A. Target

1. Is the claim about perception, lexical access, morphology, syntax, semantics, pragmatics, comprehension, or production?
2. Is it spoken, written, signed, or multimodal?
3. Is the claim about competence, online processing, or task performance?

### B. Input/perception

4. Could hearing/vision/signal quality explain performance?
5. Is accent/speaker variability relevant?
6. Is the input modality being confused with core language processing?

### C. Lexicon

7. Is the problem word-form recognition, lexical selection, or conceptual access?
8. Could frequency/familiarity/competition explain the effect?
9. Could cross-modal or cross-language activation contribute?

### D. Structure

10. What morphological/syntactic relation is required?
11. Is syntax being confused with linear word order?
12. Could lexical semantics or plausibility explain the apparent syntax effect?
13. Is hierarchical structure independently manipulated/measured?

### E. Meaning

14. Is lexical meaning distinguished from sentence/discourse meaning?
15. Could COG-06 semantic-control demands explain the result?
16. Is pragmatic inference being mistaken for literal semantics?

### F. Prediction

17. Is prediction actually measured, or inferred from easier processing?
18. Could post-input integration explain the effect?
19. What is predicted: word, feature, structure, referent, or discourse event?
20. Is surprisal/model probability being mistaken for human mechanism?

### G. Comprehension

21. Is processing incremental?
22. Could temporary ambiguity/reanalysis explain errors or delays?
23. Does final comprehension differ from early processing measures?

### H. Production

24. Is the failure in conceptual planning, lexical retrieval, morphosyntax, phonology, motor execution, monitoring, or repair?
25. Could speech-motor demands explain the result?
26. Is a production error being treated as loss of language knowledge?

### I. Language–thought boundary

27. Is a nonlinguistic reasoning/knowledge system required?
28. Is language difficulty being mistaken for general thought difficulty?
29. Is language support for a task being confused with constituting the underlying thought?

### J. Memory/control

30. What working-memory load exists?
31. What cognitive-control demands exist?
32. Is ambiguity/language switching driving domain-general control?
33. Could poor performance reflect memory/attention rather than language?

### K. Social/pragmatic context

34. Is common ground required?
35. Are speaker intentions/social norms relevant?
36. Is face-to-face multimodal communication different from isolated text/speech?

### L. Multilingualism

37. What languages does the participant know?
38. What are proficiency, dominance, age of acquisition, and use context?
39. Could L1 transfer or cross-language competition explain the result?
40. Is a monolingual norm being overgeneralized?

### M. Measurement

41. What language task is used?
42. Which nonlanguage processes does it require?
43. Is task reliability/validity sufficient for the claim?
44. Does the conclusion generalize across modalities/tasks?

### N. Individual inference

45. Is one error/task being converted into a stable language trait?
46. Is the result replicated across contexts/occasions?
47. What alternative locus remains viable?
48. What second task/manipulation would discriminate the alternatives?
49. What is the strongest supported language conclusion?
50. What wording would overclaim mechanism or general ability?

## 51. High-value failure modes

### Failure A — language = thought

Incorrect.

Correct:

Core language and many general reasoning/knowledge functions are functionally distinguishable while interacting.

### Failure B — word = meaning

Incorrect.

Correct:

Words are linguistic forms linked to conceptual/semantic representations.

### Failure C — naming failure = concept loss

Incorrect.

Correct:

Naming can fail at lexical, phonological, motor, or control stages despite preserved conceptual knowledge.

### Failure D — syntax = word order

Incorrect.

Correct:

Syntax includes hierarchical and dependency structure not reducible to adjacency.

### Failure E — semantic plausibility = syntactic parse

Incorrect.

Correct:

Structure and meaning jointly constrain interpretation but are distinct sources.

### Failure F — prediction = comprehension

Incorrect.

Correct:

Prediction is one dynamically engaged process family and is neither unitary nor obligatory.

### Failure G — surprisal effect = brain is an LLM

Incorrect.

Correct:

Computational predictability can model human processing without establishing identical mechanisms.

### Failure H — language network = entire communication system

Incorrect.

Correct:

Communication also requires perception, motor systems, pragmatics, social cognition, and context.

### Failure I — speech = language

Incorrect.

Correct:

Signed and written language demonstrate modality-independent linguistic computation plus modality-specific interfaces.

### Failure J — production = comprehension reversed

Incorrect.

Correct:

They share representations and interact, but production has distinct planning/monitoring/output demands.

### Failure K — speech error = language knowledge deficit

Incorrect.

Correct:

Errors can arise during planning, lexical access, phonology, motor execution, monitoring, or control.

### Failure L — pragmatics = semantics

Incorrect.

Correct:

Pragmatic interpretation adds speaker/context/common-ground inference.

### Failure M — inner speech = thought

Incorrect.

Correct:

Inner speech is one tool for cognition, not the totality of cognition.

### Failure N — bilingual = one cognitive type

Incorrect.

Correct:

Multilingual experience varies in proficiency, dominance, acquisition, use, and switching ecology.

### Failure O — bilingual language control = general executive advantage

Incorrect.

Correct:

Language control can engage control systems without implying universal domain-general enhancement.

### Failure P — language task = pure language measure

Incorrect.

Correct:

Most tasks include perceptual, memory, decision, control, or motor demands.

## Canonical working rule

**Specify the linguistic level and modality, separate form from meaning and language from neighboring systems, localize comprehension/production demands, test prediction/control/pragmatic alternatives, and only then infer a language-specific mechanism or stable language ability.**

Never:

**language-task performance -> direct readout of thought or general cognition.**

## Current-standard note

As of 2026-11-19:

- NIMH RDoC continues to include Language within Cognitive Systems and defines it broadly as shared symbolic representations supporting thought and communication;
- strong evidence supports functional specialization of a core language network and its partial dissociation from general reasoning/knowledge and peripheral perceptual/motor systems, while the strongest claims about one monolithic/localized network remain actively debated;
- current language-comprehension research treats prediction as dynamic, multifactorial, and non-obligatory rather than one universal next-word-prediction mechanism;
- current production research treats message planning, lexical access, morphosyntactic planning, motor realization, monitoring, control, and repair as distinguishable contributors;
- contemporary psycholinguistics increasingly treats comprehension and production as coupled during real conversation and incorporates common ground, turn-taking, multimodality, and joint action;
- multilingualism is increasingly modeled dimensionally through proficiency, dominance, acquisition history, use, and switching ecology rather than as one binary bilingual/monolingual contrast.
