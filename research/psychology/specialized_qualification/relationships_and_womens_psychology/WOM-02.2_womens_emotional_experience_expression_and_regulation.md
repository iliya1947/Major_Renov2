E1. Trampe, Quoidbach & Taquet (2015), Emotions in Everyday Life. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0145450
Access: publisher HTML selected methods and results including gender-specific frequency section and network results. 11,572 app users, 75% women, 65,721 reports; median participation span 8.6 days, mean age 32.9. Overall frequency of any reported emotion was nearly identical across the two reported groups (89.93% men, 89.92% women). Women reported exclusively negative emotions at 16.83% of reports versus 14.02% for men, exclusively positive at 38.96% versus 45.30%. These descriptive percentages concern reports in this selected sample, not measured intensity or clinical mood. Self-selected users, variable contribution per participant, customizable prompts and reporting bias limit transport. Group-level emotion network relations are not person-specific causal dynamics. No complete article reread.

E2. Little evidence for sex or ovarian hormone influences on affective variability (2021). https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-00143-7
Access: publisher methods (participants), selected results/discussion, study considerations and conclusions directly inspected. 142 retained participants (30 men, 28 naturally cycling women, 84 users of three oral contraceptive formulations), ages 18–38; 75 daily assessments, inclusion at least 80% completion. Compared positive/negative affect volatility, first-order inertia and cyclicity. Average Bayes-factor evidence favored no sex differences by 3.3 to 1; group comparisons within women averaged 2.7 to 1 favoring no differences. These are averages over reported comparisons, not posterior probabilities, and the evidence was not overwhelming. One formulation comparison indicated possibly greater positive affect inertia/cyclicity among users. Small selected groups, attrition (235 began), nonrandom contraceptive use and daily aggregate measurements matter. No daily hormone assay; the study explicitly does not establish direct hormone–emotion associations or absence of cycle phase effects. The main inference concerns overall variability over the studied daily timescale, not all emotional outcomes.

E3. Johnson & Whisman (2013), Gender differences in rumination: A meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2013.03.019 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24089583/
Access: publisher indexed abstract/highlights and primary indexed methods excerpts; direct PMC opening blocked. 57 studies, 59 samples, N=14,321. Fixed-effect estimates for depressive rumination d=.24 (SE=.02), brooding d=.19 (SE=.03), reflection d=.17 (SE=.03), higher reported scores among women; subtype analysis k=23. Small mean differences, not categorical profiles or evidence of any specific woman's ruminative process. The historical self-report literature and fixed-effect model do not establish universality, causal mediation or the effectiveness of a regulatory strategy. Authors found no detected heterogeneity/publication bias; absence of detection is not proof of absence.

E4. McDuff, Kodra, el Kaliouby & LaFrance (2017), A large-scale analysis of sex differences in facial expressions. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0173942
Access: publisher abstract, participants/procedure, selected results, discussion, conclusion and limitations inspected. 2,106 recruited viewers across five countries; 1,862 retained after discomfort exclusions (879 women, 983 men); 18,620 recordings before video-quality exclusions. Automated facial action coding during ad viewing at home. More lip-corner pulls and inner-brow raises in women, more brow furrows in men; not universally greater expressivity across all actions. A facial action is not a uniquely identified emotion: brow furrowing may reflect cognition or confusion, for example. Cameras known to be active, possible audience effects, different ads across countries, webcam selection and coding validity limit transfer. No covert observation, emotion detection or motive-reading application follows.

E5. Examining Daily Affect Variability by Individual Differences Among a Diverse Community Sample (2026). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42189520/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13215141/
Access: indexed primary abstract and article excerpts; direct PubMed opening cache miss. N=300 adults, ages 18–80, 50% women, two days of EMA with random sampling within 45-minute waking blocks. Reported unadjusted independent-sample comparisons associate being female with greater positive-affect variability. This qualifies a universal null claim and should not be treated as a direct contradiction of E2's daily 75-day estimates: different temporal granularity, sample and variability operationalization can matter. Adjusted effects, exact magnitudes and analysis robustness were not verified; no claim about greater negative-affect variability, hormonal mechanism or a particular woman.

E6. Sels, Debeer & Kalokerinos (2025 preprint), Men Do Not Suppress Emotions More than Women in Everyday Life: Evidence from Large Scale Experience-Sampling Data. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/3vxzj_v1 ; https://sciety.org/articles/activity/10.31234/osf.io/3vxzj_v1
Access: preprint abstract and version record, read in full; no full manuscript inspection. Reported preregistered integration of 11 experience-sampling studies, N=1,616, 106,120 observations. Global questionnaires indicated more habitual expressive suppression among men, but authors reported no corresponding difference in momentary suppression or tested negative emotion categories. Peer-reviewed final publication not located in this pass; treat as provisional, not established equivalence. Both methods rely on reports and may capture different constructs or contexts; momentary reports are not necessarily objective behavior. Potential overlap with other pooled emotion-regulation datasets should be checked before counting as independent replication. Supports a measurement-specific hypothesis rather than a conclusion that one gender always conceals more emotion.

E7. Patterns in affect and personality states across the menstrual cycle (2025). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39804343/
Access: indexed primary abstract snippets; direct PubMed blocked. Two longitudinal studies (N=757 online diary; N=257 laboratory study including repeated hormone assays). Study 1 found broadly similar daily dynamics among naturally cycling women and contraceptive users, except greater negative-affect variability in the latter and a descriptive nonsignificant premenstrual positive-affect decrease in naturally cycling women. This provides a within-women comparison and indicates that overall variability and cycle-related associations should be distinguished. Detailed hormone effects, design and uncertainty not verified here; defer to WOM-06. No individual inference from cycle phase or contraceptive status.

## 3. Integrated distinctions

Do not use "emotionality" as a single explanatory variable. Separate:
1. Frequency, valence, intensity and duration of experienced emotions.
2. Within-person variability, instability, inertia, event reactivity, recovery and recurrence; specify measurement timescale.
3. Reported experience and global self-description versus momentary report, observable expressive action and physiological response.
4. Emotion-specific expression, normative display expectations, role constraints and actual goals; do not presume norms explain all observed differences.
5. Regulation purpose, strategy, frequency of use, ability and actual effectiveness. Rumination is not equivalent to emotion intensity, caring, attachment or commitment. Limited expression is not proof of intentional suppression or concealed desire.
6. Mixed simultaneous affect versus alternation, attitudinal ambivalence and competing goal appraisal (IND-02.2, WOM-02.1). A sample-level percentage of women moderating mixed-emotion effects in an earlier meta-analysis is not an individual sex/gender difference.
7. Contextual exposure, developmental stage, individual history and measured biological factors. A group category does not measure a hormone or a causal mechanism.

Evidence supports some average differences on particular measures, not a uniform female emotional profile. E2/E5 show why timescale and population matter; E3 shows a small questionnaire mean difference; E4 demonstrates emotion-specific observed actions; E6 is a provisional trait/momentary counterexample. Do not substitute group tendencies for a woman's person-specific observations.

## 4. Application and checks

Identify the emotion, target, time window, evidence channel, situation and potential regulation aim. Distinguish report from observation and inference. Compare hypotheses for low or high expression, including actual affect, other channels, norms, strategic choice, and missing evidence; none is established simply by the display. Trace repeated naturally occurring contexts and opportunities, compare evidence provenance and temporal sequence, distinguish state changes from durable preferences and actions. Preserve explicit decisions and boundaries independently of inferred feelings. No covert tests, engineered distress or manipulative guidance.

Generic invented exercises, unrelated to private records:
A. In an imaginary training sample, women and men have similar average frequency of reported emotion but differ in the proportion of positive reports. These are distinct estimands; neither establishes global emotional intensity or an individual's state.
B. An adult woman smiles during a formal ceremony and later reports frustration. A smile alone does not identify her experience or prove intentional concealment; examine her report, context, timing and other observations without assuming a unique cause.
C. Two studies find different variability effects using daily means and frequent within-day reports. Distinguish sampling interval and statistic before concluding one refutes the other. E2/E5 external comparison motivates this rule.
D. Global strategy questionnaires suggest a group difference absent in momentary reports. Consider recall, identity, construct coverage, contexts and uncertainty. Do not declare equivalence or stereotype causation from a nonsignificant difference. E6 is a preliminary empirical challenge.
