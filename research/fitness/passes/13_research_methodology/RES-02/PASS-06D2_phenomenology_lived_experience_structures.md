# 2. Canonical phenomenology purpose

Phenomenology is not simply asking participants what they think.

Its central target is:

**the lived experience of a phenomenon and the structure/meaning of that experience as it appears to persons who have lived it.**

Canonical question:

What is it like to live, undergo or embody phenomenon X, and how is that experience constituted or made meaningful?

Examples:
- what is it like to train with persistent pain?;
- how is fatigue experienced during return to training?;
- what does competitive performance mean to an elite athlete?;
- how is bodily change experienced during rehabilitation?;
- how does fear appear during re-exposure to an injured movement?

Critical rule:

The phrase "lived experience" in a title or aim does not itself establish phenomenology.

A phenomenological claim needs alignment among:
1. question;
2. phenomenological orientation;
3. participant eligibility;
4. data generation;
5. analysis;
6. claim language.

---

# 3. Phenomenology is a family, not one procedure

## Primarily descriptive phenomenology

Typical lineage:
- Husserl;
- Giorgi / descriptive phenomenological psychology;
- related descriptive adaptations.

Primary target:

Describe the experiential structure or essential constituents of the phenomenon while restraining premature explanatory or theoretical interpretation.

## Primarily interpretive / hermeneutic phenomenology

Typical lineage:
- Heidegger;
- Gadamer;
- van Manen and related traditions.

Primary target:

Interpret the meaning of lived experience as situated, embodied, relational, temporal and embedded in a lifeworld.

Project rule:

Do not store only "design = phenomenology".

Record at least:
phenomenology → descriptive OR interpretive/hermeneutic OR IPA/other specified variant.

# 4. Descriptive phenomenology

## 4.1 Scientific target

Descriptive phenomenology seeks disciplined description of experience as experienced.

In Giorgi-type phenomenological psychology the analytic target is more than a list of themes. It moves from concrete descriptions toward:
- meaning units;
- psychologically relevant transformations;
- structural/essential description.

## 4.2 Reduction / epoché / bracketing

A central descriptive commitment is phenomenological reduction: restrain taken-for-granted assumptions, prior theories and premature explanations so the phenomenon can be examined as given in experience.

Applied practices can include:
- bracketing;
- reflexive notes;
- explicit accounting of prior assumptions.

Critical nuance:

Bracketing does not mean the researcher became bias-free.

Project interpretation:
a disciplined attempt to identify and restrain presuppositions so they do not silently dominate description.

## 4.3 Analysis alignment

A Giorgi-style analysis commonly has whole–part–whole logic:
1. read the full description for the whole;
2. identify meaning units;
3. transform units into phenomenologically/psychologically relevant expressions;
4. synthesize the structure/constituents.

Different descriptive schools can use different procedures.

Rule:

Do not infer descriptive phenomenology merely because a study coded transcripts, generated themes or mentioned bracketing.

---

# 5. Interpretive / hermeneutic phenomenology

## 5.1 Scientific target

Hermeneutic phenomenology treats lived experience as already:
- situated;
- historical/cultural;
- embodied;
- relational;
- interpreted by the person living it.

Primary target:

Interpret meaning structures of lived experience in context.

## 5.2 Researcher role

The researcher is part of the interpretive process.

Relevant metadata:
- prior relationship with the phenomenon;
- disciplinary background;
- assumptions/pre-understandings;
- relationship to participants;
- reflexive practices;
- how interpretation was developed and challenged.

## 5.3 Hermeneutic movement

Analysis commonly moves recursively between:
parts ↔ whole
and
participant account ↔ context ↔ researcher interpretation.

This should not be converted into one rigid algorithm for all hermeneutic traditions.

## 5.4 van Manen boundary

van Manen's phenomenology of practice is an interpretive/hermeneutic approach centered on lived meaning and phenomenological writing/reflection.

A paper citing van Manen but performing only generic code-frequency thematic analysis should be checked for methodological fit rather than automatically accepted as phenomenological.

---

# 6. Descriptive vs interpretive phenomenology

| Axis | Primarily descriptive | Primarily interpretive/hermeneutic |
|---|---|---|
| Main target | experiential structure / essence / constituents | interpreted meaning of lived experience in lifeworld/context |
| Researcher stance | restrain/suspend presuppositions | researcher interpretation is unavoidable and reflexively examined |
| Theory during analysis | generally constrained/bracketed | may participate in interpretation depending approach |
| Typical logic | meaning units → transformed description → structure | recursive interpretation of parts/whole, meaning and context |
| Reflexivity | supports reduction/bracketing discipline | makes interpretive position transparent |
| Output | structural description | interpretive account of meaning/lifeworld |
| Examples | Giorgi-type descriptive phenomenology | Heideggerian / van Manen-type hermeneutic phenomenology |

Caveat:

This is a useful routing distinction, not a perfect philosophical binary. Methodological literature explicitly notes overlap and disagreement about how sharp the boundary should be.

# 7. Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA)

## 7.1 Position

IPA is a distinct experiential qualitative methodology developed in psychology/health psychology.

It is informed by:
- phenomenology;
- hermeneutics;
- idiography.

It is not simply phenomenology plus generic thematic analysis.

## 7.2 Core target

IPA asks:

How is this participant making sense of a major lived experience?

The focus is personal meaning-making rather than only identifying common topics across a sample.

## 7.3 Idiography

IPA has a strong idiographic commitment:

Analyze each case deeply before moving to cross-case patterns.

Canonical sequence:
case 1 deep analysis → case 2 deep analysis → further cases → cross-case convergence/divergence.

## 7.4 Double hermeneutic

Canonical IPA relation:

participant tries to make sense of experience;
researcher tries to make sense of the participant making sense of experience.

Therefore researcher interpretation is:
- expected;
- central;
- constrained by participant material;
- reflexively accountable.

## 7.5 Giving voice + making sense

Larkin et al. distinguish complementary commitments:
1. phenomenological: understand/give voice to participant concerns;
2. interpretive: contextualize/make sense of those concerns.

A coherent IPA account should avoid both extremes:
- pure transcript summary with no interpretation;
- unconstrained theorizing detached from the accounts.

---

# 8. IPA is not interchangeable with all hermeneutic phenomenology

IPA and van Manen-type approaches overlap in interpretive phenomenological commitments but are not the same method.

IPA has distinctive emphasis on:
- idiography;
- case-by-case analysis;
- psychological meaning-making;
- double hermeneutic;
- cross-case convergence/divergence after idiographic analysis.

Project rule:

Record IPA as its own specified phenomenological methodology.

# 9. Sampling logic

## 9.1 Direct lived-experience criterion

The participant/data source must provide access to the target lived experience.

Canonical condition:

The participant personally lived the phenomenon being studied.

Example:
If the target is lived experience of ACL rehabilitation, a clinician treating ACL patients but never undergoing that rehabilitation is not a direct phenomenological participant for that target.

They can be a stakeholder in a different inquiry.

## 9.2 Purposive sampling

Purposeful selection is common because direct experiential relevance is required.

Possible dimensions:
- shared phenomenon;
- stage/time since event;
- severity/exposure;
- age;
- sex;
- training level;
- sport;
- context.

## 9.3 Homogeneity vs variation

IPA often uses relative homogeneity around the target experiential context so idiographic comparison is coherent.

Other phenomenological studies can deliberately seek contextual variation.

Rule:

Homogeneity is not intrinsically better. It must fit the target claim.

# 10. Sample size and data adequacy

## No universal phenomenology N

There is no valid universal rule such as "phenomenology requires exactly 5–10 participants."

Published ranges are heuristics, not validity thresholds.

## IPA and small samples

Small relatively homogeneous samples are common because idiographic depth is analytically expensive.

Even single-case IPA can be coherent when the target is genuinely idiographic.

Rule:

Small N is not automatically weak design.

But small N plus shallow analysis is not justified merely by invoking idiography.

## Adequacy criteria

Phenomenological adequacy can be justified through:
- experiential richness;
- study specificity;
- depth of accounts;
- analysis strategy;
- information power;
- methodological tradition;
- conceptual/meaning sufficiency.

## Saturation boundary

Do not require saturation as a universal phenomenology criterion.

If saturation is claimed, extract:
- what saturation meant;
- how it was assessed;
- why it fit the chosen phenomenological methodology.

---

# 11. Data sources are not limited to interviews

## Interviews

Common because they allow first-person experiential accounts.

But questions should elicit concrete experience rather than only abstract opinion.

## Written narratives

Can provide detailed experiential descriptions.

## Diaries / journals

Useful when:
- experience unfolds over time;
- recall matters;
- moment-to-moment experience is relevant.

## Observation

Can complement accounts for:
- embodied action;
- contextual behavior;
- practice environments.

Observation alone cannot automatically reveal subjective meaning.

## Participant-created material

Potential sources include:
- photographs;
- drawings;
- audio/video;
- body maps;
- other elicitation materials.

Rule:

The defining feature is not "interview used" but whether the data source gives methodologically coherent access to the lived phenomenon.

---

# 12. Longitudinal phenomenology

Phenomenological inquiry need not be one-time/cross-sectional.

A longitudinal design can ask how meaning/experience changes through time.

Longitudinal IPA retains:
- idiographic trajectory;
- within-person change;
- repeated sense-making;
- cross-time interpretation.

Fitness relevance:
- injury → rehabilitation → return to sport;
- novice → trained identity transition;
- competition season;
- overtraining/burnout/recovery;
- body-image change;
- adaptation to chronic pain.

Critical boundary:

Repeated interviews alone do not create longitudinal phenomenology.

The analysis must explicitly examine continuity/change in lived meaning over time.

# 13. Reflexivity differs by tradition

## Descriptive phenomenology

Reflexivity supports:
- identifying assumptions;
- reduction/bracketing;
- protecting descriptive attention.

## Hermeneutic phenomenology

Reflexivity supports:
- exposing pre-understandings;
- documenting interpretive position;
- examining researcher/context influence.

## IPA

Reflexivity is central because researcher interpretation is part of the double hermeneutic.

Project extraction:
1. researcher discipline;
2. prior experience with phenomenon;
3. participant relationship;
4. assumptions/pre-understandings;
5. reflexive method;
6. how interpretive decisions were documented/challenged.

---

# 14. Analysis-method alignment

A phenomenology label is credible only if analysis matches the claimed approach.

## Descriptive
Look for:
- reduction/bracketing logic;
- experiential descriptions;
- meaning units/transformations;
- structural synthesis.

## Hermeneutic
Look for:
- explicit interpretive stance;
- lifeworld/context;
- recursive parts–whole interpretation;
- phenomenological reflection/writing;
- reflexivity.

## IPA
Look for:
- idiographic case-by-case analysis;
- experiential meaning;
- researcher interpretation;
- cross-case patterns after individual analyses;
- convergence/divergence.

Red flag:

"We used phenomenology because we interviewed people, then applied generic thematic analysis."

This may still yield useful qualitative findings, but the phenomenological design claim requires stronger methodological alignment.

---

# 15. What phenomenological evidence can establish

Phenomenological evidence can strongly support claims about:
- structure of lived experience;
- personal meaning;
- embodiment;
- temporality;
- relational experience;
- contextual meaning;
- how participants make sense of events;
- experiential similarities/divergences;
- evolution of meaning over time.

It can generate:
- hypotheses;
- candidate mechanisms;
- intervention targets;
- quantitative research questions;
- patient/athlete-centered design requirements.

---

# 16. What it cannot establish by itself

Phenomenology alone does not estimate:
- prevalence;
- incidence;
- population distribution;
- average causal effect;
- intervention efficacy;
- mediator effect size;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance;
- biological mechanism.

Critical causal boundary:

A participant statement such as "I became stronger because the program made me confident" supports the participant's experienced/explanatory meaning.

It does not establish that confidence causally mediated strength gain.

Generalization boundary:

Phenomenological transfer is conceptual/experiential/contextual, not statistical population generalization.

# 19. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| lived-experience wording vs phenomenology | wording alone does not establish methodology |
| interview vs phenomenology | interview is collection method |
| themes vs phenomenology | thematic output alone is insufficient |
| descriptive vs hermeneutic phenomenology | materially different commitments |
| bracketing vs elimination of researcher influence | bracketing is methodological restraint, not neutrality proof |
| hermeneutic interpretation vs arbitrary interpretation | interpretation remains accountable to data/method |
| IPA vs all phenomenology | IPA is one specific interpretive/idiographic approach |
| IPA vs generic thematic analysis | IPA requires idiographic experiential interpretation |
| small N vs poor design | small N can be coherent for deep idiography |
| small N vs automatically adequate | adequacy still requires depth/information |
| saturation vs universal requirement | may not fit every phenomenological tradition |
| participant explanation vs causal mechanism | experiential meaning is not causal identification |
| transferability vs statistical generalization | different inferential targets |
| repeated interviews vs longitudinal phenomenology | temporal meaning change must be analyzed |
| phenomenology vs qualitative description | lived-experience structure/meaning vs direct descriptive summary |
| single-case IPA vs clinical case report | idiographic qualitative analysis vs clinical description |
