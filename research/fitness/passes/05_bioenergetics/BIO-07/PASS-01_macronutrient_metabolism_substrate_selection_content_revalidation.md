## 1. Qualification purpose / Question Card

A fitness trainer needs a correct substrate-selection model to avoid:

- teaching “carbs = anaerobic / fat = aerobic”;
- assuming carbohydrate disappears from fuel use at low intensity;
- assuming fat oxidation stops during hard work;
- equating lipolysis, fatty-acid availability, fatty-acid uptake and fatty-acid oxidation;
- treating beta-oxidation as the entire pathway from adipose tissue triglyceride to ATP;
- using one universal “fat-burning zone” or Fatmax percentage;
- assuming endurance training always increases relative fat use at the same relative intensity;
- assuming low glycogen means carbohydrate oxidation becomes impossible;
- assuming fasted exercise automatically produces more long-term fat loss because acute fat oxidation is higher;
- treating protein oxidation as zero;
- treating “metabolic flexibility” as synonymous with maximal fat oxidation;
- interpreting whole-body RER as a direct muscle-specific flux measurement under all exercise intensities.

Primary trainer-level decisions supported later:

- understand why fuel use shifts across workloads and session duration;
- distinguish acute fuel selection from chronic body-composition outcomes;
- interpret low-glycogen, fasted/fed and endurance-trained states without single-variable stories;
- understand why high-intensity performance remains carbohydrate dependent even in highly fat-adapted states;
- interpret RER/Fatmax/metabolic-flexibility outputs with method boundaries;
- avoid diet/programming prescriptions from an acute oxidation measurement alone.

Target population for this content pass:

- healthy adults performing aerobic/endurance exercise;
- human substrate-metabolism studies receive priority;

Question type:

- primarily **mechanism + evidence mapping**;

## 2. Core substrate-selection framework

During exercise, ATP demand can be supported by oxidation of multiple carbon sources simultaneously.

Major oxidative fuel inputs include:

### Carbohydrate-derived
- muscle glycogen;
- blood glucose;
- liver-derived glucose;
- ingested/exogenous carbohydrate during exercise;
- lactate and other carbohydrate-derived intermediates that can be oxidized.

### Lipid-derived
- plasma non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA/FFA), largely released from adipose tissue triglyceride;
- intramuscular triglyceride (IMTG);
- to a smaller/context-dependent extent, fatty acids made available from circulating triglyceride-rich lipoproteins.

### Amino-acid/protein-derived
- selected amino acids oxidized directly or after conversion to TCA intermediates;
- amino acids contributing carbon to gluconeogenesis and anaplerosis.

The relative contribution is not fixed.

It depends on interacting:

- exercise intensity;
- exercise duration;
- absolute power/speed and relative intensity;
- training status;
- muscle-fibre recruitment;
- glycogen status;
- glucose/FFA availability;
- fed vs fasted state;
- carbohydrate ingestion;
- hormonal state;
- exercise mode;
- environment;
- sex/body composition and other individual factors.

Canonical rule:

**substrate selection is a dynamic distribution problem, not a binary carbohydrate-vs-fat switch.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 3. Carbohydrate oxidation

PASS at content level.

Carbohydrate supports ATP resynthesis through:

1. muscle glycogen breakdown or glucose uptake;
2. glycolysis;
3. pyruvate formation;
4. regulated PDH conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA;
5. TCA/ETC/OXPHOS for complete oxidative metabolism.

Muscle glycogen can also support substrate-level glycolytic ATP production without complete oxidation of all glycolytic carbon.

Therefore:

**carbohydrate use ≠ only blood-glucose oxidation.**

Whole-body carbohydrate oxidation can include different endogenous and exogenous pools whose relative contributions vary over time.

Hargreaves & Spriet (PMID **32747792**) retain carbohydrate as a major fuel for both high rates of substrate-level ATP production and oxidative metabolism during exercise.

Canonical rule:

**carbohydrate is both an anaerobic/substrate-level and an oxidative fuel; “carbohydrate metabolism” cannot be equated with anaerobic metabolism.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 4. Muscle glycogen, blood glucose and exogenous carbohydrate are distinct pools

Exercise carbohydrate use should not be collapsed into one pool.

Distinguish:

- **muscle glycogen oxidation/use**;
- **blood glucose uptake/oxidation**;
- **hepatic glucose output**;
- **exogenous carbohydrate appearance/oxidation**.

Starting muscle glycogen strongly affects endogenous substrate use.

Human randomized crossover work (PMID **31095946**) showed that at ~64% VO2peak:

- low starting muscle glycogen reduced endogenous carbohydrate oxidation;
- fat oxidation increased;
- exogenous carbohydrate oxidation remained similar despite lower glycogen.

This establishes an important boundary:

**low muscle glycogen ≠ inability to oxidize ingested/blood-borne carbohydrate.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

Detailed carbohydrate dosing and glycogen repletion remain NUT/BIO-10.

## 5. Fat oxidation

PASS at content level.

“Fat oxidation” during exercise is the integrated result of multiple upstream steps:

1. triglyceride lipolysis;
2. fatty-acid release/availability;
3. delivery in blood;
4. transport into muscle;
5. transport/partitioning within muscle;
6. mitochondrial entry of long-chain fatty acyl groups;
7. beta-oxidation;
8. TCA/ETC/OXPHOS of resulting acetyl-CoA/reducing equivalents.

Sources include:

- adipose-derived plasma NEFA;
- IMTG;
- other circulating lipid sources depending on context.

Canonical rule:

**lipolysis ≠ fatty-acid availability ≠ muscle uptake ≠ mitochondrial entry ≠ beta-oxidation ≠ whole-body fat oxidation.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 6. Beta-oxidation

PASS at content level.

At trainer-level resolution:

1. fatty acids are activated to fatty acyl-CoA;
2. long-chain fatty acyl groups require mitochondrial transport involving the carnitine shuttle;
3. CPT1, carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase and CPT2 participate in long-chain mitochondrial entry;
4. beta-oxidation sequentially shortens fatty acyl-CoA;
5. cycles generate acetyl-CoA plus NADH/FADH2;
6. acetyl-CoA enters TCA metabolism and reducing equivalents support ETC/OXPHOS.

Review PMID **29221849** supports mitochondrial fatty-acyl import/carnitine availability as an important control site during exercise and emphasizes interaction between glycolytic flux and fatty-acid oxidation.

Current review PMID **42627570** retains a multi-site model of exercise fat metabolism rather than a single “fat-burning enzyme.”

Canonical rule:

**beta-oxidation is the mitochondrial fatty-acid degradation pathway; it is not synonymous with adipose lipolysis or with total-body fat loss.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 7. Fat oxidation is jointly limited by supply and muscle oxidation capacity

Increasing plasma fatty-acid availability does not guarantee proportional fat oxidation.

Human work and reviews show control at multiple levels:

- adipose lipolysis;
- muscle blood flow and delivery;
- sarcolemmal transport;
- intracellular partitioning;
- mitochondrial transport;
- carnitine availability;
- beta-oxidation/TCA/respiratory capacity;
- competition/integration with carbohydrate flux.

At high exercise intensities, reduced fat oxidation cannot be explained only by lower plasma FFA availability.

This is important because:

**“more FFA in blood” ≠ “more fat oxidized.”**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 8. Protein / amino-acid contribution during exercise

PASS at content level.

Protein/amino acids are not normally the dominant exercise fuel in healthy, adequately fed humans, but their energetic contribution is not zero.

A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID **40135445**) included:

- 30 articles;
- 286 participants;
- endurance exercise of at least 60 min.

Across included studies:

- protein oxidation increased during endurance exercise;
- pooled protein contribution averaged approximately **3.28%** of total exercise energy expenditure;
- protein oxidation rate increased with exercise intensity in the meta-regression.

This pooled mean is not a universal constant.

Protein contribution depends on:

- exercise duration/intensity;
- glycogen/carbohydrate availability;
- energy availability;
- feeding state;
- measurement method;
- training context.

Older mechanistic reviews also support oxidation of branched-chain and other amino acids during prolonged exercise.

Canonical rule:

**protein is usually a subsidiary exercise fuel, not a zero-contribution fuel; exact percentage is context- and method-dependent.**

Confidence: **HIGH for non-zero/subsidiary role; MODERATE-HIGH for pooled quantitative estimate outside the included endurance contexts**.

## 9. Substrate selection by intensity

PASS at content level.

The most robust qualitative pattern is:

- at lower exercise intensities, a larger fraction of oxidative energy can come from fat;
- as intensity rises, carbohydrate contribution generally increases;
- absolute fat oxidation often rises from low to moderate intensity, reaches an individual peak, then declines at higher intensities;
- at high intensity, carbohydrate usually becomes the dominant fuel because ATP turnover and glycolytic flux rise substantially.

Classic stable-isotope work (PMID **8214047**) showed:

- plasma glucose uptake and muscle glycogen oxidation increased with intensity;
- plasma fatty-acid release/oxidation patterns did not simply rise with workload.

Current human fuel-use review PMID **40763088** likewise describes:

- highest relative fat contribution at low intensity;
- peak absolute fat oxidation at mid-intensity;
- lower total/relative fat oxidation at high workloads.

Canonical rule:

**higher intensity generally shifts fuel selection toward carbohydrate, but this is a graded shift rather than a binary crossover where fat suddenly becomes zero.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 10. No universal Fatmax / crossover percentage

The project does not retain a single universal statement such as:

- “Fatmax is 50% VO2max”;
- “Fatmax is 65% VO2max”;
- “the crossover always occurs at X%.”

Why:

- nutritional state shifts the curve;
- training status shifts the curve;
- exercise mode matters;
- stage duration/protocol matters;
- sex/body composition may matter;
- measurement equations matter;
- there is large inter-individual variability.

A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID **42166325**) found that Fatmax was, on average, below LT/GET, but the difference varied with participant characteristics, nutritional status, design, protocol and analytical method.

A current review (PMID **42627570**) reports very wide observed individual Fatmax ranges.

Canonical rule:

**Fatmax is an individualized, protocol-dependent measurement, not a universal physiological intensity boundary.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 11. “Fat-burning zone” does not equal best fat-loss intensity

BIO-07 only establishes substrate selection.

Acute exercise at an intensity with high fat oxidation can produce a larger fraction of exercise energy from fat.

That does **not** establish:

- greater 24-h fat balance;
- greater weekly fat loss;
- greater total energy expenditure;
- superior body-composition change;
- superior training adaptation.

Those outcomes depend on broader energy balance, diet, training dose and adaptation.

Canonical boundary:

**acute fat oxidation during a session ≠ chronic adipose-fat loss.**

Confidence: **HIGH inference boundary**.

Body-composition prescription remains NUT/PRO.

## 12. Substrate selection by duration

PASS at content level.

At a fixed submaximal workload, fuel sources can shift as exercise continues.

Classic tracer work (PMID **8214047**) during prolonged exercise at ~65% VO2max found over time:

- increasing contribution from plasma-derived substrates;
- declining contribution from muscle glycogen;
- declining contribution from muscle triglyceride in that protocol.

More broadly, prolonged exercise tends to increase reliance on:

- plasma fatty acids;
- blood glucose / hepatic glucose output;
- ingested carbohydrate when provided;

as local intramuscular stores are progressively used.

However duration effects are not independent of:

- intensity;
- carbohydrate ingestion;
- starting glycogen;
- training status;
- environmental stress.

Canonical rule:

**duration changes both which macronutrient is used and which storage pool supplies that macronutrient.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 13. Duration does not create a universal “fat takeover”

It is incorrect to teach:

“after X minutes → fat becomes the main fuel”

because the time course depends on workload and substrate state.

Examples:

- prolonged moderate exercise may show increasing relative fat contribution;
- prolonged high-intensity endurance competition can remain strongly carbohydrate dependent;
- carbohydrate ingestion can maintain high exogenous/whole-body carbohydrate oxidation during prolonged exercise;
- low glycogen can shift oxidation toward fat from the start.

Canonical rule:

**time alone does not determine substrate selection; duration interacts with intensity and availability.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 14. Influence of training status

PASS at content level.

Endurance training can increase skeletal-muscle capacity for fat oxidation through adaptations including:

- greater mitochondrial content/capacity;
- greater fatty-acid transport/handling capacity;
- changes in IMTG utilization;
- oxidative enzyme adaptations;
- altered metabolic control at a given workload.

At the **same absolute workload**, endurance training commonly produces:

- lower relative exercise intensity;
- lower carbohydrate/glycogen reliance;
- greater relative fat oxidation.

Human longitudinal studies support this pattern.

However the statement becomes more complex at the **same relative intensity**.

Evidence is mixed/context-dependent:

- some studies show greater fat oxidation in trained participants;
- some longitudinal studies show little/no difference in fuel proportions when pre/post testing is matched to the same relative intensity;
- trained participants can perform substantially greater absolute work at the same %VO2max, changing total substrate flux.

The review in PMCID **PMC4672006** specifically highlights this absolute-vs-relative workload problem.

Canonical rule:

**training status modifies substrate use, but the direction/magnitude must be interpreted with absolute workload, relative intensity, exercise mode and nutritional state specified.**

Confidence: **HIGH for training influence / MODERATE-HIGH for exact same-relative-intensity effect**.

---

## 15. Training status ≠ automatic carbohydrate sparing in every task

For high-intensity performance:

- trained athletes may oxidize large absolute amounts of carbohydrate because they sustain higher absolute power/speed;
- enhanced fat oxidation does not eliminate carbohydrate dependence at high workloads;
- “better fat oxidation” is not equivalent to “use less carbohydrate in all exercise.”

Canonical rule:

**greater oxidative/fat capacity expands fuel options; it does not abolish the requirement for high carbohydrate flux when ATP demand is high.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 16. Influence of glycogen availability

PASS at content level.

Starting muscle glycogen is an important substrate-selection variable.

Human crossover study PMID **31095946**:

- low glycogen reduced endogenous carbohydrate oxidation;
- increased fat oxidation;
- did not reduce exogenous carbohydrate oxidation during carbohydrate feeding.

A large meta-analysis of human muscle glycogen studies (PMID **29923148**) found baseline glycogen and exercise intensity were important determinants of glycogen utilization during exercise.

Recent randomized dietary work PMID **40922559** showed that short-term diet-induced changes in muscle glycogen were associated with substantial shifts in peak fat oxidation and Fatmax.

Canonical rule:

**glycogen availability changes fuel selection; low glycogen generally shifts oxidation away from endogenous carbohydrate toward fat but does not create a carbohydrate-free state.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 17. Low glycogen is not a direct marker of mitochondrial function

Recent human work shows that manipulating glycogen/carbohydrate availability can change:

- peak fat oxidation;
- Fatmax;
- endogenous carbohydrate use;

without requiring a change in intrinsic mitochondrial coupling/function.

Therefore:

**substrate availability can change measured fuel selection even when mitochondrial machinery itself has not changed.**

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual boundary / MODERATE-HIGH direct evidence**.

Detailed train-low adaptation strategy remains NUT/BIO-16.

---

## 18. Influence of feeding state

PASS at content level.

Fed vs fasted state changes acute substrate availability and hormonal control.

Pre-exercise carbohydrate intake can:

- raise glucose/insulin;
- suppress adipose lipolysis;
- reduce plasma FFA availability;
- increase carbohydrate oxidation;
- reduce fat oxidation in many submaximal exercise protocols.

Classic human tracer work (PMID **9357807**) showed pre-exercise carbohydrate suppressed lipolysis sufficiently to limit fat oxidation during low-intensity exercise.

A systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID **27609363**) found greater acute fat oxidation during aerobic exercise performed fasted than fed across included studies.

Canonical rule:

**fasted exercise often increases acute fat oxidation relative to carbohydrate-fed exercise, but the magnitude depends on meal composition, timing, exercise intensity and duration.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 19. Fasted exercise ≠ proven superior fat-loss strategy

Acute substrate oxidation and chronic body-composition outcome are different estimands.

A session with greater fat oxidation can be offset later by:

- feeding;
- post-exercise substrate shifts;
- different total energy intake;
- different training quality;
- compensatory metabolism.

Therefore BIO-07 retains only:

**fasting can shift acute fuel selection toward fat.**

It does not infer:

**fasting causes greater long-term fat loss.**

Confidence: **HIGH inference boundary**.

---

## 20. Carbohydrate feeding during exercise can coexist with high fat oxidation when glycogen is low

Low glycogen and carbohydrate feeding are not mutually exclusive metabolic states.

PMID **31095946** showed:

- exogenous carbohydrate oxidation remained intact under low-glycogen conditions;
- endogenous carbohydrate oxidation fell;
- fat oxidation rose.

This demonstrates that whole-body substrate use can redistribute among:

- endogenous carbohydrate;
- exogenous carbohydrate;
- fat;

rather than moving on one single carbohydrate-vs-fat axis.

Canonical rule:

**substrate selection requires source-resolved thinking, not only total CHO-vs-fat percentages.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 21. Metabolic flexibility

PASS at content level.

Metabolic flexibility is best defined as the capacity to adjust substrate utilization to changes in:

- substrate availability;
- hormonal/nutritional state;
- energy demand;
- exercise intensity and duration.

Review PMID **29697773** describes metabolic flexibility as adaptation of substrate sensing, trafficking, storage and utilization to energy resources and requirements.

Current exercise review PMID **41881712** describes mitochondrial fuel flexibility as the capacity to switch between lipid and carbohydrate oxidation.

Canonical rule:

**metabolic flexibility = appropriate switching/adaptation of fuel use, not maximal use of one fuel.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 22. Metabolic flexibility ≠ maximal fat oxidation

A metabolically flexible athlete should be capable of:

- high fat oxidation when intensity/substrate state makes that useful;
- rapid carbohydrate upregulation when ATP demand becomes high;
- switching with feeding/fasting;
- adjusting to changing glycogen state;
- recovering toward homeostasis after exercise.

Therefore a very high fat-oxidation rate does not by itself prove superior metabolic flexibility.

Likewise:

- low RER at one workload;
- high Fatmax;
- one fasting RER value;

are not universal standalone definitions of metabolic flexibility.

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual boundary**.

---

## 23. Metabolic-flexibility measurement is not standardized

A 2025 current-opinion paper (PMID **40164840**) notes:

- no consensus exercise protocol;
- different maximal/submaximal protocols;
- stage durations vary;
- data averaging differs;
- stoichiometric equations differ;
- nutritional state and exercise mode materially affect results.

Therefore:

**metabolic flexibility is a physiological construct; any measured “score” is protocol dependent.**

TST will own test-selection and interpretation.

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 24. Measurement boundary — RER / indirect calorimetry

Whole-body substrate oxidation is commonly estimated from:

- VO2;
- VCO2;
- RER = VCO2 / VO2;
- stoichiometric equations.

Under appropriate steady-state aerobic conditions:

- higher RER generally indicates greater carbohydrate contribution;
- lower RER generally indicates greater fat contribution.

But indirect calorimetry has important limitations.

Review PMID **15702454** and current human fuel-use review PMID **40763088** emphasize:

- bicarbonate pool changes at high intensity can invalidate simple substrate equations;
- extra CO2 from acid buffering is not substrate-derived CO2;
- equations differ depending on whether glucose or glycogen is assumed;
- protein oxidation is often ignored;
- whole-body gas exchange is not a direct local-muscle flux measurement.

Canonical rule:

**RER is a useful whole-body substrate-use estimator under suitable conditions, not a universally valid direct measurement of muscle carbohydrate/fat oxidation.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 25. Intensity × duration × availability interaction

A trainer-level model should use three interacting axes:

### Axis A — ATP demand / intensity
Higher ATP flux generally increases carbohydrate reliance.

### Axis B — time / duration
As time passes, local stores are used and plasma/exogenous sources become more important.

### Axis C — substrate availability
Glycogen, feeding/fasting, carbohydrate ingestion and FFA availability shift the available fuel pool.

Training status changes the capacity and regulation through which these three axes are handled.

Canonical model:

**fuel selection = demand × duration × availability × adaptation**, not one isolated variable.

Confidence: **HIGH conceptual synthesis**.

## 26. Practical trainer-level framework

For a given exercise bout ask:

1. What is the absolute work rate?
2. What is the relative intensity for this person?
3. How long has the bout lasted?
4. What was starting muscle/liver glycogen likely to be?
5. Fed, fasted, or carbohydrate-fed during exercise?
6. What is the athlete's endurance-training status?
7. Is the measurement whole-body RER, tracer flux, biopsy, or local muscle data?
8. Is the task steady-state enough for substrate oxidation equations?
9. Are we discussing acute oxidation or chronic body-composition/performance outcomes?

Only after these are specified should “carbohydrate vs fat” language be used quantitatively.

## 27. Important non-equivalences

1. carbohydrate metabolism ≠ anaerobic metabolism.
2. carbohydrate oxidation ≠ blood-glucose oxidation only.
3. muscle glycogen use ≠ total carbohydrate oxidation.
4. endogenous carbohydrate oxidation ≠ exogenous carbohydrate oxidation.
5. lipolysis ≠ fat oxidation.
6. plasma FFA concentration ≠ FFA flux.
7. fatty-acid availability ≠ fatty-acid uptake.
8. fatty-acid uptake ≠ mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation.
9. beta-oxidation ≠ total pathway from adipose triglyceride to ATP.
10. fat oxidation ≠ body-fat loss.
11. Fatmax ≠ universal percentage of VO2max.
12. Fatmax ≠ lactate threshold / ventilatory threshold by definition.
13. low glycogen ≠ zero carbohydrate oxidation.
14. low glycogen ≠ inability to oxidize exogenous carbohydrate.
15. fasted exercise ≠ guaranteed superior chronic fat loss.
16. trained status ≠ greater fat fraction at every matched relative workload.
17. greater fat oxidation ≠ lower absolute carbohydrate flux in every task.
18. protein contribution is small ≠ protein contribution is zero.
19. metabolic flexibility ≠ maximal fat oxidation.
20. one RER value ≠ metabolic flexibility.
21. whole-body RER ≠ direct local-muscle substrate flux.
22. acute substrate selection ≠ chronic adaptation.
23. acute substrate selection ≠ long-term performance outcome.
24. acute substrate selection ≠ body-composition outcome.

## 29. Established content-level findings

### HIGH

1. Carbohydrate and fat are major oxidative fuels during exercise and are used concurrently.
2. Carbohydrate can support both substrate-level glycolytic ATP and oxidative ATP production.
3. Muscle glycogen, blood glucose and exogenous carbohydrate are distinct fuel pools.
4. Lipolysis, fatty-acid availability, uptake, mitochondrial entry and oxidation are distinct processes.
5. Beta-oxidation generates acetyl-CoA and reducing equivalents from fatty acyl-CoA within mitochondrial metabolism.
6. Protein/amino-acid oxidation contributes non-zero but usually subsidiary energy during prolonged endurance exercise.
7. Increasing exercise intensity generally shifts substrate use toward carbohydrate.
8. Absolute fat oxidation commonly peaks at an individual moderate/submaximal intensity and declines at higher intensity.
9. There is no universal Fatmax percentage.
10. Duration changes both macronutrient contribution and the source pool supplying a macronutrient.
11. Starting glycogen materially influences endogenous carbohydrate and fat oxidation.
12. Low muscle glycogen can increase fat oxidation while exogenous carbohydrate oxidation remains available.
13. Feeding/fasting state acutely changes substrate selection.
14. Fasted exercise commonly increases acute fat oxidation compared with carbohydrate-fed exercise.
15. Acute fat oxidation cannot be translated directly into chronic body-fat loss.
16. Metabolic flexibility means adapting/switching substrate use to demand and availability.
17. Metabolic flexibility is not equivalent to maximal fat oxidation.
18. RER is a useful but method-dependent whole-body estimate and becomes problematic in high-intensity/non-steady-state conditions.

### MODERATE-HIGH

19. Endurance training commonly increases relative fat oxidation/reduces carbohydrate reliance at the same absolute workload.
20. Training-status effects at the same relative intensity are heterogeneous and protocol dependent.

## 30. Evidence-ledger candidates

1. Exercise carbohydrate metabolism includes distinct endogenous and exogenous pools; muscle glycogen use is not equivalent to total carbohydrate oxidation.
2. Exercise fat oxidation is a multi-step process; lipolysis and plasma FFA availability are not equivalent to mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation.
3. Beta-oxidation converts mitochondrial fatty acyl-CoA into acetyl-CoA and reducing equivalents; it is not synonymous with whole-body lipolysis or fat loss.
4. Protein/amino acids provide a small but non-zero share of exercise energy in prolonged endurance work, with exact contribution context dependent.
5. Increasing exercise intensity generally shifts substrate use toward carbohydrate; absolute fat oxidation typically peaks at a submaximal intensity and then declines, with no universal Fatmax percentage.
6. Exercise duration changes both macronutrient contribution and source-pool contribution, with plasma-derived fuels becoming more important during prolonged exercise.
7. Endurance training modifies substrate selection, but interpretation requires explicit absolute-vs-relative workload matching.
8. Lower muscle glycogen generally reduces endogenous carbohydrate oxidation and increases fat oxidation without necessarily reducing exogenous carbohydrate oxidation.
9. Fed/fasted state changes acute substrate use; higher fasted fat oxidation does not establish superior long-term fat loss.
10. Metabolic flexibility is the capacity to adapt fuel use to demand/availability; no single RER/Fatmax value is a universal measure.
