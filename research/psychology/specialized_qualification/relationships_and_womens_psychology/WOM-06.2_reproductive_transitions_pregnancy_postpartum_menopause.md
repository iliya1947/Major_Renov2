# WOM-06.2 — Reproductive transitions: pregnancy, postpartum and menopause

Date: 2026-11-26. Status: bounded block COMPLETE_FOR_SPECIFIED_SCOPE (internal review only); WOM-06 IN_PROGRESS.

## 1. Question and dependency gate
Distinguish reproductive-stage indicators, endocrine exposures and sensitivities, symptoms, role transitions, and concurrent psychosocial conditions when interpreting changes in affect and cognition. Reopened WOM-06.1 at the active branch; SPQ-00 and WOM-05.3 were available in preceding context. Apply IND-03 person-specific transfer and WOM-05 role/opportunity distinctions. Scope: selected gestational, postpartum and menopausal evidence; no diagnostic or treatment protocol. Candidate explanations: direct endocrine sensitivity, symptom/sleep pathways, preexisting vulnerability, role and resource changes, ordinary developmental change, measurement and selection. Closure requires longitudinal and experimental evidence, counterevidence and an operational differential inference framework.

## 2. Evidence and access ledger
Public targeted searches on 2026-11-26: postpartum steroid withdrawal experiment; perinatal symptom trajectories; pregnancy cognition meta-analysis and null prospective findings; STRAW+10 staging; SWAN hormones, mood and stress; menopausal-stage depression meta-analysis; randomized estradiol prevention trial; objective peripartum sleep. Selected primary studies and reviews, not a systematic search. No article read end-to-end. Directly opened sections and indexed-only evidence are differentiated below; some PubMed/PMC requests returned CAPTCHA and the ASRM page timed out. No unverified precise estimates are inferred.

E1. Bloch et al. (2000), Effects of gonadal steroids in women with a history of postpartum depression. https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.157.6.924 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/ . Complete author abstract directly inspected. Experimental simulation in 16 euthymic nonpregnant participants, eight with and eight without prior postpartum depression. After ovarian suppression, combined steroid exposure and blinded withdrawal, five of eight in the prior-depression group and zero of eight comparison participants developed significant mood symptoms during withdrawal. This supports differential sensitivity to the manipulated endocrine environment, not the population frequency of hormone-mediated depression. Small selected groups; simulation excludes pregnancy/childbirth and caregiving context, combined exposure cannot isolate a single steroid or receptor mechanism; group membership was not randomized.

E2. Perinatal Trajectories of Maternal Depressive Symptoms in Prospective, Community-Based Cohorts Across 3 Continents (2023). https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2811106 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37883082/ . Publisher abstract, methods, results and limitations sections directly inspected. Seven cohorts, 11,563 participants, repeated EPDS or CES-D measures from pregnancy up to two years after birth. Three broadly stable symptom-level clusters were reported, including among participants exceeding screening cutoffs. The dominant UK cohort contributed 8,704 participants; some cohorts excluded psychotropic medication users. Clustering and sample timing can obscure short-lived and minority onset trajectories. Screening thresholds are not clinical diagnosis. This challenges a universal birth-triggered onset account, but does not refute E1's selected-subgroup effect. Hormones were not the identified cause.

E3. The longitudinal course of depressive symptoms during the perinatal period: A systematic review (2022). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35803394/ . Indexed author abstract only. Studies described episodic trajectories and associations with prior depression, psychosocial stress and socioeconomic or relationship factors. Exact included-study counts, effect precision and risk-of-bias tables not checked. Contrast stable average clusters in E2 with episodic subgroups; different sampling schedules, samples and models may account for divergent descriptions. Cohort overlap unknown; no independent replication claim.

E4. Davies et al. (2018), Cognitive impairment during pregnancy: a meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.5694/mja17.00131 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29320671/ . Indexed publisher and author abstract extracts; direct PubMed open blocked. Twenty studies, random-effects synthesis. Longitudinal estimates for decline from first to second trimester: general cognition SMD 0.29 (95% CI 0.08–0.50), memory 0.33 (0.12–0.54); no significant further decline between second and third trimesters in those analyses. Test battery, comparator choice, heterogeneity and confounding details not independently verified. Group-level objective test change does not establish clinically meaningful impairment or reduced individual reasoning ability, nor does it identify endocrine causation.

E5. Christensen, Leach & Mackinnon (2010), Cognition in pregnancy and motherhood: prospective cohort study. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20118458/ . Indexed author abstract; direct open blocked. No significant overall pregnancy- or motherhood-related differences in cognitive change; late pregnancy was associated with poorer performance on one of four tests. Counterevidence to uniform decline. Exact sample, test-specific estimates and precision not verified here. Not a demonstration of equivalence or exact contradiction of E4's narrower trimester comparisons.

E6. Harlow et al. (2012), STRAW+10 executive summary. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3580996/ ; https://prod.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/executive-summary-of-the-stages-of-reproductive-aging-workshopd10-addressing-the-unfinished-agenda-of-staging-reproductive-aging-2012/ . Indexed consensus-paper and professional-society extracts; direct society page timed out. Staging emphasizes menstrual patterns and supportive endocrine indicators, not chronological age alone. Special circumstances affecting bleeding and ovarian function require attention. This is a staging framework, not an individual mood predictor; exact stage criteria and special-population details not established in this review.

E7. Bromberger et al. (2011), Major depression during and after the menopausal transition: SWAN. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21306662/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3584692/ . Indexed author and publisher extracts. Longitudinal Pittsburgh subsample, 221 women premenopausal at enrollment, ages 42–52. Elevated odds of major depressive episodes during peri/postmenopause persisted after adjustment for recorded prior depression, stress, vasomotor symptoms, medications and annually measured reproductive hormones. Annual hormone sampling cannot resolve rapid fluctuation; residual confounding and mediation remain. No inference that stage is itself a biological mechanism. Exact adjusted odds ratios not verified here.

E8. Depressive symptoms over the final menstrual period: SWAN (2024). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39233250/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/11495996/ . Indexed author abstract and discussion extracts; direct opens blocked. 1,582 participants; symptom trajectories and correlates differed by starting symptom levels. In higher-baseline groups, support and less sleep disturbance were among correlates of declining symptoms; stress and anxiety among correlates of increasing symptoms in low-baseline groups. Hormone measures were also associated with change in some groups. Observational subgroup associations, not identified pathways. E7/E8 share SWAN infrastructure and potentially participants: do not treat as independent corroboration.

E9. The risk of depression in the menopausal stages: A systematic review and meta-analysis (2024). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38642901/ . Indexed abstract; direct open blocked. Reported elevated risk in perimenopause. Exact pooled estimates, postmenopausal comparison and cohort overlap not independently verified; supporting evidence only. Population risk is not individual probability without baseline and calibration.

E10. Gordon et al. (2018), Efficacy of Transdermal Estradiol and Micronized Progesterone in the Prevention of Depressive Symptoms in the Menopause Transition: A Randomized Clinical Trial. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2017.3998 ; https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2668205 . Direct publisher abstract, participant methods, results and discussion extracts. Double-blind placebo-controlled trial of 172 initially euthymic women aged 45–60 during peri/early postmenopause. Over 12 months, CES-D-defined clinically significant symptoms occurred in 17.3% on the combined treatment versus 32.3% on placebo. Stage and recent stressful events moderated some symptom outcomes. Randomization supports an effect of the assigned regimen in the sampled population; it does not identify which component or endogenous physiological mechanism caused the difference. Moderator estimates require replication; outcome is a symptom threshold, not necessarily diagnosed major depression. Treatment had adverse effects. No treatment recommendation.

E11. Longitudinal Changes in Sleep, Biological Rhythms, and Light Exposure From Late Pregnancy to Postpartum and Their Impact on Peripartum Mood and Anxiety (2022). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044728/ . Indexed author abstract describes prospective subjective and objective measures, including two weeks of actigraphy per visit, melatonin and light exposure. Direct open blocked. Used only as evidence for an appropriate multimodal measurement design; specific effects and sample size not verified. No asserted causal direction.

## 3. Integration and differential inference
Pregnancy and childbirth change endocrine physiology and physical state; postpartum recovery, feeding, sleep and caregiving demands have distinct trajectories. Parenthood need not involve pregnancy. Ovarian aging and menopausal stage are distinct from chronological age, social role and actual measured endocrine exposure. Stage-associated symptoms can occur without an established hormone-specific explanation. Circulating concentrations, rates of change, individual sensitivity, subjective symptoms, measured task performance, preferences and observable behavior are different constructs.

Candidate multilevel model: earlier vulnerabilities and resources -> selection/baseline; reproductive transition -> multiple concurrent endocrine, somatic and social changes; context and appraisal interact with physical symptoms and sleep; affect and behavior may feed back into social conditions. This is a hypothesis map, not an identified causal DAG. A sleep variable may be mediator, confounder or outcome depending on timing and estimand. Adjustment for postexposure symptoms does not prove direct biological effect. An experimental regimen can identify its total effect under assumptions but not necessarily natural endocrine mediation.

Differential inference rules:
1. Define precise outcome and timing; use actual prior baseline rather than reconstructing it from a stage stereotype. Distinguish symptom intensity, functional impairment, trait, motivation and preference.
2. Keep reproductive and clinical status unknown unless voluntarily and reliably established. Age or behavior is not a diagnostic measure. Never infer pregnancy or menopause from social cues.
3. Compare endocrine-sensitivity, physical discomfort/sleep, stable vulnerability, changed demands/resources and relationship-specific accounts. Allow interactions. Look for naturally occurring repeated observations and earlier symptoms; no intrusive data collection.
4. Evaluate exposure validity, sampling interval, population selection, time-varying confounding, missingness and outcome measurement. Group clusters are analytic summaries, not fixed personal types.
5. Experimental evidence in selected vulnerable participants and aggregate longitudinal stability can coexist. Do not extrapolate clinical findings to all women or dismiss individual symptoms because of average null results.
6. Separate association with reproductive stage from association with measured hormone levels and identified causal mechanisms. Update only the supported claim. Respect explicit choices and autonomy; no discounting someone's account or consent through biological attribution.
7. Clinical screening, diagnosis and medication decisions require appropriate professional assessment; this block does not establish clinical thresholds or intervention suitability. Serious symptoms warrant appropriate clinical/safety evaluation rather than interpersonal interpretation.

## 4. Internal transfer checks
Rubric: construct separation, competing explanation, distinguishing evidence, inference boundary; critical errors include determinism, causal inflation, unsupported diagnosis, ignoring contradictory evidence, coercion and privacy breach. Self-authored neutral examples, not held-out.

A. In an experimental simulation, a prior-symptom subgroup reacts to steroid withdrawal and comparison participants do not. Response: differentiate exposure from sensitivity; baseline vulnerability and other correlated factors remain possible modifiers; replicate with adequate samples and exposure measures. No population prevalence or individual diagnosis. Four criteria addressed.
B. A cohort has stable average symptom trajectories, but an individual reports new symptoms after birth. Response: average stability does not exclude new onset; assess earlier baseline, symptom timing and concurrent stress/recovery. No assumption of endocrine causation or dismissal of symptoms. Four criteria addressed.
C. Two studies differ on pregnancy-related memory changes. Response: compare actual test domains, sampling windows, reference groups and precision; a null finding is not equivalence. Subjective effort and objective performance remain separate. Four criteria addressed.
D. An older participant reports changing interests and fatigue. Response: age, reproductive stage, health, opportunity and goal value are different; compare voluntary history and actual functional observations. No inferred menopause or loss of competence. Four criteria addressed.
E. A randomized combined treatment reduces symptom-threshold crossings. Response: distinguish assigned intervention effect from natural hormone variation, diagnosis and component mechanisms; evaluate replication, adverse effects and population eligibility. No regimen advice or guarantee. Four criteria addressed.
F. A longitudinal association between disrupted sleep and mood remains after adjustment for stage. Response: sleep may affect mood, mood may affect sleep, and shared causes may affect both; determine timing and estimand, assess repeated data and alternative causal structures. No identified mechanism from adjustment alone. Four criteria addressed.
