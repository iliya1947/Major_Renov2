# REL-01 — Interpersonal and attachment foundations: constructs, dimensional measurement, dyadic interdependence, regulation, partner effects, developmental continuity/change, and inference limits

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Establish the foundational interpersonal/attachment reasoning layer required before studying conflict and communication-specific processes.

The aim is not to assign people attachment "types."

The aim is to reason correctly about:

1. attachment as a relational-regulatory construct;
2. adult attachment anxiety and avoidance as dimensional tendencies;
3. categorical prototypes versus dimensional measurement;
4. global, relationship-specific, trait, and state attachment;
5. self-report versus interview-based attachment measures;
6. actor, partner, and shared couple-level effects;
7. support seeking, responsiveness, dyadic coping, autonomy, and regulation;
8. developmental continuity and change;
9. partner/context effects;
10. limits of causal and individualized inference.

Canonical rule:

`attachment construct -> measurement channel/level -> relationship context -> actor/partner/shared effects -> time scale -> regulation/support process -> alternatives -> narrow inference`.

Never:

`attachment label -> personality essence`

or:

`childhood attachment -> adult destiny`.

# II. Attachment is a regulatory relational construct

## 12. Attachment is not a personality diagnosis

Attachment theory concerns patterns of regulation under conditions of:

- threat;
- vulnerability;
- separation;
- need for support/proximity.

Adult attachment constructs are related to personality and mental health but are not equivalent to either.

## 13. Attachment-related behavior is context-sensitive

The same person may behave differently depending on:

- partner;
- perceived availability;
- stress;
- relationship stage;
- threat intensity;
- recent conflict;
- cultural norms.

Therefore:

`one attachment behavior -> stable attachment type`

is invalid.

## 14. Security is not "never needing reassurance"

Secure attachment does not imply:

- emotional independence;
- no distress;
- no jealousy;
- no conflict;
- no need for support.

It concerns the organization of expectations/regulation around closeness, support, and threat.

## 15. Insecurity is not pathology by itself

Higher attachment anxiety or avoidance:

- can be associated with distress and relationship difficulty;
- does not constitute a mental disorder;
- does not establish dysfunction in every context.

## 16. Attachment language should remain dimensional and functional

Prefer:

- "higher attachment anxiety";
- "higher attachment avoidance";
- "more secure/less secure relationship-specific orientation";
- "attachment-related deactivation/hyperactivation hypothesis."

Avoid identity language unless quoting a validated categorical scheme.

# III. Adult attachment is better represented dimensionally than as natural types

## 17. Common adult self-report dimensions

Modern adult romantic-attachment research commonly uses two dimensions:

1. **attachment anxiety**;
2. **attachment avoidance**.

## 18. Attachment anxiety

Broadly concerns:

- fear of rejection/abandonment;
- uncertainty about partner availability;
- heightened proximity/reassurance concerns under attachment threat.

It is not identical to:

- generalized anxiety;
- neuroticism;
- dependency;
- jealousy.

## 19. Attachment avoidance

Broadly concerns:

- discomfort with closeness/dependence;
- preference for emotional distance/self-reliance;
- deactivation of attachment needs under some contexts.

It is not identical to:

- introversion;
- low empathy;
- lack of love;
- schizoid pathology;
- "emotionless personality."

## 20. Security in the two-dimensional framework

Low attachment anxiety + low avoidance is commonly described as relatively secure.

But:

- security can also be measured directly as a state;
- "low insecurity dimensions" and momentary felt security are not automatically the same measurement target.

## 21. Four-category prototypes are descriptive configurations

A common mapping creates:

- secure;
- preoccupied/anxious;
- dismissing/avoidant;
- fearful

profiles from anxiety/avoidance patterns.

These can be clinically/descriptively useful.

## 22. Categories are not established natural kinds

Taxometric evidence from general and relationship-specific adult attachment found the latent structure more consistent with dimensions than taxa.

Therefore:

`person belongs to one true attachment type`

is too strong.

## 23. Cutoffs create artificial boundaries

If continuous anxiety/avoidance scores are cut into four quadrants:

- people near the cutoff can receive different labels despite very similar scores;
- within-category variation remains large.

This is a psychometric decision, not discovery of four natural populations.

## 24. Latent profiles can recover useful clusters without proving types

Recent profile analyses can identify empirically useful configurations.

A latent profile is a model-dependent summary.

It does not prove discrete biological/psychological kinds.

# IV. Measurement families are not interchangeable

## 25. ECR/ECR-R family

The Experiences in Close Relationships family measures self-reported adult attachment orientations, usually:

- anxiety;
- avoidance.

It is commonly framed around romantic or close relationships.

## 26. ECR-RS

Relationship Structures variants can measure anxiety/avoidance separately for:

- mother;
- father;
- romantic partner;
- friend;
- general attachment.

This explicitly allows relationship-specific variation.

## 27. AAI

The Adult Attachment Interview measures a different target:

- organization/state of mind regarding childhood attachment experiences;
- coherence of discourse/representation.

It is not simply a verbal ECR.

## 28. Self-report and interview methods show limited convergence

A study comparing multiple adult attachment measures found:

- low convergence among categorical measures;
- moderate/high convergence among similar dimensional self-report scales;
- low convergence between dimensional self-report and categorical interview/projective measures.

Therefore:

`AAI category = ECR attachment style`

is invalid.

## 29. Measurement disagreement can be substantive

Possible reasons:

- different target relationship;
- different timescale;
- explicit self-beliefs versus narrative organization;
- state of mind versus romantic orientation;
- method variance.

Do not automatically treat one instrument as wrong.

## 30. Attachment score != attachment essence

It is:

- a model-based measurement of a specified attachment construct;
- under a specified instruction/context;
- with measurement error.

PSYCHOMETRICS controls interpretation.

---

# V. General versus relationship-specific attachment

## 31. Attachment can vary across relationships

A person can show different anxiety/avoidance levels with:

- romantic partner;
- parent;
- friend.

## 32. Global orientation and relationship-specific orientation are different levels

General attachment orientation summarizes a broader pattern.

Relationship-specific orientation captures expectations in a particular relationship.

## 33. One partner-specific pattern should not automatically become a global trait

Example:

high avoidance in one unsafe/unsatisfying relationship

does not prove:

global avoidant attachment.

## 34. Global pattern does not force identical behavior with every partner

Partner responsiveness and context can alter expression.

## 35. Relationship-specific measurement is often preferable for relationship questions

If the question is:

> how someone relates to their current romantic partner,

a current-partner measure is generally more proximal than a global score.

But global scores can still provide useful prior/background information.

---

# VI. Trait and state attachment

## 36. Adult attachment includes relatively stable tendencies

Trait anxiety/avoidance predict average patterns across time and situations.

## 37. Attachment also fluctuates moment to moment

EMA studies demonstrate state:

- security;
- anxiety;
- avoidance

vary within persons over daily life.

## 38. Trait and state are related but not identical

2025 EMA evidence across two samples found trait anxiety/avoidance predicted:

- corresponding state baselines;
- aspects of security level/variability.

But substantial within-person state variation remained.

## 39. State security can affect later insecure states

2026 temporal EMA evidence found state security predicted later decreases in:

- state avoidance;
- state anxiety.

State avoidance also predicted lower later security.

This supports dynamic regulation.

It does not prove one universal interpersonal mechanism.

## 40. One stressful interaction should not be read as trait attachment

A temporary spike in:

- reassurance seeking;
- withdrawal;
- fear;
- distance

may reflect state activation.

Need repeated/contextual evidence before trait inference.

---

# VII. Attachment strategies under threat

## 41. Hyperactivation

Higher attachment anxiety is often theorized to involve:

- intensified monitoring of partner availability;
- proximity/reassurance seeking;
- difficulty settling uncertainty.

## 42. Deactivation

Higher avoidance is often theorized to involve:

- downregulation of attachment signals;
- distancing;
- self-reliance;
- reduced support seeking/disclosure.

## 43. Strategy language is probabilistic

Do not infer:

`anxious person always pursues`

or:

`avoidant person always withdraws`.

Context, partner, stress, and learning matter.

## 44. The same behavior can have different functions

Withdrawal may reflect:

- deactivation;
- overwhelm;
- anger;
- safety strategy;
- depression;
- need for processing time.

Reassurance seeking may reflect:

- attachment anxiety;
- acute uncertainty;
- real partner inconsistency;
- trauma;
- situational stress.

Function must be assessed.

---

# VIII. Attachment and relationship outcomes

## 45. Insecurity is robustly associated with poorer mental-health outcomes

A meta-analysis of 224 studies / 245 samples / 79,722 participants found both attachment anxiety and avoidance associated with:

- more negative mental-health indicators;
- fewer positive mental-health indicators.

Attachment anxiety showed somewhat larger associations on average.

## 46. These are associations, not diagnoses or causal effects

Possible common causes include:

- temperament/personality;
- adversity;
- current relationship stress;
- psychopathology;
- measurement overlap.

## 47. Attachment insecurity is also associated with relationship satisfaction

Recent dyadic/longitudinal work commonly finds higher anxiety/avoidance associated with lower relationship satisfaction.

## 48. Relationship satisfaction is not determined by attachment

Satisfaction also depends on:

- partner behavior;
- stress;
- conflict;
- values/goals;
- health;
- finances;
- sexual/relational fit;
- support;
- life stage.

Attachment is one layer.

---

# IX. Perceived partner responsiveness

## 49. Responsiveness is a key relational construct

It concerns feeling that a partner:

- understands;
- validates;
- cares/responds to needs.

## 50. Insecure attachment can correlate with lower perceived responsiveness

Recent 2026 young-adult evidence found anxiety/avoidance associated with lower perceived partner responsiveness, which in turn correlated with satisfaction.

## 51. Perceived responsiveness is not objective partner responsiveness

It can reflect:

- actual behavior;
- perception bias;
- attachment expectations;
- current mood;
- relationship context.

## 52. Statistical mediation is not mechanism proof

Cross-sectional:

`attachment -> perceived responsiveness -> satisfaction`

cannot establish temporal/causal mediation.

BHC-01 controls this inference.

---

# X. Perception of the partner can be biased

## 53. Attachment can color partner perception

A 2026 dyadic truth-and-bias study found attachment anxiety/avoidance related to biased perceptions of partner attachment characteristics.

## 54. Perception bias and partner truth can coexist

A person's negative perception can be partly biased while the partner also genuinely shows relevant behavior.

Therefore:

`it is all projection`

and:

`perception is purely objective`

are both unsafe.

## 55. Relationship inference requires multiple channels

Useful evidence may include:

- both partners' reports;
- observed behavior;
- diary data;
- longitudinal change;
- context.

One partner's interpretation is psychologically important but not automatically objective description.

---

# XI. Dyadic interdependence

## 56. Couple data are nonindependent

Partners share:

- environment;
- history;
- stressors;
- routines;
- outcomes.

Standard independent-person assumptions can be wrong.

## 57. Actor effect

An actor effect asks:

> Is my characteristic associated with my own outcome?

## 58. Partner effect

A partner effect asks:

> Is my characteristic associated with my partner's outcome, controlling the relevant dyadic structure?

## 59. Dyadic interdependence/shared couple effect

Partners may resemble each other because of:

- selection;
- shared environment;
- mutual influence;
- common stressors.

## 60. Cross-partner correlation != partner causal effect

A 2026 meta-analytic APIM of mindfulness in 12,396 couples found:

- positive cross-partner zero-order correlations;
- but near-zero partner effects after modeling actor/interdependence structure.

This demonstrates a crucial rule:

`partner A variable correlates with partner B outcome -> A causes B outcome`

is invalid.

## 61. Partner effects can be real in other domains

A 2025 meta-analytic APIM of dyadic coping in chronic illness found:

- significant actor effects;
- significant partner effects for relationship satisfaction.

Therefore:

**partner effects are an empirical question, not a default assumption or default null.**

---

# XII. Dyadic coping and co-regulation

## 62. Relationships regulate stress at multiple levels

Partners can:

- seek/provide support;
- coordinate coping;
- share appraisal;
- buffer or amplify stress.

## 63. Dyadic coping is not simply "being supportive"

It includes:

- one partner's coping;
- responses to the other's stress;
- joint coping processes.

## 64. Attachment insecurity can be associated with dyadic-coping difficulty

2024 couple data linked avoidance with less effective dyadic coping in some actor/partner pathways.

## 65. Cross-sectional dyadic coping evidence is not causal mechanism evidence

Need longitudinal/experimental designs to establish:

`attachment insecurity causes poor dyadic coping which causes poor relationship outcome`.

---

# XIII. Autonomy and closeness are not opposites

## 66. Secure relating does not require loss of autonomy

Close relationships can support:

- dependence when needed;
- independence;
- authenticity;
- exploration.

## 67. Recent longitudinal dyadic evidence

Two studies of long-term couples found higher attachment avoidance—and in one study anxiety—associated with lower daily autonomy, which in turn related to lower satisfaction.

## 68. Mediation language remains cautious

These results support a plausible process relation.

They do not establish autonomy as the unique causal mediator of attachment effects.

## 69. Avoidance is not synonymous with autonomy

High avoidance can involve distancing while still being associated with lower experienced relational autonomy.

Therefore:

`avoidant = independent/autonomous`

is conceptually inaccurate.

---

# XIV. Partner buffering and context

## 70. Attachment-related difficulties are not expressed identically with every partner

Partner behavior can:

- buffer;
- amplify;
- redirect

attachment-related responses.

## 71. Daily context can moderate associations

Recent 2026 dyadic diary research found different shared-experience contexts were associated with reduced satisfaction penalties for higher anxiety versus avoidance.

## 72. These are not universal prescriptions

Three dyadic diary studies in one research program do not establish:

- a general intervention algorithm;
- person-specific treatment rule.

## 73. Partner responsiveness can create corrective experiences

Theoretical and correlational evidence supports this possibility.

But:

`good partner -> insecure attachment repaired`

is too strong.

---

# XV. Communication as an attachment-linked process

## 74. Attachment and communication are related but not identical

Higher anxiety/avoidance can be associated with:

- demand/withdraw;
- demand/demand;
- withdraw/withdraw

patterns.

## 75. 2026 longitudinal dyadic evidence

In 263 couples followed for one year:

- attachment insecurity at baseline predicted later dysfunctional communication patterns;
- those patterns were associated with later lower relationship satisfaction;
- stressful life events moderated some pathways.

## 76. This is stronger than cross-sectional correlation

Temporal ordering improves inference.

## 77. It still does not prove a unique causal chain

Remaining alternatives:

- unmeasured third variables;
- reciprocal influence;
- relationship dissatisfaction shaping communication;
- measurement overlap.

## 78. Communication patterns should not be attachment labels

Demand/withdraw can arise from:

- topic structure;
- power;
- stress;
- partner mismatch;
- safety concerns;
- communication habits.

REL-02 will address conflict/communication architecture directly.

# XVI. Developmental continuity

## 79. Early attachment matters but does not determine adult attachment

Classic developmental theory predicts continuity through internal working models and relational learning.

Empirical continuity is moderate/incomplete.

## 80. Meta-analytic stability from infancy to early adulthood is not perfect

A meta-analysis of 127 papers / 21,072 attachment observations found overall attachment-security stability around:

- r ≈ .39.

Stability weakened over longer intervals.

## 81. Very long continuity is limited

In that meta-analysis, stability was not significant for intervals longer than 15 years.

Therefore:

`infant attachment category -> adult attachment category`

is unsafe.

## 82. 2026 prospective 30-year evidence refines continuity

A prospective longitudinal study of 705 participants found early caregiver and friendship quality predicted adult attachment orientations.

Explained variance was generally modest and relationship-specific.

## 83. Early relationships are one source, not destiny

The 2026 data support developmental linkage while also showing substantial variance remains unexplained.

## 84. Relationship experiences across development matter

Friendships, romantic relationships, family relationships, and later experiences can contribute.

---

# XVII. Attachment change in adulthood

## 85. Adult attachment is relatively stable but changeable

Trait-like continuity and plasticity coexist.

## 86. Life events can shift attachment

A multiwave longitudinal study of over 4,000 adults found:

- about half of studied events associated with immediate attachment changes;
- people often moved back toward pre-event trajectory;
- a subset of events produced enduring average changes;
- individual differences in response were substantial.

## 87. Immediate change != enduring change

A breakup or new relationship may produce temporary insecurity/security.

Longer follow-up is needed for trait-change claims.

## 88. Perceived event meaning matters

The same objective event can be experienced differently.

Interpretation related to attachment change in the longitudinal study.

## 89. Change does not imply attachment theory is useless

A system can be:

- structured;
- history-sensitive;
- relatively stable;
- context-sensitive;
- plastic

at the same time.

---

# XVIII. Security priming

## 90. Attachment states can be experimentally shifted in the short term

A 2022 meta-analysis of 120 security-priming studies / 18,949 participants found a positive average effect across affective, cognitive, and behavioral outcomes.

## 91. Priming effect != lasting trait change

Short-term activation of security representations does not establish permanent change in adult attachment orientation.

## 92. Priming evidence supports state plasticity

It strengthens the distinction:

`state attachment != stable trait attachment`.

---

# XIX. Attachment and psychopathology

## 93. Attachment insecurity is transdiagnostic and nonspecific

It correlates with multiple forms of distress.

Therefore:

`high attachment anxiety -> diagnosis X`

is invalid.

## 94. Mental disorder can alter relationship behavior

Depression, anxiety, trauma, personality pathology, substance use and other conditions can influence:

- closeness;
- reassurance;
- withdrawal;
- trust.

## 95. Attachment can be a formulation variable without being a diagnosis

It may help organize hypotheses about:

- support seeking;
- threat interpretation;
- regulation;
- interpersonal expectations.

But diagnosis routes to CLN/NDEV.

# XX. Attachment versus personality

## 96. Attachment anxiety is not neuroticism

They can correlate but differ in relational content and target.

## 97. Attachment avoidance is not introversion

Avoidance concerns attachment-related closeness/dependence.

Introversion concerns broader social/energy tendencies.

## 98. Trait overlap does not eliminate attachment construct

Incremental value depends on:

- outcome;
- measurement;
- context.

## 99. Attachment should not become an all-purpose personality theory

PER retains ownership of broad trait structure.

REL owns close-relationship regulatory patterns.

# XXI. Measurement and cultural boundary

## 100. Attachment measures are not culture-free

Norms about:

- dependence;
- emotional expression;
- family obligation;
- privacy;
- intimacy

vary.

## 101. Scale invariance matters

Cross-cultural mean comparisons require evidence that items function comparably.

## 102. Relationship form matters

Most classic romantic-attachment research disproportionately samples:

- heterosexual;
- Western;
- educated

populations.

Generalization to diverse relationship structures requires caution.

## 103. Categorical labels can be especially culturally misleading

A behavior described as "avoidant" in one norm system may reflect:

- culturally normative reserve;
- context-specific boundaries.

---

# XXII. Power, coercion, and safety override attachment interpretation

## 104. Not every pursuit/withdrawal cycle is symmetric

One partner's withdrawal may be:

- safety-seeking;
- response to coercion;
- conflict de-escalation.

## 105. Attachment theory must not erase harmful behavior

Examples:

- coercive control;
- threats;
- stalking;
- violence;
- severe manipulation

must not be normalized as "anxious attachment" or "avoidant attachment."

## 106. Safety inference precedes relational typology

If there is:

- abuse;
- coercion;
- threat;
- severe risk,

route to SAF/clinical/legal safety reasoning as appropriate.

# XXIII. Actor/partner/shared-level causal architecture

## 107. Within-person actor pathway

Example:

`my anxiety -> my perceived threat -> my behavior -> my satisfaction`.

This requires within-person/longitudinal evidence.

## 108. Partner pathway

Example:

`my avoidance -> partner's experience -> partner outcome`.

Requires dyadic modeling.

## 109. Shared-couple pathway

Example:

`common stress -> both partners' insecurity/low satisfaction`.

Can create apparent crossover effects.

## 110. Reciprocal pathway

Example:

`A behavior -> B response -> A interpretation -> next A behavior`.

This is a feedback system, not one-way trait expression.

## 111. Cross-sectional dyadic association cannot identify direction

Even APIM coefficients are associations unless design supports causality.

---

# XXIV. Interpersonal inference hierarchy

## 112. Level 0 — isolated behavior

Example:

one withdrawal episode.

No attachment inference.

## 113. Level 1 — repeated relational pattern

Repeated proximity seeking/distancing across relevant contexts.

Attachment-related hypothesis becomes plausible.

## 114. Level 2 — dimensional measurement

Validated anxiety/avoidance measure supports measured orientation.

Still not mechanism or history.

## 115. Level 3 — relationship-specific convergence

Self-report + partner/behavioral/diary evidence converge in the target relationship.

Confidence increases.

## 116. Level 4 — longitudinal regulation evidence

Pattern predicts/responds to threat/support over time.

Stronger process inference.

## 117. Level 5 — developmental attachment claim

Requires developmental/longitudinal evidence.

Cannot be reconstructed confidently from adult style alone.

## 118. Level 6 — causal mechanism

Requires stronger causal/experimental or quasi-experimental evidence.

# XXV. High-value failure modes

## Failure A — secure/anxious/avoidant/fearful are natural types

Unsupported as a universal ontology.

Dimensional evidence is stronger.

## Failure B — attachment style = diagnosis

Incorrect.

Adult attachment orientation is not DSM/ICD diagnosis.

## Failure C — one questionnaire = attachment truth

Incorrect.

Measurement target/method matters.

## Failure D — ECR and AAI measure the same thing

Incorrect.

They show limited convergence and different construct targets.

## Failure E — anxiety score = generalized anxiety

Incorrect.

Attachment anxiety is relationship/attachment-specific.

## Failure F — avoidance score = introversion

Incorrect.

Different constructs.

## Failure G — avoidant = independent/autonomous

Incorrect.

Distancing and autonomy are not synonyms.

## Failure H — anxious = needy personality

Reductionistic and unsupported.

Behavior is context-dependent.

## Failure I — one withdrawal episode = avoidant attachment

Incorrect.

Need repeated/contextual evidence.

## Failure J — reassurance seeking = anxious attachment

Not uniquely.

Situational uncertainty and real partner inconsistency matter.

## Failure K — same attachment pattern with every person

Incorrect.

Relationship-specific attachment exists.

## Failure L — trait attachment = current state

Incorrect.

EMA evidence shows within-person fluctuation.

## Failure M — childhood attachment determines adult relationship style

Incorrect.

Continuity is moderate/incomplete and change occurs.

## Failure N — no continuity = attachment theory false

Incorrect.

Probabilistic continuity and plasticity coexist.

## Failure O — life event changes attachment = permanent change

Incorrect.

Many changes regress toward prior trajectory.

## Failure P — security priming = trait change

Incorrect.

Priming demonstrates short-term state malleability.

## Failure Q — cross-partner correlation = partner effect

Incorrect.

Dyadic interdependence/shared context can explain it.

## Failure R — APIM partner coefficient = causal partner influence

Incorrect without causal design.

## Failure S — relationship satisfaction = attachment outcome only

Incorrect.

Many relational/contextual factors matter.

## Failure T — insecure attachment = psychopathology

Incorrect.

It is a transdiagnostic relational dimension, not diagnosis.

## Failure U — relationship conflict = attachment activation by definition

Incorrect.

Conflict has many causes/functions.

## Failure V — partner behavior irrelevant because attachment is internal model

Incorrect.

Attachment expression is relational/contextual.

## Failure W — harmful behavior is merely attachment insecurity

Unsafe.

Power/coercion/safety require separate assessment.

## Failure X — attachment label explains mechanism

Incorrect.

Label describes pattern; mechanism requires evidence.

# XXVIII. Evidence-strength map

## High-confidence practical boundaries

1. adult attachment is not a psychiatric diagnosis;
2. anxiety and avoidance are useful continuous adult attachment dimensions;
3. taxometric evidence favors dimensional over categorical adult attachment structure;
4. categorical prototypes can summarize but should not be reified as natural kinds;
5. ECR/self-report and AAI/interview methods target different attachment constructs and converge only partially;
6. relationship-specific attachment can differ from global orientation;
7. state attachment fluctuates within person;
8. trait and state attachment are related but nonidentical;
9. attachment insecurity is associated with poorer mental-health and relationship outcomes;
10. these associations do not establish diagnosis or unique mechanism;
11. dyadic data require nonindependence modeling;
12. cross-partner correlation does not establish partner effect;
13. partner effects vary by construct/context and must be estimated;
14. attachment-related behavior depends on partner/context/stress;
15. early relationships predict adult attachment only probabilistically;
16. adult attachment can change after life events/relationships;
17. immediate change is not necessarily enduring change;
18. power/safety concerns override attachment labeling.

## Supported but context-sensitive

1. hyperactivating/deactivating strategy models;
2. partner responsiveness as attachment-relevant process;
3. dyadic coping as relational regulatory pathway;
4. attachment-related communication patterns;
5. partner buffering;
6. autonomy as one pathway linking insecurity and satisfaction;
7. security priming as short-term state manipulation.

## Not established as universal

1. four natural adult attachment types;
2. one attachment style across every relationship;
3. one measurement method as ground truth;
4. direct infant-to-adult category transmission;
5. one-way partner causality from dyadic correlations;
6. one attachment mechanism explaining relationship satisfaction;
7. all pursuit/withdrawal as attachment;
8. attachment insecurity as disorder;
9. permanent change from one life event;
10. attachment label as complete relationship formulation.

## Source-boundary note

Adult attachment anxiety/avoidance are treated primarily as dimensions because taxometric evidence favors dimensional structure. Four-category labels are retained only as descriptive/prototype summaries, not natural kinds.

ECR-family self-report measures and AAI-type interview classifications are not treated as interchangeable; published convergence evidence is limited.

The 2026 longitudinal and dyadic studies strengthen temporal/process inference but do not override causal-identification requirements.

APIM distinguishes actor/partner/interdependence associations; APIM coefficients are not automatically causal effects.

The mindfulness APIM meta-analysis is used specifically as a demonstration that bivariate cross-partner association can disappear after dyadic interdependence modeling, not as a general claim that partner effects are absent.

The chronic-illness dyadic-coping meta-analysis is used to demonstrate that partner effects can exist in other domains, not as a universal relationship model.

No source is used to infer any individual's attachment orientation or relationship history.
