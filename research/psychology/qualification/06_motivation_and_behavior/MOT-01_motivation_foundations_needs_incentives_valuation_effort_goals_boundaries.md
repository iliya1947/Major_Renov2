# MOT-01 — Motivation foundations: needs, incentives, valuation, effort, goals, and construct boundaries

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**
Evidence reviewed: **2026-11-19**
Research scope: public population-level science and generic analytical methodology; not individualized psychological inference.

## Purpose

After LEA-01…06 established how prior experience can modify prediction, value, habits and memory, the next dependent question is **why an action is selected, initiated, sustained, abandoned or resumed in a particular context**.

Failure modes to prevent:
- "low action rate = low motivation";
- "high activation = high motivation";
- "wanting = liking = reward learning = action";
- "intrinsic motivation = absence of any external value";
- "one strong motivational drive explains all behavior";
- "effort avoidance = laziness or low willpower";
- "reward magnitude alone determines effort allocation";
- "goal endorsement automatically produces action";
- "a computational value coefficient proves a stable personal need".

## Core scientific rule

**Motivation is a family of interacting processes that orient, energize and maintain or redirect behavior in relation to needs, goals, expected outcomes and costs. It is not a single observed quantity or one neural transmitter.**

Behavioral expression depends on opportunity, knowledge, ability, task difficulty, physical state, competing goals, resource demands and social/environmental context. The same action can follow different motives; the same motive can result in different actions.

## 1. Distinguish learning, valuation, motivation, control and performance

- Learning: changes predictions, associations, strategies, representations or values with experience.
- Valuation: estimates subjective desirability/utility of prospects.
- Motivation: organizes the direction, intensity, persistence and disengagement of action.
- Executive control: implements selections, policies and task rules, sometimes at substantial effort cost.
- Performance: observable output under available conditions.

These concepts overlap but cannot be used interchangeably. A well-learned action may never occur because its outcome has lost value; strong desire may not translate into action because constraints or conflicting values block it.

## 2. Current NIMH RDoC boundary

The current NIMH Positive Valence Systems scaffold distinguishes:
- Reward Responsiveness (including anticipation, response to reward and satiation);
- Reward Learning (including probabilistic/RL, prediction error and habit);
- Reward Valuation (including reward probability, delay and effort).

These are functional research constructs, **not a total taxonomy of human motivation**. Avoid treating all social, moral, defensive or identity-related action as simple positive reward seeking.

NIMH's Effort construct concerns the computation of reinforcer value against perceived physical/cognitive costs; it is not a clinical score of laziness.

## 3. Need versus desire versus incentive versus goal

- Physiological need: relevant homeostatic/resource state, not simply a conscious wish.
- Basic psychological need in Self-Determination Theory (SDT): autonomy, competence, relatedness as theoretically important conditions supporting functioning.
- Desire/want: motivational attraction toward an outcome, cue or action.
- Incentive: an environmental consequence or cue that changes the attractiveness/priority of behavior.
- Goal: represented desired end state or reference condition for action and monitoring.
- Value: context-dependent preference/utility estimate.
- Drive: theory-dependent label for arousal/activation or deprivation; not a universal measured substance.

**A theory's proposed set of "basic needs" is a theoretical construct supported to varying degrees by evidence, not an objectively exhaustive list of every human motive.**

## 4. SDT needs: autonomy, competence, relatedness

Under SDT:
- autonomy: experiencing volition/ownership of action;
- competence: experiencing effectiveness and capacity to influence relevant outcomes;
- relatedness: experiencing connection/belonging with significant others.

Autonomy is **not synonymous with social isolation, unrestricted options, independence from all others, or dominance over others**. External structure can be autonomously endorsed.

Competence is not identical to IQ or actual objective task skill; perceived competence and feedback matter.

Relatedness is not a diagnostic requirement that every person must seek equal social contact.

2024 meta-analysis of interpersonal need supports and 2026 workplace SDT meta-analysis support associations between need-supportive contexts, need satisfaction, autonomous motivation and some outcomes; effects vary by mediator and outcome and do not prove a single universal mechanism.

## 5. Need satisfaction versus need frustration

Lack of satisfaction is not necessarily the same as active thwarting/frustration.

Distinguish:
- neutral absence of choice versus actively coercive constraint;
- low perceived effectiveness versus humiliating/incompetence-inducing feedback;
- low social connection versus active exclusion or rejection.

Studies of need support/thwarting in student populations distinguish these patterns. Whether they constitute separable latent factors depends on measure and sample.

## 6. Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation are not a binary switch

Intrinsic motivation: engagement for inherent interest, enjoyment or satisfaction of the activity.

Extrinsic motivation: engagement for consequences distinct from activity enjoyment. SDT proposes distinct regulatory forms:
- external: rewards, sanctions, demands;
- introjected: internal pressure/contingent self-worth;
- identified: consciously endorsed importance;
- integrated: coherence with broader self-endorsed values;
- intrinsic: inherent interest.

**Extrinsically instrumental action can be highly autonomous** when the goal is personally endorsed. Avoid equating "external consequence" with "not my choice".

Integrated regulation differs from intrinsic enjoyment: an activity can be valuable despite being dull.

## 7. Self-report autonomy and causal inference

A person can endorse a goal after external persuasion or social pressure. Self-reported choice alone does not establish causal autonomy, nor do arbitrary external rewards always eliminate intrinsic motivation.

The effect of rewards depends on how they are perceived (controlling vs informative), task type, expectedness and performance contingency. Domain-specific meta-analyses cannot be generalized without controls.

2024 education SDT intervention meta-analysis supports several need/motivation outcomes but not all equally (relatedness effect not consistently significant). Avoid claiming universal effects across needs and settings.

## 8. Incentive salience: wanting is not liking

Berridge and Robinson distinguish:
- "liking": hedonic impact/pleasure;
- "wanting": incentive salience/attraction or pursuit;
- learning: acquired associations and predictions.

These can dissociate. Strong cue-triggered attraction does not require equally strong hedonic enjoyment.

This theory has compelling animal and convergent human evidence but its terms do not constitute a single direct clinical measurement method.

**Wanting ≠ liking ≠ expectation ≠ action.**

## 9. Dopamine boundary

Mesolimbic dopamine participates in incentive salience, reward learning, effort allocation and action-related processes. It cannot be mapped one-to-one onto pleasure, desire, willingness, reward prediction error or a person's general motivation level.

Dopamine theories from LEA-03 and the incentive-salience account address different aspects of function and can coexist; the correct question is which task and signal are being measured.

Do not infer a specific neural mechanism solely from a questionnaire, behavior frequency or subjective urge.

## 10. Approach/avoidance and positive/negative valuation

Action can be motivated by:
- approach toward a valued outcome;
- escape from an aversive state;
- avoidance of possible future harm;
- prevention of social/ethical loss;
- pursuit of a personally endorsed goal;
- satisfaction of curiosity, affiliation or competence.

Avoidance behavior is not necessarily passive or unmotivated; safety-seeking may require substantial effort.

Instrumental negative reinforcement (removing an aversive stimulus increases responding) is distinct from punishment and from generic negative emotions.

## 11. State-dependent motivation

Internal bodily state, fatigue, sleepiness, satiety, arousal and environmental context can change outcome valuation and action costs.

A stable goal or preference need not yield a stable willingness to act under all states.

Keep state effects separate from putative stable traits; assess repeated behavior across conditions before inferring individual motivational disposition.

## 12. Expected utility is a model, not complete psychology

Simple illustrative option value:

\`V(a) ≈ Σ_o P(o|a,s) × U(o|s,goal) − C(a,s) − opportunity_cost(a,s)\`.

Here probabilities, subjective utility, costs and opportunity costs depend on state/goal representation. This is a schematic decision model rather than a universally validated brain algorithm.

Do not assume:
- utility is always conscious;
- valuation is always scalar;
- probability estimates are accurate;
- costs are purely monetary or effort-based;
- the model's parameters identify stable personal traits.

## 13. Reward probability, delay and effort are separable

Reward valuation can depend on:
- magnitude;
- probability/uncertainty;
- delay and timing;
- physical effort;
- cognitive effort;
- risk;
- identity/features of the outcome;
- present goals and internal state.

A low acceptance rate can reflect a cost of waiting, uncertainty, inability to execute, perceived control or unattractive alternatives—not purely reduced reward sensitivity.

Effort-discounting tasks must control effort–delay confounding; 2024 validation research emphasizes that these can be entangled.

## 14. Cognitive versus physical effort

Physical effort and cognitive effort often have different task demands, ability constraints and costs. Willingness to expend one does not prove willingness to expend the other.

Cognitive effort is not necessarily equal to time spent, objective difficulty, subjective fatigue, pupil size or error rate. Measures can have nonmotivational determinants.

2024 research indicates working-memory capacity can help predict willingness to expend cognitive effort for rewards, warning that ability can masquerade as motivation.

## 15. Expected Value of Control (EVC)

Shenhav, Botvinick and Cohen (2013) formalized a normative account in which the expected payoff of cognitive control is evaluated against how much control must be applied and the cost of effort.

A simplified expression is:

\`EVC(signal) = expected_payoff(signal) − effort_cost(signal)\`.

This is an explanatory/computational framework for control allocation, **not proof that dorsal ACC or any one region literally computes a unique scalar at all times**.

The account complements EXE's control-allocation mechanisms; MOT concerns incentive structure and value, EXE concerns implementation/control processes.

## 16. Opportunity cost of effort

Kurzban et al. (2013) argued that subjective effort may partly reflect opportunity costs—what competing uses of limited processing resources are forgone while doing the present task.

This is a theoretical framework with empirical motivation, not a universal explanation for all fatigue, task disengagement or boredom.

Compare actual available alternatives and context; the same cognitive task can be experienced differently when an appealing alternative is visible.

## 17. Effort is not only a cost

The effort paradox: effort can be aversive when instrumentally costly but can also acquire positive value as a marker of growth, identity, agency, competence or meaningful investment.

Inzlicht, Shenhav and Olivola (2018) reviewed evidence for effort being both costly and valued. An experiment published in 2022 found that effort-contingent reward increased subsequent preference for demanding cognitive tasks.

**High effort seeking is not globally irrational; effort avoidance is not necessarily a moral defect.**

## 18. Boredom and demand avoidance are context dependent

A 2024/2025 published study comparing demanding work with easy or boring alternatives found context-dependent effort and boredom avoidance.

Thus "choosing the more difficult task" may reflect escaping boredom, valuing information or seeking challenge; "choosing the easier task" may reflect cost minimization, uncertainty or competing workload.

Difficulty is only one variable.

## 19. Activation versus direction versus persistence

Distinguish:
- activation/energization: readiness or vigor;
- direction: which action/goal is prioritized;
- intensity: current investment;
- persistence: maintaining pursuit under obstacles;
- disengagement: adaptive stopping/reprioritization.

High motor activity or physiological activation does not imply strong commitment to a specific goal.

Persistence despite declining utility is not automatically adaptive motivation, and disengagement is not necessarily failure.

## 20. Goal formation versus goal implementation

Goal choice, commitment and planning are separate from actual enactment.

Explicit endorsement of a goal can fail to yield action because of cues, opportunity, competing actions, habit, inaccurate forecasting, resource constraints and environment.

2023 meta-analysis of physical activity found a substantial intention–behavior gap; its domain-specific proportions should not be generalized to all human decisions.

## 21. Goal-setting theory and moderators

Specific challenging goals may improve performance under appropriate ability, commitment, feedback and task conditions.

Failure conditions include:
- insufficient skill, opportunity or time;
- excessively narrow performance targets;
- conflicting goals;
- complex tasks requiring learning rather than immediate performance;
- perverse incentives or metric gaming.

Goal difficulty and specificity are not universal prescriptions.

## 22. Implementation intentions

If–then plans specify a cue and response ("if situation X, then I perform Y"). A 2024 meta-analysis of 642 tests found that effects vary with plan structure, situation, outcome and sample.

These plans can link cues to response initiation but **do not guarantee execution or reveal an S–R habit**.

This belongs to action enactment, overlapping with executive control and behavior-change science.

## 23. Multiple goals and opportunity costs

Competing goals can be simultaneously valuable; acting on one necessarily delays or excludes some alternatives.

A decline in effort toward one task may signal increased value of another, not deterioration in general motivation.

Goal hierarchy, switching costs, conflict, temporal horizons and social norms can shape allocation.

Avoid treating all goals as reducible to a single conscious maximand.

## 24. Goal adjustment, persistence and disengagement

A 2025 meta-analytic review examined reengagement and disengagement under goal-striving difficulty. Goal adjustment is context-sensitive and may support adaptation when constraints are genuinely insurmountable.

**Stopping a goal pursuit does not prove weak will or failed self-regulation.** The value of persistence depends on feasibility, opportunity costs, and alternative goals.

## 25. Expectancy-value-control distinction

Separate:
- desirability of outcome;
- belief one can attain it;
- expectancy of action–outcome contingency;
- control over implementation;
- cost of acting;
- currently available alternatives.

Someone may value an outcome and be able to execute a task but not expect the task to influence the outcome. Conversely, someone may expect high efficacy but not care about the result.

Observed nonaction alone cannot identify which variable is low.

## 26. Social and moral motivation

Affiliation, internalized norms, reputation, fairness and identity can motivate action even when immediate monetary or hedonic outcomes are unattractive.

A "reward" explanation is too broad to be informative if every valued consequence is retrospectively redefined as reward.

Define the valuation target and evidence rather than renaming complex motives.

Detailed social, moral and identity mechanisms are deferred to SOC/PER/DEC.

## 27. Time-varying values

Valuation can change with:
- learning about outcomes;
- changing bodily state;
- exposure or satiation;
- updated goals;
- cue-triggered wanting;
- shifting social context;
- alternative opportunities;
- effort and opportunity costs.

Repeatedly observing a different choice does not by itself identify which variable changed. Distinguish state/goal revaluation from changed reward learning in LEA.

## 28. Measurement of motivation

Candidate outcome measures: choice, latency, initiation probability, effort expenditure, time on task, persistence, willingness to pay/work, repeated decisions, self-report, physiological arousal.

Each measure is nonunique:
- latency also reflects motor and executive processes;
- time on task reflects opportunity and task design;
- effort expenditure reflects ability and constraints;
- self-report can mix values, social desirability and retrospective rationalization;
- physiological arousal can reflect stress and other causes.

A measure should specify its operational target and discriminate plausible alternatives.

## 29. Cross-task trait validity

A task-specific effort-discounting slope, questionnaire need score or preference under one incentive scheme is **not automatically a global personal motivational trait**.

Require score reliability, parameter recovery/identifiability if modeling, measurement invariance, test–retest, convergent validity and real-world transfer.

The LEA-03 lesson applies: good model fit and stable within-task parameters do not ensure construct-level generalization.

## 30. Intrinsic motivation and external rewards: false zero-sum assumption

External incentives can increase behavior while sometimes changing interest, perceived control or attribution. Depending on context they may support competence or act as controlling pressure.

It is invalid to assert either:
- all financial/external incentives destroy intrinsic motivation;
- external reward never affects intrinsic interest.

Distinguish short-term performance increase, later intrinsic interest, internalization and goal congruence. Studies have heterogeneous reward contingencies and tasks.

## 31. Psychological needs are not one biological energy reservoir

SDT need satisfaction and laboratory expected-value/control models operate at different descriptive levels. Neither licenses a literal equation:

\`need_satisfaction = dopamine = motivation = cognitive_capacity\`.

Combining them requires explicit causal bridging studies. Metaphors such as "fuel", "resource allocator" and "drive" are heuristics, not direct neural measurements.

## 32. Scientific pluralism without indiscriminate mixing

Different theories target different questions:
- SDT: quality/internalization of motivation and psychosocial context;
- incentive salience: cue-triggered wanting versus hedonic liking;
- expectancy-value: choice among outcomes under probabilities/costs;
- EVC: allocation of cognitive control under benefits/costs;
- opportunity cost: alternative use of limited time/processes;
- goal-setting/implementation intentions: translation of goals into action.

These frameworks need not be merged into one latent mechanism. Require a causal link before claiming one is the biological implementation of another.

## 33. Negative evidence and falsifiable alternatives

Nonaction may reflect:
1. low outcome value;
2. low perceived probability of success;
3. weak perceived causal control;
4. high cognitive/physical effort cost;
5. high opportunity cost;
6. competing goals;
7. deficient cue/plan for initiation;
8. task or ability barrier;
9. contextual constraints;
10. adaptive abandonment or changed priorities.

A useful study manipulates these dimensions separately where possible.

## 34. Motivation Inference Gate — 50 questions

### A. Construct and observation
1. Is the datum action, self-report, intention, valuation or physiological activation?
2. What action and goal are specified?
3. Is motivation being conflated with learning or performance?
4. What evidence identifies the claimed motive?
5. Could the same action have multiple motives?

### B. Needs and regulatory quality
6. Are physiological and psychological needs distinguished?
7. Does autonomy mean volition rather than independence or dominance?
8. Is competence objective ability or experienced effectiveness?
9. Is relatedness need satisfaction separated from amount of social contact?
10. Are need satisfaction and frustration separated?
11. Is intrinsic engagement distinguished from identified/integrated instrumental goals?
12. Are external and introjected regulation separated?
13. Could imposed incentives affect perceived control rather than task value?
14. Is SDT correlation being treated as a universal causal mechanism?

### C. Reward, incentive, and valuation
15. Is reward responsiveness separated from reward learning and valuation?
16. Are wanting, liking and learning distinct?
17. What current outcome identity/goal determines value?
18. Are magnitude and probability separated?
19. Are delay and effort confounded?
20. Does the choice involve risk or uncertainty?
21. Could internal state or satiation change valuation?
22. Are cue-triggered attractions being mistaken for conscious hedonic liking?
23. Is dopamine being reduced to one motivational substance?

### D. Effort and control
24. Is effort physical or cognitive?
25. Is effort objective demand, perceived cost or actual expenditure?
26. Could task ability masquerade as low motivation?
27. Are alternative activities and opportunity costs controlled?
28. Could boredom or interest explain demand seeking/avoidance?
29. Could effort itself acquire positive value?
30. Is the model of control cost normative rather than established circuitry?
31. Are stress, fatigue and arousal alternative explanations?
32. Is persistence adaptive under the current goal and constraints?

### E. Goals and implementation
33. Is the outcome valued but control over it low?
34. Is intention distinguished from execution?
35. Is there a precise implementation cue?
36. Could conflicting goals explain nonaction?
37. Could goal hierarchy explain priority shifts?
38. Is goal difficulty appropriate to skill and task complexity?
39. Is feedback available?
40. Could goal adjustment/disengagement be adaptive?

### F. Measurement and inference
41. Are behavior, intention and task performance separately recorded?
42. Does the measure actually track the claimed motivational construct?
43. Is its reliability known?
44. Are model parameters recoverable and identifiable?
45. Does the construct generalize across tasks and states?
46. Have context and opportunity been controlled?
47. Is a population average being treated as a universal person trait?
48. Are competing motivational and nonmotivational causes compared?
49. What intervention or test would discriminate the leading mechanisms?
50. What is the narrowest warranted inference?

## 35. Failure modes

A. Nonaction -> no motivation: **invalid without opportunity/ability/value/control checks**.

B. Task avoidance -> laziness: **unsupported moral attribution**.

C. High activation -> strong goal pursuit: **invalid conflation**.

D. Wanting -> pleasure: **invalid; incentive salience and hedonic impact dissociate**.

E. Dopamine -> pure motivation/pleasure: **invalid reduction**.

F. Autonomous -> intrinsically enjoyable: **invalid; endorsed instrumental motives can be autonomous**.

G. Extrinsic -> coerced: **invalid; regulation has multiple forms**.

H. Strong goal -> certain implementation: **intention–behavior gap**.

I. High effort -> high motivation trait: **invalid without ability/difficulty/context controls**.

J. Choosing high demand -> effort always desirable: **invalid generalization**.

K. Choosing low demand -> stable low effort capacity: **invalid without alternatives/cost control**.

L. Need satisfaction -> direct neural power: **cross-level category error**.

M. Reliable discounting parameter -> domain-general motivation: **invalid without cross-task validity**.

N. Stopping pursuit -> poor self-regulation: **goal adjustment may be adaptive**.

O. One SDT result -> all motivational mechanisms: **theory overextension**.

## Canonical rule

**Observation -> goal/need and regulation quality -> outcome valuation and controllability -> cost, effort and alternatives -> goal implementation and competing actions -> task constraints -> measurement validity -> warranted motivational inference.**

Never: **not acting -> not motivated; wanting -> liking; dopamine -> pleasure; high effort -> trait; extrinsic -> coerced; goal -> behavior; cost model -> unique neural implementation**.
