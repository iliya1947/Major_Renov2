# 2. Canonical longitudinal qualitative principle

Longitudinal qualitative research is not defined merely by collecting qualitative data more than once.

Its core target is:

**how experience, meaning, identity, practice, behavior, context or social process changes, remains stable, or is reinterpreted through time.**

A strong longitudinal qualitative architecture contains three aligned elements:

1. a longitudinal research question;
2. data collected across multiple time points, waves or evolving stages;
3. analysis that explicitly treats time, change and continuity as scientific objects.

### Critical rule

Repeated interviews + ordinary pooled thematic analysis does not automatically equal longitudinal qualitative research.

---

# 3. Time must be designed into the study

LQR differs from ordinary qualitative research because time is not incidental metadata.

Time can be operationalized as:

- fixed chronological intervals;
- intervention phases;
- disease/recovery stages;
- life transitions;
- event-triggered points;
- participant-defined milestones;
- continuous/diary sampling;
- fieldwork waves.

### Project rule

For every longitudinal qualitative paper record:

1. what change was expected?;
2. what temporal unit mattered?;
3. why these time points/events were chosen?;
4. how time entered analysis?;
5. whether time was chronological, event-based, developmental or process-based.

---

# 4. No universal minimum study duration

Methodology literature does not support a fixed rule such as:

> longitudinal qualitative research must last at least 6 months.

Two or more points are necessary for direct observation of change, but duration should fit the process being studied.

Examples:

- acute postsurgical adaptation may change over weeks;
- physical-activity maintenance may require months;
- athletic identity reconstruction may require years.

### Rule

Duration is determined by the temporal scale of the phenomenon, not by a universal calendar threshold.

---

# 5. Repeated interviewing vs longitudinal inquiry

Repeat interviews can be used for several different purposes:

- build rapport;
- deepen one topic;
- clarify prior answers;
- reduce one-session burden;
- collect retrospective accounts in multiple sessions;
- prospectively study change.

Therefore:

> repeated interviewing ≠ automatically LQR.

A 2024 methodological paper explicitly distinguishes high-intensity repeated interviewing from prospective longitudinal designs.

### Project router

Classify:

- repeat interviewing for depth only;
- retrospective/quasi-longitudinal repeated interviewing;
- prospective longitudinal interviewing;

separately.

---

# 6. Prospective longitudinal qualitative design

Prospective LQR follows participants, groups or settings as events unfold.

Advantages:

- less dependence on long-range retrospective reconstruction;
- changing expectations can be observed before outcomes are known;
- turning points can be captured near occurrence;
- within-person change can be traced;
- uncertainty itself can be studied.

### Fitness example

Athlete interviewed:

- within one month of major injury;
- during early rehabilitation;
- during sport-specific rehabilitation;
- at return to training;
- at return to competition/performance.

This captures recovery as an unfolding process rather than asking later for one reconstructed story.

---

# 7. Retrospective qualitative temporality

A one-time interview can still contain a strong temporal account.

Participants can reconstruct:

- before;
- transition;
- after;
- turning points;
- changing identity.

This can support retrospective process/narrative analysis.

But it is not the same as observing the person's changing account prospectively.

### Critical distinction

Retrospective temporal narrative:
> present-day reconstruction of prior change.

Prospective LQR:
> repeated access to experience while change is unfolding.

---

# 8. Quasi-longitudinal retrospective designs

Repeat interviews conducted over a short period can improve:

- trust;
- nuance;
- reflexivity;
- completeness.

They may also reconstruct dynamic processes retrospectively.

The 2024 repeat-interview methodology paper calls such designs quasi-longitudinal where they look backward across a longer life/process period from a short data-collection window.

### Project rule

Do not overclassify short repeated retrospective interviewing as prospective LQR.

---

# 9. Entity followed through time

A major 2022 method study found longitudinal qualitative research can follow:

1. individuals;
2. dyads or individual cases;
3. groups;
4. settings.

### Individual

Same person followed repeatedly.

### Dyad/case

For example:
athlete + caregiver or athlete + clinician.

### Group

Team, family or cohort followed as a social unit.

### Setting

Clinic, intervention program, gym or organization followed over time even if individual members change.

### Critical rule

Longitudinal continuity can exist at different analytic levels.

Same individual at every wave is essential for person-level trajectory inference, but not for every setting-level longitudinal question.

---

# 10. Qualitative panel / cohort structure

A qualitative panel follows the same participants across repeated waves.

A qualitative cohort can follow a group sharing:

- starting event;
- intervention exposure;
- diagnosis;
- career stage;
- rehabilitation phase.

### Example

Twenty-nine older adults from one physical-activity RCT interviewed at 6, 12 and 24 months form a longitudinal qualitative cohort embedded in the trial.

### Rule

Qualitative panel/cohort does not imply statistical representativeness.

The sampling/inference remains qualitative unless a probability-sampling design independently supports population inference.

---

# 11. Recurrent cross-sectional analysis

Recurrent cross-sectional analysis compares qualitative findings at distinct time points at the sample/group level.

The participants at each time point can be:

- the same;
- partly overlapping;
- different.

Typical question:

> How did group-level views differ before vs after implementation?

Strength:

- good for time-point comparison;
- usable when cohort retention is impossible;
- can follow changing settings.

Limitation:

- weak for individual trajectory inference.

### Critical rule

Group-level difference across waves is not the same as within-person change.

---

# 12. Trajectory analysis

Trajectory analysis follows the same individual or small social unit across time.

Core target:

> how this person's/case's experience, meaning or process changed.

Typical tools:

- case summaries;
- longitudinal matrices;
- timelines;
- pen portraits;
- within-case comparisons;
- trajectory maps.

### Canonical logic

person/case at T1
→ T2
→ T3
→ turning points / continuity / divergence
→ cross-case trajectory comparison.

### Rule

Trajectory analysis requires enough continuity in cases to trace change.

---

# 13. Recurrent cross-sectional vs trajectory

| Axis | Recurrent cross-sectional | Trajectory |
|---|---|---|
| Main target | difference between time points | change within person/case |
| Sample | same or changing | same person/case required |
| Main unit | whole sample/subgroups | person, dyad, family, case |
| Strength | group-level comparison | lived/process trajectory |
| Main risk | snapshot series mistaken for change process | attrition and very large case-level data burden |
| Typical output | themes at T1 vs T2/T3 | trajectory patterns/turning points |

### Project rule

Both can be longitudinally informative, but they support different claims.

---

# 14. Serial cross-sectional vs genuinely through-time analysis

A weak longitudinal pattern is:

- analyze T1 separately;
- analyze T2 separately;
- report different themes;
- call the result change.

A stronger through-time analysis asks:

- what changed?;
- what stayed stable?;
- when?;
- in whom?;
- in what sequence?;
- under what context?;
- what turning points mattered?;
- how did earlier states shape later ones?

### Critical rule

A sequence of snapshots is not automatically a process explanation.

---

# 15. Cross-sectional + longitudinal analysis can be combined

Complex LQR often needs both:

## Cross-sectional strand

What patterns exist across participants at T1, T2 and T3?

## Longitudinal strand

How did each participant/case evolve?

Then:

> compare trajectory types across cases.

### Example

At week 24 and week 36, PA-maintenance participants may be classified into:

- sustainers;
- disengagers;
- fluctuating participants;

while still preserving individual case histories.

---

# 16. 2023 time/change typology

A 2023 methodology study showed that papers using longitudinal qualitative data vary in how strongly they actually analyze time/change.

Useful project distinction:

1. longitudinal data only;
2. partial longitudinal analysis;
3. recurrent cross-sectional comparison;
4. sequence-of-events presentation;
5. case/trajectory change;
6. group-level change;
7. process-focused longitudinal explanation.

### Project rule

Do not treat every paper with repeated qualitative data as equally longitudinal.

Extract the **strength of temporal integration**.

---

# 17. Fixed-clock sampling

Examples:

- baseline;
- 3 months;
- 6 months;
- 12 months.

Advantages:

- standardized comparison;
- easier scheduling;
- aligns with trials.

Risks:

- the meaningful event may happen between waves;
- participants move through processes at different speeds.

### Example

Return to sport does not occur at the same month for every athlete.

---

# 18. Event-contingent sampling

Interview timing can be linked to milestones:

- surgery;
- first pain-free session;
- return to running;
- return to team training;
- competition;
- relapse;
- program completion.

Advantages:

- captures change near meaningful transitions;
- aligns time with lived/process events.

Risks:

- different participants contribute at different clock times;
- analysis becomes more complex.

### Project rule

Record both calendar time and process/event time where relevant.

---

# 19. Adaptive timing

Calman et al. recommend considering meaningful turning points instead of relying only on fixed intervals.

The 2026 elite-athlete injury study scheduled later interviews partly according to each athlete's upcoming rehabilitation milestones.

### Fitness implication

Event-based timing can be more informative than interviewing every athlete at identical weeks if rehabilitation trajectories differ markedly.

---

# 20. Serial interviews

Serial interviews are one of the most common LQR collection methods.

Later interviews can:

- revisit earlier claims;
- ask what changed;
- explore emerging themes;
- test prior interpretations;
- follow new events.

### Critical rule

Later interviews should not simply repeat the first interview guide unchanged unless the scientific question specifically requires fixed repeated comparison.

---

# 21. Iterative interview guides

A strong LQR design often combines:

## Stable core questions

Enable comparison across time.

## Adaptive questions

Follow:

- new experiences;
- previous statements;
- turning points;
- contradictions;
- emerging theory.

### Rule

The project should record what remained stable and what changed in the interview guide.

---

# 22. Participant feedback to prior interview

Researchers may summarize a previous interview and ask:

- is this still accurate?;
- what changed?;
- why?;
- what do you now see differently?

This can improve longitudinal focus.

But it can also shape the participant's later account.

### Critical rule

Prior-summary prompts are both:

- data-generation tools;
- potential sources of reactivity/co-construction.

Record them.

---

# 23. Diaries and event sampling

Longitudinal qualitative data can also come from:

- diaries;
- voice notes;
- experience sampling;
- photographs;
- training logs;
- digital traces;
- repeated observations.

These can reduce recall gaps and capture micro-change between major interviews.

### Fitness example

Training diary + monthly interview can show:

- actual disruptions;
- changing motivation;
- injury concerns;
- return-to-training milestones.

---

# 24. Longitudinal observation / ethnography

Longitudinal qualitative research is not interview-only.

Repeated or sustained observation can examine:

- changing team culture;
- coaching practices;
- program implementation;
- gym routines;
- rehabilitation interactions.

### Rule

Longitudinal ethnography retains ethnographic methodology while adding explicit temporal/change architecture.

---

# 25. Longitudinal phenomenology

Longitudinal IPA/phenomenology asks how lived meaning evolves.

The 2026 LIPA methodology paper emphasizes:

- temporal scope;
- iterative interviews;
- individual and group trajectories;
- attrition;
- ethics;
- large-data management.

### Project rule

Longitudinal is a temporal layer.

IPA/phenomenology remains the parent methodology.

---

# 26. Longitudinal narrative inquiry

Narrative research can follow how stories/identities are revised across time.

The 2026 ACL recovery study used serial interviews from injury through self-defined rehabilitation goals.

Narrative analysis examined:

- story structure;
- tone;
- positioning;
- context;
- evolving identity.

### Critical distinction

Narrative change:
> changed story/meaning.

Objective physical change:
> separate clinical/quantitative question.

---

# 27. Longitudinal grounded theory

Grounded theory can use repeated data to refine process theory.

Longitudinal architecture can improve access to:

- transitions;
- sequences;
- reversals;
- changing conditions.

But full GT still requires its own core:

- comparison;
- memoing;
- theoretical sampling;
- category/theory development.

Repeated interviews alone do not create GT.

---

# 28. Embedded longitudinal qualitative studies in trials

Qualitative components can be nested within RCTs or other intervention studies.

Example:

REACT RCT:
- randomized parent trial;
- intervention-arm qualitative sample;
- interviews at 6, 12 and 24 months;
- Framework Analysis;
- changing motivational needs examined through time.

### Canonical representation

parent RCT
+
longitudinal qualitative component
+
sampling from trial participants
+
qualitative methodology/analysis
+
integration purpose.

### Critical rule

Randomization of the parent trial does not randomize qualitative interpretations.

---

# 29. Longitudinal process evaluation

Longitudinal qualitative methods are well suited to:

- implementation change;
- mechanism experience;
- adaptation;
- sustainment;
- context shifts.

But process evaluation remains a **purpose layer**.

A longitudinal qualitative process evaluation can be:

- QD;
- case study;
- ethnography;
- framework-based;
- mixed-method component.

---

# 30. Attrition

Attrition is a central LQR challenge.

Participants may leave because of:

- burden;
- illness;
- injury;
- relocation;
- loss of interest;
- intervention dropout;
- recovery;
- competing demands.

### Methodological problem

If people with certain trajectories disappear, later-wave findings may overrepresent:

- adherent participants;
- healthier participants;
- successful recoveries;
- highly engaged participants.

### Rule

Attrition can distort the observed trajectory sample even when statistical bias terminology is not the primary analytic framework.

---

# 31. Attrition can itself be data

Dropout from a study may sometimes be related to the phenomenon.

Example:

A participant who disengages from an exercise program may also become difficult to retain in qualitative follow-up.

### Critical rule

Do not automatically treat all dropout as analytically meaningful.

Researchers need evidence before interpreting reasons for attrition.

Record:

- who was lost?;
- when?;
- known reason?;
- relation to target process?;
- how analysis handled incomplete trajectories?

---

# 32. Oversampling vs trajectory integrity

Researchers may recruit more participants initially to offset expected attrition.

This can protect overall sample size.

But it does not replace missing within-person trajectories.

### Rule

Oversampling solves:

> fewer final participants.

It does not fully solve:

> selective loss of particular trajectory types.

---

# 33. Changing samples

Recurrent cross-sectional studies can legitimately use different participants at different waves.

Trajectory studies cannot make within-person change claims if the person was not followed.

### Example

T1:
10 athletes.

T2:
10 different athletes.

This can show:
> how group-level discourse differs between periods.

It cannot show:
> how those original athletes changed.

---

# 34. Missing waves

A participant may contribute at:

- T1;
- miss T2;
- return at T3.

Possible handling:

- retain partial trajectory;
- analyze T1→T3;
- exclude from specific interval analysis;
- supplement with diary/records.

### Project rule

Record missing-wave handling explicitly.

Do not silently treat uneven observation as complete trajectory data.

---

# 35. Analysis matrices

Useful structures include:

## Time-by-case matrix

Rows:
participants/cases.

Columns:
T1, T2, T3.

## Theme-by-time matrix

Rows:
themes.

Columns:
time points.

## Trajectory map

Chronological or event-based case representation.

## Pen portrait

Condensed case-level narrative integrating multiple sources through time.

### Rule

Matrix techniques organize data; they do not themselves define the parent methodology.

---

# 36. Pen portraits

Pen portraits synthesize longitudinal evidence from multiple sources into a coherent case-level account.

Useful for:

- interviews;
- observations;
- diaries;
- clinical/context data.

They can preserve:

- context;
- chronology;
- contradictions;
- trajectory.

Risk:

- over-smoothing complexity into one neat narrative.

### Rule

Maintain traceability back to original data.

---

# 37. Change, continuity and oscillation

Longitudinal qualitative analysis should not assume monotonic progress.

Possible patterns:

- improvement;
- decline;
- stability;
- oscillation;
- relapse;
- turning point;
- temporary change;
- context-dependent reversal.

### Fitness relevance

Exercise adherence often follows:

engagement → disruption → partial return → new routine,

not a simple linear increase.

---

# 38. Change at multiple levels

LQR can study:

## Within-person change
motivation, identity, experience.

## Between-person divergence
different trajectory types.

## Group change
shared views across waves.

## Setting/system change
implementation, culture, workflow.

## Context change
policy, staffing, season, life events.

### Rule

Specify the level of change before drawing conclusions.

---

# 39. Stability is a valid longitudinal finding

No change can be scientifically important.

Examples:

- persistent fear despite physical recovery;
- stable exercise identity despite interruption;
- unchanged organizational barriers after implementation effort.

### Critical rule

Longitudinal analysis should examine continuity as actively as change.

---

# 40. Temporal ordering

Prospective LQR can strengthen understanding of sequence.

Example:

reported loss of confidence
→ reduced participation
→ withdrawal from team interaction.

This can support a process hypothesis.

### Critical causal boundary

Temporal ordering is necessary for many causal explanations but is not sufficient for causal identification.

LQR can support:

- process explanation;
- perceived mechanism;
- hypothesis generation;
- temporal plausibility.

It does not estimate causal effects by itself.

---

# 41. Causality language

Some LQR methodology sources describe studying causes/consequences of change.

For this project, use calibrated wording.

Preferred:

- participants attributed change to X;
- X preceded Y in observed accounts;
- data support a plausible process linking X and Y;
- longitudinal evidence strengthens temporal/process interpretation.

Avoid unsupported:

- X caused Y;
- causal effect established;
- mediator proven.

---

# 42. Researcher-participant relationship over time

Repeated contact can increase:

- trust;
- disclosure;
- nuance;
- contextual understanding.

It can also increase:

- dependency;
- role ambiguity;
- participant expectations;
- researcher emotional involvement;
- reactivity.

### Rule

Relationship change is a methodological variable, not only an ethical issue.

---

# 43. Process consent

Consent in long projects should be treated as ongoing.

Participants may need repeated opportunities to:

- confirm participation;
- skip topics;
- pause;
- withdraw;
- reconsider use of sensitive data.

### Ethical risk

Long familiarity can make withdrawal feel socially difficult.

### Project extraction

Record whether consent was revisited and how participant autonomy was protected.

---

# 44. Confidentiality across time

Repeated detailed accounts can increase re-identification risk because:

- life events accumulate;
- trajectories become distinctive;
- organizations/sites may be recognizable.

### Rule

Longitudinal qualitative reporting may require stronger masking than a one-time interview study.

---

# 45. Research burden

LQR can produce very large data volumes.

Burden includes:

- participant time;
- repeated contact;
- transcription;
- case tracking;
- analysis;
- researcher emotional load.

### Rule

More waves are not automatically better.

Each time point should answer a temporal need.

---

# 46. Fitness/exercise exemplar: elite injury trajectory

A 2026 prospective longitudinal phenomenological study followed five elite athletes with major injuries through rehabilitation and return-to-sport stages, generating 31 interviews.

Methodological value:

- captured within-athlete change;
- identified non-linear recovery;
- linked different needs to different rehabilitation phases;
- preserved individual variability.

This supports event/stage-based qualitative timing in sport rehabilitation.

A 2026 Physical Therapy study followed four ACL reconstruction patients using serial interviews:

- around injury;
- pre-surgery;
- early post-surgery;
- every 2 months until self-defined rehabilitation goals.

Narrative analysis showed changing:

- identity;
- confidence;
- meaning;
- expectations.

Methodological lesson:

fixed and milestone-based timing can be combined.

---

# 48. Fitness/exercise exemplar: sustaining physical activity

A 2026 qualitative longitudinal study followed people after a joint-pain exercise/education program at weeks 12, 24 and 36.

It examined:

- transition from supported to self-directed activity;
- sustained vs non-sustained PA trajectories;
- evolving behavioral determinants.

Methodological lesson:

longitudinal qualitative evidence can identify changing support needs across maintenance stages, but does not by itself establish intervention effectiveness.

# 52. Confidence

## HIGH

Well-supported:

- LQR requires time/change to be part of question, data architecture and analysis;
- repeated interviews alone are insufficient;
- prospective and retrospective temporal designs differ;
- recurrent cross-sectional and trajectory analyses support different claims;
- same-case continuity is required for individual trajectory inference;
- meaningful timing can be fixed or event-contingent;
- attrition is a major methodological issue;
- longitudinal qualitative studies can be nested in RCTs/interventions without inheriting causal-effect inference;
- LQR improves access to process, transitions, stability and changing meaning but does not identify causal effects by itself.

## MODERATE-HIGH

Project routing choices:

- exact threshold separating strong LQR from partial longitudinal qualitative analysis;
- classification of quasi-longitudinal repeated retrospective interviews;
