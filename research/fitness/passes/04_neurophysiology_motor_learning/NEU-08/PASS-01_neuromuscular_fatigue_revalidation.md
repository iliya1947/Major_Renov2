## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a sufficiently accurate neural/sensorimotor fatigue model for later exercise technique, monitoring, programming, recovery and individualization without:

- treating central fatigue as mental fatigue;
- treating central fatigue as the only cause of task failure;
- treating one voluntary-activation estimate as a direct percentage of CNS capacity;
- treating surface EMG amplitude as a direct measure of neural drive or fatigue;
- assuming motor-unit firing rates uniformly decrease with fatigue;
- assuming recruitment simply “turns off” as fatigue progresses;
- assuming coordination changes have one universal direction;
- assuming proprioception always fails globally under fatigue;
- treating any fatigue-related kinematic change as automatic technical failure or injury risk;
- assigning one recovery clock to force, voluntary activation, contractile function, soreness and subjective fatigue.

### Question type

Acute human neuromuscular/sensorimotor fatigue during and after resistance or force-production tasks.

### Population/context

Healthy adults are the main transfer target. Evidence from endurance, BFR, repeated eccentric exercise or other paradigms is used only when it clarifies mechanisms and is labeled as protocol-specific rather than generalized to all resistance training.

### Critical outcomes

- central vs peripheral fatigue;
- voluntary activation;
- motor-unit discharge/recruitment;
- intermuscular/corticomuscular coordination;
- proprioceptive accuracy;
- movement kinematics/kinetics;
- task failure/performance decrement;
- recovery of neural, contractile and performance function.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

All 10 exact Section 8 items must be covered at content level. Central/peripheral fatigue must remain distinct but interacting; VA/EMG boundaries from NEU-05 must be preserved; motor-unit and coordination changes must remain task specific; proprioception findings must distinguish direct sensory measures from broader balance/motor outcomes; technique changes must not be equated with injury; recovery must remain outcome specific.

## 4. Core neuromuscular-fatigue model

Upstream PHY-10 remains authoritative:

> fatigue = task-specific reduction in sustainable performance capacity.

For NEU-08, the neural/sensorimotor model is:

`descending voluntary drive`
↔
`spinal/motoneuron output + afferent feedback`
↔
`peripheral contractile state`
+
`task mechanics / coordination / sensory state`
→
`momentary force / velocity / power / steadiness / technique`.

During fatigue, the system compensates until task demands can no longer be met.

Project rule:

**neuromuscular fatigue is an interaction between central drive, afferent feedback, motor-unit control and peripheral contractile impairment; no single layer universally explains task failure.**

---

## 5. Central fatigue

For project use, central fatigue is retained as an exercise-induced reduction in the capacity to generate effective voluntary neural drive/activation upstream of the neuromuscular junction.

This can involve:

- supraspinal drive;
- cortical inhibition/excitability;
- spinal/motoneuron excitability;
- altered afferent feedback influence;
- motivational/perceptual constraints on voluntary output.

But central fatigue is **not**:

- mental fatigue;
- subjective tiredness;
- a single neurotransmitter state;
- surface-EMG decline;
- one VA number.

The 2026 central-peripheral review (PMID 42551098) emphasizes bidirectional central-peripheral interaction rather than a one-way “brain shuts down muscle” model.

Canonical rule:

**central fatigue is a location/functional class of impaired voluntary drive, not a single mechanism.**

Confidence: **HIGH for construct; MODERATE for exact site attribution in a given task.**

---

## 6. Central fatigue ≠ mental fatigue

Mental fatigue is a distinct construct usually induced by prolonged cognitive effort.

The 2026 RCT-only meta-analysis (PMID 42168782) found a small-to-moderate reduction in resistance-exercise volume after mental-fatigue protocols.

This demonstrates that cognitive state can impair resistance performance.

It does **not** mean:

`mental fatigue = central neuromuscular fatigue`.

A person can have:

- mental fatigue with little measurable change in VA;
- neuromuscular central fatigue without prior cognitive loading;
- both simultaneously.

Project rule:

**keep mental/cognitive fatigue and neuromuscular central fatigue conceptually separate even when both affect performance.**

---

## 7. Peripheral fatigue boundary

Peripheral fatigue remains owned mechanistically by PHY-10.

Relevant sites include:

- sarcolemmal/T-tubule excitability;
- excitation-contraction coupling;
- SR Ca²⁺ release;
- myofilament sensitivity;
- cross-bridge force production;
- metabolic/ionic state.

The 2025 RT fatigue systematic review (PMID 40644670; 51 studies) found that traditional resistance-training fatigue often affects contractile function, afferent/perceptual responses and voluntary performance more consistently than muscle-activation metrics.

Canonical rule:

**a large performance decrement can occur without a large measurable central-activation decrement.**

This is closure-critical because peripheral fatigue can dominate while voluntary drive remains relatively preserved.

---

## 8. Voluntary activation under fatigue

VA can decrease during/after fatiguing exercise, but this is not universal.

Examples:

- heavy resistance/jump/sprint work produced protocol-dependent VA reductions lasting up to 24–48 h in PMID 30067591;
- low-load BFR protocols produced a mean VA decrease in PMID 40590944;
- other RT/concurrent protocols showed contractile impairment without significant VA change (PMID 34652335).

Measurement limits are important.

The central-fatigue measurement review (PMID 33966110) questions whether ITT-derived VA under fatiguing conditions can be interpreted as a reliable quantitative measure of central fatigue.

The 2026 dorsiflexor study (PMID 42709149) showed that ITT can substantially overestimate VA even before applying the additional complications of fatigue.

Canonical rule:

**fatigue-related VA decline supports reduced voluntary activation in that muscle/protocol, but unchanged VA does not prove absence of all central fatigue and VA is not a universal CNS-capacity percentage.**

Confidence: **HIGH for measurement boundary; MODERATE-HIGH for protocol-dependent VA decline.**

---

## 9. Motor-unit firing during fatigue

A simplistic model would predict:

`fatigue → all motor-unit firing rates decrease`.

The evidence does not support this universally.

Observed responses include:

- declining discharge rate in motor units active early in a sustained task;
- increased discharge in other units;
- recruitment of additional units;
- reduced recruitment thresholds;
- increased discharge variability;
- threshold-specific divergence.

In a 2026 BFR fatigue study (PMID 41543359):

- lower-threshold MU discharge rate decreased;
- higher-threshold MU discharge rate increased;
- recruitment/derecruitment thresholds decreased.

In older non-BFR work, fatigue-related firing/recruitment responses also varied by muscle, force target and unit threshold.

Canonical rule:

**motor-unit firing under fatigue is a population-control problem; different motor units can change discharge in different directions within the same task.**

Confidence: **HIGH for non-uniformity; MODERATE for exact pattern in dynamic RT.**

---

## 10. Recruitment during fatigue

As active muscle fibers lose force, maintaining an external target may require increased neural drive and recruitment of additional motor units.

Classic high-density/decomposition evidence shows:

- progressive recruitment of new units;
- lower recruitment thresholds in many fatiguing submaximal tasks;
- preservation of recruitment order despite threshold shifts in some protocols.

The 2024 resistance-trained study (PMID 39421412) showed that during a sustained isometric fatigue test:

- torque steadiness worsened;
- sEMG excitation increased;
- mean motor-unit action-potential amplitude increased,

consistent with increasing excitation/recruitment demand during the task.

But this is not equivalent to:

> every motor unit is progressively recruited in every fatigue task.

Near maximal efforts may already recruit much of the available pool; different contraction types and durations change the compensation strategy.

Canonical rule:

**fatigue often increases recruitment demand, but recruitment adaptation is constrained by initial force level, available motor-unit reserve, contraction mode and task duration.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH.**

---

## 11. Coordination under fatigue

Fatigue can alter both activation of individual muscles and coupling among muscles.

The 2026 strength-trained vs untrained study (PMID 41297828) found:

- force/RFD declined in both groups;
- selected quadriceps EMG amplitudes increased;
- rate-of-EMG-rise responses differed by training status;
- fatigue altered intermuscular coherence differently depending on muscle-pair function and training status.

The 2026 corticomuscular-coherence systematic review (PMID 42088947; 15 studies) found heterogeneous fatigue-related CMC changes:

- beta-band coherence often decreased;
- preserved or increased coherence also occurred;
- no consistent limb-specific universal pattern emerged;
- responses depended on task, intensity, muscle group and fatigue stage.

Canonical rule:

**fatigue changes coordination, but there is no universal “coordination decreases” biomarker or single desirable direction of coherence change.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for adaptation; MODERATE for interpretation of one coherence metric.**

---

## 12. Proprioception under fatigue

Fatigue can reduce some direct proprioceptive abilities, especially active joint-position accuracy.

The 2024 knee systematic review (PMID 38810655; 42 studies) found higher joint-repositioning errors after local and general fatigue protocols.

The 2024 lower-extremity meta-analysis (PMID 38311746; 43 studies) found significant increases in active absolute repositioning error at the:

- knee;
- ankle;
- hip,

while some passive-position outcomes were not significantly affected.

A 2025 knee study (PMID 41014991) showed coactivation changed after fatigue but did not simply explain error magnitude.

A 2026 MEG study (PMID 42973078) found no significant change in one early cortical proprioceptive-processing measure after plantar-flexor fatigue.

Canonical rule:

**fatigue can impair proprioceptive performance, but the effect is submodality-, joint-, task- and measurement-specific; do not equate balance/coordination change with global proprioceptive loss.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for active position-sense impairment; MODERATE for exact sensory mechanism.**

---

## 13. Fatigue and exercise technique

Fatigue can change movement strategy.

The 2025 systematic review of experienced lifters (PMID 41085612; 22 studies, 293 participants) found that fatigue/intensity commonly produced:

- lower bar velocity;
- lower power;
- longer concentric duration;
- changes/greater variability in joint mechanics;
- particularly visible changes around acceleration/sticking regions.

The exact force/joint response was inconsistent across studies and lifters.

A 2026 acute RT study (PMID 42458937) similarly found worsening within-session kinematics as proximity to failure increased in most tested conditions.

Important boundary:

`kinematic change under fatigue` does not automatically equal
`unsafe technique`,
`technical failure`,
or
`injury mechanism`.

Canonical rule:

**fatigue can alter technique/movement strategy, but there is no universal fatigued-technique pattern and biomechanical change alone does not prove injury risk.**

Confidence: **HIGH for movement change; LOW-MODERATE for direct injury inference.**

## 14. Task dependency

Task dependency is the central organizing principle of NEU-08.

Fatigue expression depends on:

- target force/intensity;
- contraction duration;
- dynamic vs isometric action;
- shortening vs lengthening;
- movement complexity;
- muscle mass;
- duty cycle/rest;
- blood flow/perfusion;
- proximity to failure;
- training status;
- sensory demands;
- measurement time point.

This explains apparently contradictory findings such as:

- VA decreasing in one protocol but not another;
- some motor-unit discharge rates decreasing while others increase;
- recruitment thresholds falling in some tasks;
- coherence decreasing, increasing or remaining stable;
- proprioception changing in some submodalities but not others.

Canonical rule:

**the correct question is not “what does fatigue do to the nervous system?” but “what changes in this task, at this fatigue stage, measured by this method?”**

Confidence: **HIGH.**

## 15. Recovery of neuromuscular function

Recovery is multidimensional.

Different outcomes can normalize at different times:

- voluntary force;
- movement velocity;
- RFD;
- VA;
- twitch/contractile function;
- proprioception;
- coordination;
- soreness;
- perceived fatigue.

The classic failure-vs-nonfailure study (PMID 28965198) found slower recovery of mechanical performance after training to failure, with differences persisting roughly 24–48 h.

The 2025 RT systematic review (PMID 40644670) similarly identifies proximity to failure, set duration, volume and density as major determinants of acute fatigue.

A 2026 study of squat vs deadlift fatigue (PMID 42001478) showed exercise-specific CMJ recovery profiles despite similar soreness.

The 2026 repeated-bout systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID 42762323) found peripheral contractile outcomes more consistent than central activation/motor-unit outcomes during post-eccentric recovery.

The 2026 methodological study (PMID 42881562) further shows that measured fatigue magnitude/reliability depends on **when** the post-training test is taken.

Canonical rule:

**there is no single neuromuscular recovery clock; recovery must be defined for a specific function, test and time point.**

Confidence: **HIGH.**

---

## 16. One-marker fatigue / recovery error

The following inferences are rejected:

- normal VA → fully recovered;
- normal EMG → fully recovered;
- normal soreness → fully recovered;
- normal CMJ → every neuromuscular subsystem recovered;
- reduced CMJ → specifically central fatigue;
- reduced bar velocity → specifically peripheral fatigue.

The same observed performance change can arise from different mechanism combinations.

Project rule:

**use performance tests to answer performance-readiness questions and mechanistic tests only for the mechanism they validly probe.**

This boundary will matter downstream in TST/IND.

---

## 17. Practical downstream rules

1. **Do not use surface EMG alone to diagnose central fatigue.**
2. **Do not interpret VA as a literal percent of CNS capacity.**
3. **Do not assume central fatigue is always the dominant limiter near failure.**
4. **Do not assume all motor-unit firing rates fall with fatigue.**
5. **Expect recruitment strategy to depend on initial force and remaining motor-unit reserve.**
6. **Treat coordination changes as task specific rather than universally worse.**
7. **Expect some active proprioceptive errors to increase with fatigue, but do not infer global sensory failure.**
8. **Technique can change with fatigue without a universal pattern; define technical failure separately in TEC.**
9. **Near-failure/high-volume/high-density work generally increases acute fatigue burden.**
10. **Recovery decisions must reference the target performance function, not one biomarker.**
11. **Mental fatigue and neuromuscular central fatigue are separate constructs.**
12. **Detailed peripheral fatigue mechanisms remain PHY-10/BIO, not NEU-08.**

## 19. Confidence / unresolved issues

### HIGH

- central and peripheral fatigue can coexist;
- central fatigue is not synonymous with mental fatigue;
- VA is method/muscle/protocol dependent;
- motor-unit behavior during fatigue is non-uniform;
- fatigue can alter recruitment, coordination, proprioception and movement strategy;
- task dependency is fundamental;
- neuromuscular recovery is outcome specific.

### MODERATE-HIGH

- active lower-limb position-sense error commonly increases after fatigue;
- proximity to failure, volume and density increase acute RT fatigue;
- fatigue-related technique changes are common in major barbell lifts;
- central activation can remain relatively preserved despite substantial peripheral/contractile impairment.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- exact central vs peripheral contribution to task failure;
- exact motor-unit firing/recruitment strategy in dynamic multijoint RT;
- exact meaning of one coherence change;
- exact proprioceptive mechanism;
- exact time course of neural vs contractile recovery;

- one fatigue-related motor-unit firing direction;
- one coordination signature of fatigue;
- one universal fatigued-technique pattern;
- a direct causal rule that fatigue-induced kinematic change equals injury risk;
