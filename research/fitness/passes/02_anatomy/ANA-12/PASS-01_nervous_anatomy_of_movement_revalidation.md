## 3. Spinal cord — minimum structural organization

For movement-related anatomy:

- spinal cord contains gray matter surrounded by white matter;
- anterior/ventral horn contains somatic lower-motor-neuron cell bodies;
- posterior/dorsal horn receives/organizes sensory input;
- spinal segments give rise to paired spinal nerve roots.

Canonical adult segment count:
- 8 cervical nerve pairs;
- 12 thoracic;
- 5 lumbar;
- 5 sacral;
- 1 coccygeal.

Project boundary:

Detailed descending tracts, interneuronal circuitry and motor-control physiology belong to NEU.

---

## 4. Dorsal root vs ventral root

### Dorsal/posterior root

Primarily afferent sensory fibers.

Sensory neuron cell bodies:
- dorsal-root ganglion.

### Ventral/anterior root

Primarily efferent fibers:
- somatic motor axons;
- at relevant levels, preganglionic autonomic axons.

Roots unite to form:
- a short **mixed spinal nerve**.

Project rule:

**root ≠ ramus.**

This distinction is mandatory.

---

## 5. Mixed spinal nerve and rami

After dorsal + ventral roots unite, the mixed spinal nerve divides into:

### Dorsal ramus
Mixed sensory + motor fibers.

Broad supply:
- intrinsic/paraspinal back muscles;
- posterior cutaneous territories;
- spinal-joint branches.

### Ventral ramus
Mixed sensory + motor fibers.

Broad supply:
- anterolateral trunk;
- limbs;
- major somatic plexuses.

Project rule:

**dorsal/ventral roots are pre-spinal-nerve structures; dorsal/ventral rami are post-spinal-nerve branches.**

---

## 6. Cervical exit rule / C8

Key naming point:

- C1–C7 spinal nerves exit above the correspondingly numbered vertebra;
- **C8** exits between C7 and T1;
- from T1 downward, nerves exit below the correspondingly numbered vertebra.

Because spinal cord ends cranial to the end of the vertebral canal, lower roots descend as cauda-equina rootlets before exiting.

This is relevant to root-level localization later, but diagnostic interpretation belongs to MED.

---

## 7. Plexus concept

A somatic nerve plexus is formed when **ventral rami** exchange/recombine axons before producing named peripheral nerves.

Consequences:

1. one named peripheral nerve usually contains axons from multiple spinal levels;
2. one spinal root contributes to multiple peripheral nerves;
3. one muscle can receive fibers from several root levels through one or more branches;
4. root-level and peripheral-nerve lesion patterns therefore differ.

Project rule:

**plexus recombination is why myotome maps and named-nerve maps are not interchangeable.**

---

## 8. Brachial plexus

Typical organization:

`C5–T1 ventral rami (“roots” of plexus) → trunks → divisions → cords → terminal nerves`

### Trunks
- superior: C5–C6;
- middle: C7;
- inferior: C8–T1.

### Divisions
Each trunk:
- anterior;
- posterior.

### Cords
Named relative to axillary artery:
- lateral;
- posterior;
- medial.

### Five terminal nerves
- musculocutaneous;
- axillary;
- radial;
- median;
- ulnar.

Project precision:

The structures called “roots” **inside brachial-plexus terminology are ventral rami**, not the dorsal/ventral spinal roots defined in section 4.

---

## 9. Brachial-plexus variation

Textbook C5–T1 organization is modal, not universal.

Known variation includes:
- prefixed/postfixed root contribution patterns;
- variant communications;
- altered terminal-branch formation.

Current 2026 cadaveric work continues to identify terminal-branch variation.

Current 2026 meta-analyses also confirm frequent communications between major upper-limb peripheral nerves.

Project rule:

**a textbook peripheral-nerve map is a useful default, not a guarantee of identical personal wiring.**

No attempt is made to infer a variant from symptoms alone.

---

## 10. Axillary nerve

Typical source:
- posterior cord;
- predominantly C5–C6.

Course relation:
- exits posterior axillary region near quadrangular-space structures.

Main motor:
- deltoid;
- teres minor.

Main cutaneous territory:
- superior/lateral shoulder via superior lateral cutaneous nerve of arm.

Project use:

Axillary nerve map is relevant to shoulder weakness/sensory localization, but diagnosis remains MED.

---

## 11. Musculocutaneous nerve

Typical source:
- lateral cord;
- C5–C7 contribution.

Main motor:
- coracobrachialis;
- biceps brachii;
- brachialis.

Terminal sensory continuation:
- lateral cutaneous nerve of forearm.

Project rule:

**musculocutaneous nerve is not “biceps nerve only”.**

It carries a broader anterior-arm motor pattern plus lateral-forearm sensation.

---

## 12. Radial nerve

Typical source:
- posterior cord;
- contributions commonly C5–T1.

Broad motor territory:
- posterior arm;
- posterior forearm/extensor-supinator system;
- brachioradialis.

Important distal organization:
- superficial radial branch → primarily sensory;
- deep radial/posterior interosseous pathway → primarily motor to extensor system.

Broad sensory:
- posterior arm/forearm;
- dorsolateral hand, with clinically useful autonomous-region emphasis near first dorsal web space.

Project rule:

**radial nerve ≠ triceps-only or wrist-extension-only nerve.**

---

## 13. Median nerve

Typical formation:
- lateral + medial cord roots;
- broad C5–T1 contribution.

Broad motor:
- most forearm flexor-pronator muscles;
- thenar motor system via recurrent branch;
- lateral lumbrical contribution.

Broad sensory:
- lateral palm/fingers, with distal digital distribution.

Important structural branches include:
- anterior interosseous nerve;
- palmar cutaneous branch;
- recurrent motor branch in hand.

Project rule:

**median nerve territory spans forearm + hand and is not synonymous with carpal tunnel.**

Entrapment/pathology → MED.

---

## 14. Ulnar nerve

Typical source:
- medial cord;
- predominantly C8–T1.

Broad motor:
- FCU;
- medial FDP;
- most intrinsic hand muscles.

Broad sensory:
- medial hand;
- little finger + ulnar side of ring finger, with palmar/dorsal cutaneous branching.

Project rule:

**ulnar nerve supplies both forearm and hand structures; “ulnar = intrinsic hand only” is incomplete.**

---

## 15. Median–ulnar communications — current update

Current 2026 systematic reviews/meta-analyses confirm common anatomical communications such as:
- Martin–Gruber;
- Marinacci;
- Riche–Cannieu;
- Berrettini patterns.

These can alter:
- expected motor distribution;
- expected sensory distribution;
- electrodiagnostic patterns.

Project conclusion:

**named-nerve maps are modal, not perfectly isolated electrical territories.**

Exact prevalence varies by:
- anatomical vs electrophysiological method;
- population;
- classification.

No variant is inferred without direct evidence.

---

## 16. Lumbosacral plexus — project-level model

The lower-limb peripheral nervous system is organized through:

### Lumbar plexus
Typical major contributions:
- L1–L4 ventral rami;
- variable T12 contribution.

Key lower-limb nerves:
- femoral;
- obturator;
- lateral femoral cutaneous.

### Lumbosacral trunk
- L4–L5 contribution bridges lumbar and sacral plexus.

### Sacral plexus
Broadly:
- lumbosacral trunk + sacral ventral rami.

Important branches:
- sciatic;
- superior gluteal;
- inferior gluteal;
- pudendal and other pelvic branches.

Project rule:

**lumbosacral plexus is a recombining network, not a one-root/one-nerve cable map.**

---

## 17. Lumbosacral-plexus variation

2025 lumbar-plexus review and 2026 imaging review emphasize variation in:
- origin;
- course;
- branching;
- accessory/absent branches;
- morphology.

Project consequence:

**root values and branch maps should be written as typical/common contributions, not immutable personal anatomy.**

This is especially important when later interpreting unusual motor/sensory patterns.

## 18. Femoral nerve

Typical source:
- posterior divisions of L2–L4 ventral rami.

Course:
- within/lateral to psoas region;
- between psoas and iliacus;
- under inguinal ligament;
- into anterior thigh.

Broad motor:
- quadriceps;
- sartorius;
- iliacus;
- variable pectineus contribution.

Broad sensory:
- anterior thigh via cutaneous branches;
- medial leg/foot via saphenous nerve.

Project rule:

**femoral nerve territory extends beyond quadriceps.**

---

## 19. Obturator nerve

Typical source:
- anterior divisions of L2–L4.

Broad course:
- medial psoas/pelvic course;
- obturator canal;
- anterior/posterior divisions in medial thigh.

Broad motor:
- major adductor compartment muscles.

Broad sensory/articular:
- small/variable medial-thigh cutaneous territory;
- hip/knee articular contributions.

Project rule:

**obturator cutaneous territory is variable and should not be treated as a large fixed dermatome-like zone.**

---

## 20. Sciatic nerve

Typical source:
- L4–S3.

Structurally contains two major components from proximal levels:
- tibial;
- common fibular.

Broad course:
- leaves pelvis through greater sciatic foramen, commonly inferior to piriformis;
- descends posterior thigh;
- divides into tibial + common fibular components, typically near or above popliteal region.

Motor before terminal division:
- posterior thigh/hamstring-related distribution.

Project rule:

**sciatic nerve is already a composite tibial/common-fibular system proximally.**

---

## 21. Sciatic division / piriformis variation — current update

2026 systematic reviews/meta-analyses continue to show variation in:
- division level;
- relationship to piriformis;
- course within pelvis/posterior hip.

Project rule:

**“sciatic always divides at one fixed popliteal level and always passes identically below piriformis” is not universal anatomy.**

Variation alone ≠ pathology.

---

## 22. Tibial nerve

Typical root contribution:
- L4–S3.

Broad motor:
- posterior leg;
- plantar intrinsic-foot system through medial/lateral plantar branches.

Broad actions represented by supplied muscles:
- plantarflexion;
- inversion contribution;
- toe flexion;
- intrinsic plantar-foot actions.

Broad sensory:
- plantar foot through plantar branches;
- heel/calcaneal branches;
- sural contribution via medial sural cutaneous branch.

Current NCBI reference updated 2026-09-14 retains this broad map.

---

## 23. Common fibular / peroneal nerve

Typical root contribution:
- L4–S2.

Course:
- lateral popliteal region;
- around fibular head/neck.

Major terminal branches:

### Superficial fibular
Motor:
- fibularis longus/brevis.

Sensory:
- most dorsum of foot / anterolateral distal leg.

### Deep fibular
Motor:
- anterior compartment;
- dorsiflexors/toe extensors;
- dorsal intrinsic extensor system.

Sensory:
- first dorsal web space.

Project rule:

**common fibular nerve is anatomically exposed around fibular neck, but compression/injury diagnosis belongs to MED.**

---

## 24. Root map vs peripheral nerve map

Example principle:

A movement such as elbow flexion can receive:
- several root-level contributions;
- several muscles;
- more than one peripheral nerve contribution depending the movement.

Likewise knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion, grip or shoulder abduction cannot be reduced to:
- one root;
- one nerve;
- one muscle.

Project rule:

**movement localization requires keeping segmental and named-nerve maps separate.**

---

## 25. Myotome

Project definition:

A **myotome** is a functional segmental motor map describing muscles/actions receiving a major contribution from a spinal cord/root level.

Critical caveats:

1. most limb muscles receive axons from multiple roots;
2. one root contributes to multiple muscles and peripheral nerves;
3. published myotome tables differ;
4. a “key muscle” is a testing convention, not proof that one root exclusively owns the muscle.

Project rule:

**myotome = overlapping segmental motor territory, not one-root/one-muscle exclusivity.**

Detailed clinical localization → MED/TST.

---

## 26. Dermatome

Project definition:

A **dermatome** is an area of skin receiving sensory input predominantly from one spinal nerve/root level.

Critical caveats:

1. adjacent dermatomes overlap;
2. maps differ between sources;
3. individual variation exists;
4. dermatomes are not identical to named peripheral cutaneous nerve territories.

The evidence-based dermatome literature specifically emphasizes overlap and variability.

Project rule:

**dermatome maps are localization aids, not exact personal borders.**

---

## 27. Dermatome vs peripheral cutaneous nerve territory

These answer different questions.

### Dermatome
Segmental/root-level organization.

### Peripheral cutaneous territory
Named peripheral nerve after plexus recombination.

Example principle:
- a median-nerve sensory territory in the hand is not “the C6/C7 dermatome”;
- a radial first-web-space territory is not a one-root map.

Project rule:

**never substitute a named-nerve sensory map for a dermatome map or vice versa.**

---

## 28. Myotome vs peripheral motor nerve

These also answer different questions.

### Myotome
Segmental contribution.

### Peripheral nerve
Axons recombined through plexus into a named nerve.

Consequences:
- one peripheral nerve includes multiple root levels;
- one root reaches muscles through multiple peripheral nerves.

This is structurally central to distinguishing:
- radicular;
- plexus;
- peripheral-nerve;
- muscle-level patterns later.

Diagnosis remains MED.

## 29. Motor unit — structural-functional minimum

Current structural definition:

**one α motor neuron + all extrafusal skeletal-muscle fibers it innervates = one motor unit.**

α motor-neuron cell body:
- anterior/ventral horn (for spinal muscles/limbs).

Axon:
- exits through ventral root;
- travels through mixed spinal/peripheral nerve;
- branches within target muscle;
- terminates at neuromuscular junctions.

Project boundary:

This structural unit belongs in ANA-12.

Detailed:
- recruitment order;
- rate coding;
- synchronization;
- fatigue behavior

belong to NEU/PHY.

---

## 30. Neuromuscular junction — minimum structural boundary

NMJ = synapse between:
- motor-axon terminal;
- skeletal-muscle fiber motor end plate.

Canonical transmitter:
- acetylcholine.

Project rule:

ANA-12 only needs the structural link:

`lower motor neuron → peripheral axon → NMJ → extrafusal muscle fiber`

Transmission physiology → PHY/NEU.

---

## 31. Muscle spindle — structural basis

Muscle spindle:
- encapsulated proprioceptive receptor within skeletal muscle;
- contains **intrafusal** muscle fibers;
- receives sensory afferent endings;
- receives γ-motor innervation regulating spindle sensitivity.

Current proprioception literature retains principal sensory classes:
- group Ia;
- group II.

Project rule:

**muscle spindle is not an ordinary force-producing extrafusal motor unit.**

Detailed firing/reflex/gamma-loop physiology → NEU.

---

## 32. Muscle-spindle heterogeneity — current update

2025–2026 proprioception reviews emphasize that spindle/proprioceptive neurons are not one completely homogeneous sensor class.

Current evidence supports diversity in:
- morphology;
- molecular identity;
- connectivity;
- response properties.

Project conclusion:

**retain classic spindle structure for anatomy, but do not assume one universal afferent behavior.**

This does not require expanding ANA-12 into molecular neurobiology.

---

## 33. Golgi tendon organ — structural basis

Golgi tendon organ:
- encapsulated mechanosensory receptor associated with tendon / musculotendinous connective tissue;
- receives group Ib sensory afferent fibers in the classical organization;
- positioned to report tension/force-related mechanical state.

Project rule:

**GTO is a sensory structure, not a “safety switch that simply shuts off the muscle”.**

The classic “autogenic inhibition” story is a physiology simplification and belongs to NEU, not anatomical definition.

---

## 34. Muscle spindle vs GTO

### Muscle spindle
Located within muscle:
- intrafusal fibers;
- strong relation to muscle length/velocity sensing;
- Ia/II afferents;
- γ-motor innervation.

### GTO
Associated with tendon/musculotendinous connective tissue:
- Ib afferent organization;
- strong relation to force/tension sensing.

Project rule:

**both are proprioceptive structures; their central effects are not reducible to “stretch sensor vs shutdown sensor”.**

---

## 35. Neural-symptom boundary relevant to fitness

ANA-12 does not diagnose disease.

But structural maps justify the following **localization principle**:

A symptom pattern may be more plausibly:
- local muscle/tendon;
- single peripheral nerve;
- plexus;
- spinal root;
- central

depending on whether motor/sensory findings respect those anatomical distributions.

Project rule:

**anatomy can guide localization hypotheses; it cannot confirm diagnosis from exercise symptoms alone.**

MED/TST owns diagnostic/red-flag decisions.

## 38. Current changes / updates relative to generic textbook model

Because no current legacy ANA-12 finding exists, changes are framed against common simplified teaching models.

### Strengthened

1. **Root ≠ ramus** distinction is explicit.
2. Brachial-plexus “roots” are identified as **ventral rami**, avoiding terminology collision with dorsal/ventral spinal roots.
3. Myotome and dermatome are treated as overlapping variable maps rather than exact boundaries.
4. Named peripheral nerve territory is explicitly separated from root-level maps.
5. Upper-limb nerve communications are retained as common normal variants.
6. Sciatic division/piriformis relationships are retained as variable.
7. Lumbar/lumbosacral plexus root/branch values are written as typical, not immutable.
8. Motor unit, muscle spindle and GTO are included only to the structural level needed before NEU.

### Current literature updates

1. 2026 upper-limb anastomosis meta-analyses reinforce substantial peripheral-nerve communication variability.
2. 2026 sciatic systematic reviews/meta-analyses reinforce variation in division/course.
3. 2025 lumbar-plexus review and 2026 LSP imaging review reinforce origin/course/branch variability.
4. 2025–2026 proprioception reviews reinforce biological heterogeneity within spindle/proprioceptor systems.

## 39. Established findings

### HIGH

1. Dorsal roots are primarily sensory; ventral roots primarily motor/efferent.
2. Mixed spinal nerves form before dorsal/ventral rami divide.
3. Dorsal and ventral rami are both mixed.
4. Major limb plexuses are formed from ventral rami.
5. Brachial plexus typical core is C5–T1 with roots→trunks→divisions→cords→terminal nerves.
6. Axillary, musculocutaneous, radial, median and ulnar nerves have distinct but overlapping functional territories.
7. Lumbar/sacral plexuses recombine multiple root levels into lower-limb peripheral nerves.
8. Femoral, obturator, sciatic, tibial and common-fibular systems have distinct broad motor/sensory territories.
9. Myotomes and dermatomes overlap and are not identical to named peripheral-nerve territories.
10. Motor unit = α motor neuron + its extrafusal fibers.
11. Muscle spindles contain intrafusal fibers and proprioceptive sensory endings.
