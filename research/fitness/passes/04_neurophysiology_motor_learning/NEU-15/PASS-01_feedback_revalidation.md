## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a feedback framework for later resistance-exercise skill coaching without:

- assuming more feedback is always better;
- assuming reduced feedback frequency is universally superior;
- treating the guidance hypothesis as settled law;
- assuming immediate feedback is always better than delayed feedback;
- assuming terminal feedback is always better than concurrent feedback;
- treating KR and KP as interchangeable;
- treating descriptive KP as automatically useful;
- treating faded/bandwidth/summary schedules as universally optimal;
- assuming self-controlled feedback is universally superior;
- equating acute performance enhancement with durable motor learning;
- inferring feedback dependency merely from high feedback frequency;
- preventing development of learner error-detection capability;
- importing clinical/rehab findings numerically into compound-lift coaching.

### Primary downstream context

Healthy adult novice/intermediate resistance trainees learning or refining exercise technique.

### Critical outcomes

- acquisition performance;
- delayed retention;
- transfer;
- learner error estimation/detection;
- independence from augmented feedback;
- acute RT performance;
- technical competency.

## 3. Intrinsic feedback

Intrinsic feedback is sensory information naturally available as a consequence of performing the movement.

It can include:

- proprioception;
- vision;
- tactile sensation;
- vestibular information;
- audition;
- effort/force sensation.

Important taxonomy boundary:

**intrinsic does not mean “only information from inside the body.”**

For example:

- naturally seeing the bar path can be intrinsic visual information;
- a video replay, overlay, velocity display or coach statement is augmented information.

Intrinsic feedback can be available:

- during movement;
- immediately after movement through residual sensory/perceptual consequences.

Project rule:

**intrinsic feedback is task-generated sensory information available without an added external information channel.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 4. Extrinsic / augmented feedback

Augmented feedback is externally supplied information that supplements task-intrinsic feedback.

Sources include:

- coach;
- video;
- mirror/overlay when specifically added as an instructional display;
- velocity device;
- force display;
- auditory cue;
- haptic/tactile guidance;
- software/AI.

Augmented feedback can improve:

- acquisition;
- current performance;
- some retention/transfer outcomes.

But its effects depend on:

- information content;
- timing;
- frequency;
- modality;
- task complexity;
- learner expertise;
- whether the learner can use intrinsic feedback.

Project rule:

**augmented feedback is an information source, not a single intervention.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 5. Knowledge of results (KR)

KR provides information about the **outcome relative to the task goal**.

Examples:

- target miss distance;
- repetition velocity;
- time;
- accuracy score;
- “the bar reached the intended height”;
- “knee position was within the predefined target range” if framed as criterion success.

KR answers primarily:

> What happened?

It does not necessarily tell the learner how movement was produced or what to change next.

Project rule:

**KR is outcome information, not synonymous with all augmented feedback.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 6. Knowledge of performance (KP)

KP provides information about **movement characteristics / movement quality**.

Examples:

- trunk angle;
- knee displacement;
- bar path;
- timing relation between segments;
- “your trunk flexed earlier on that repetition.”

KP can be:

### Descriptive
What happened in the movement.

### Prescriptive
What movement feature should be changed or how to change it.

This distinction is closure-critical.

The KR-vs-KP systematic review (19 studies, almost entirely novices) found:

- KR vs KP overall direction was unclear;
- KR + **prescriptive** KP was consistently favored over KR alone;
- prescriptive KP tended to favor KP;
- descriptive KP could be inferior to KR.

The review could not meta-analyze due heterogeneity and was mainly novice evidence.

Canonical rule:

**feedback content matters more than the KR/KP label alone; prescriptive KP can be useful for novices, but descriptive KP is not automatically beneficial.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 7. KR vs KP is not a universal hierarchy

No defensible universal rule exists:

`KP > KR`

or

`KR > KP`.

Selection depends on the information problem.

KR is particularly useful when:

- task outcome is clear;
- learner can search for a solution from outcome error;
- movement does not require one prescribed geometry.

KP is more useful when:

- learner cannot diagnose why the outcome occurred;
- a specific movement feature is relevant;
- novice needs movement information not available from intrinsic feedback.

KR + prescriptive KP can be useful when both outcome and movement correction matter.

For resistance-exercise technique, this is important because:

- many tasks are redundant;
- one exact visual movement template is often not universally optimal;
- KP should therefore target a justified error/constraint rather than “copy this shape” by default.

## 8. Immediate vs delayed feedback

This dimension refers to the interval between movement completion and augmented feedback.

### Immediate feedback
Provided almost immediately after the attempt.

### Delayed feedback
A period is allowed before feedback.

Classic timing evidence does **not** support a universal “immediate is best” rule.

The temporal-locus meta-analysis (PMID 7624211) found some statistically significant timing effects, but effect sizes were small and mainly affected acquisition.

The KR-delay RCT (PMID 9200248) found:

- immediate KR improved acquisition accuracy;
- delayed KR produced better retention;
- requiring the learner to estimate movement form improved retention/error estimation.

Therefore:

**feedback delay can create time for intrinsic processing/error estimation, but no universal optimal delay duration is established.**

Confidence: **MODERATE**.

---

## 9. Concurrent vs terminal feedback

This is distinct from immediate vs delayed post-response feedback.

### Concurrent feedback
Provided during movement execution.

### Terminal feedback
Provided after the movement ends.

Concurrent feedback can strongly improve current execution because it guides online correction.

But it can sometimes create a mismatch between:

- practice with guidance;
- later performance without guidance.

The 2025 grasping-force study (PMID 41050791) found:

- concurrent feedback produced smallest errors during practice;
- terminal and self-controlled groups performed better than concurrent at 1-week no-feedback retention.

However:

- complex tasks sometimes benefit from frequent/concurrent information;
- complex rowing-type evidence and older feedback reviews show task-specific effects.

Canonical rule:

**concurrent feedback can optimize guided performance; terminal feedback can better preserve learner-generated control in some tasks, but neither is universally superior.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 10. Feedback frequency

Feedback frequency can mean:

- absolute number of feedback events;
- relative percentage of trials receiving feedback.

The classic guidance hypothesis predicts:

- high-frequency feedback improves acquisition;
- reduced feedback improves retention by preventing dependency.

### Critical revalidation

The 2022 meta-analysis:

- 61 eligible papers;
- k = 75;
- N = 2228;
- substantial heterogeneity;
- no significant effect of reduced feedback frequency at acquisition, immediate retention or delayed retention;
- no significant acquisition-to-retention reversal;
- evidence base appeared severely underpowered.

Therefore:

**reduced feedback frequency is not a robust universal motor-learning law.**

Individual studies still show:

- 67% better than 0/33/100% in one postural task (PMID 38474940);
- 100% better than 50%/none in one complex ski-simulator task (PMID 20037033).

Project rule:

**do not prescribe a universal feedback percentage.**

Confidence: **HIGH against universal reduced-frequency rule**.

---

## 11. Faded feedback

Faded feedback gradually reduces feedback frequency over practice.

The rationale is plausible:

1. more support early;
2. increasing reliance on intrinsic/error-detection processes later.

Positive evidence exists:

- faded KR improved retention in an isometric force task (PMID 31528010);
- a recent walking RCT (PMID 41353792) found 50% faded KR superior to constant KR at 24/48-h retention and transfer.

But the broad feedback-frequency meta-analysis did not establish a robust general reduced-frequency advantage.

Therefore:

**fading is a defensible adaptive strategy, not a universal schedule.**

Confidence: **MODERATE**.

---

## 12. Bandwidth feedback

Bandwidth feedback is provided only when performance/error falls outside a predefined tolerance band.

Potential functions:

- reduce feedback frequency;
- avoid correcting acceptable variation;
- direct attention to meaningful errors;
- allow intrinsic error-detection processing within the acceptable band.

Classic studies found retention benefits from bandwidth KR in some timing tasks.

However:

- optimal bandwidth depends on task scale/error cost;
- bandwidth manipulates both frequency and information meaning;
- old evidence is mainly simple laboratory tasks.

Canonical rule:

**bandwidth feedback means “intervene outside a meaningful tolerance,” not “always use X% error band.”**

Confidence: **MODERATE**.

---

## 13. Summary feedback

Summary feedback delays feedback until a set of trials is completed and then presents information about several trials.

Potential benefits:

- reduces trial-by-trial guidance;
- forces intrinsic processing between attempts;
- allows pattern recognition across repetitions.

Classic summary-KR experiments found:

- worse acquisition with longer summaries;
- sometimes better no-feedback retention;
- the “optimal” summary length changed across tasks (for example 5 vs 15/16 trials in different paradigms).

Therefore no fixed summary length generalizes.

Canonical rule:

**summary feedback can reduce moment-to-moment guidance, but there is no universal number of repetitions to summarize.**

Confidence: **LOW-MODERATE for applied RT prescription**.

---

## 14. Self-controlled feedback

Self-controlled feedback lets the learner choose:

- whether;
- when;
- sometimes what type/timing of feedback to receive.

The 2025 meta-analysis (29 studies, 1147 participants) found:

- no significant acquisition advantage overall;
- positive retention and transfer effects relative to passively received/yoked schedules;
- substantial heterogeneity;
- significant publication-bias signals in retention and transfer comparisons vs yoked groups.

A 2025 feedback-timing experiment found:

- self-controlled timing outperformed concurrent feedback at retention;
- self-controlled outperformed yoked control;
- participants naturally shifted from more concurrent feedback early toward more terminal feedback later.

But replication failures exist (e.g. PMID 34963413).

Canonical rule:

**self-controlled feedback is promising for retention/transfer and learner autonomy, but not a universal superiority law.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for potential benefit / MODERATE after bias concerns**.

---

## 15. Risk of dependency on constant external feedback

Feedback dependency means the learner performs well **with** augmented feedback but substantially worse when it is removed.

This risk is most plausible when feedback:

- substitutes for task-intrinsic information;
- continuously guides online correction;
- is available every attempt;
- prevents learner error estimation.

Classic concurrent-feedback studies demonstrate such dependency in some tasks.

But closure-critical boundary:

**high feedback frequency alone does not prove dependency.**

The 2022 frequency meta-analysis did not support a general reduced-frequency advantage.

In complex tasks, frequent feedback can even improve retention.

Dependency must be demonstrated through:

- no-feedback retention;
- transfer;
- degraded performance when feedback is withdrawn.

Project rule:

**diagnose feedback dependency empirically; do not infer it merely from frequent coaching.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 16. Error detection by the learner

A core learning goal is the ability to detect/evaluate one's own error using intrinsic information.

Evidence shows that learner engagement before augmented feedback matters.

PMID 11404216 found a critical interaction:

- 100% KR + required error estimation produced the best retention;
- 100% KR without error estimation produced the poorest retention;
- reduced KR was not automatically superior.

PMID 9200248 similarly found that subjective form estimation improved later throwing accuracy/error estimation.

Bandwidth and reduced-feedback studies also suggest improved error-detection capability in some paradigms.

Therefore:

**the problem is not simply how often feedback is given; it is whether the learner processes and tests an internal estimate before/against augmented feedback.**

Practical heuristic:

1. perform attempt;
2. learner estimates outcome/error;
3. augmented feedback confirms/corrects estimate;
4. next attempt tests the updated hypothesis.

Status: **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC grounded in motor-learning evidence**.

## 17. Feedback modality is not feedback function

Visual, auditory, tactile/haptic and verbal feedback are modalities.

KR/KP are information-content categories.

Concurrent/terminal and immediate/delayed are timing categories.

Frequency/faded/bandwidth/summary/self-controlled are scheduling categories.

These dimensions can be combined independently.

Example:

- terminal visual KP;
- concurrent auditory KR;
- faded verbal prescriptive KP;
- self-controlled video KP.

Project rule:

**modality, content, timing and schedule must be reported separately.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 18. Direct resistance-training evidence

### RT feedback meta-analysis

PMID 37410360 included 20 studies.

Findings:

- augmented feedback improved acute barbell velocity by ~8.4%;
- pooled acute effect g ≈ 0.63;
- visual feedback showed larger acute effects than verbal feedback;
- high-frequency feedback (including every repetition) often enhanced acute performance;
- chronic studies suggested possible improvements in speed/strength/jump/technical outcomes.

Important boundary:

**this literature is primarily performance/adaptation evidence, not clean motor-learning retention evidence.**

Therefore its high-frequency recommendation cannot be transferred directly into:

> “feedback every repetition is best for technique learning.”

### Squat acquisition/retention RCT

PMID 37916599:

- n = 30 novices;
- visual video feedback after each set vs tactile feedback vs control;
- visual group improved several squat variables;
- retention persisted 3 days to 1 week for selected measures;
- tactile group did not show comparable retention.

This is direct evidence that augmented visual feedback can improve squat acquisition/retention.

But:

- small sample;
- one exercise;
- no KR/KP timing/frequency factorial design;
- no evidence that the exact schedule is universally optimal.

### Technology vs trainer feedback

PMID 36056403:

- 19 untrained women;
- 5 weeks;
- technological vs traditional verbal feedback;
- no significant between-group differences in observed changes.

Therefore:

**feedback can aid RT performance/skill, but current direct evidence does not define one optimal feedback architecture for compound lifts.**

Confidence: **HIGH for evidence boundary**.

---

## 19. Current real-time/AI feedback boundary

A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of AI-based real-time motor feedback (PMID 43016567):

- pooled post-intervention effect was large;
- heterogeneity was extreme;
- prediction interval crossed zero;
- certainty was very low;
- none of the pooled outcomes assessed delayed retention or transfer.

This is a current example of the same project rule:

**real-time performance improvement is not durable motor learning unless retention/transfer is tested.**

## 20. Feedback frequency × task complexity × learner state

The same schedule can have different effects because feedback has different information value.

High frequency can be more defensible when:

- task is initially complex;
- learner cannot detect relevant errors;
- safety-critical mistakes need correction;
- feedback contains necessary information not available intrinsically.

Reduced frequency/fading can be more defensible when:

- learner can estimate error;
- feedback is becoming redundant;
- independence is the target;
- continuous feedback strongly guides online corrections.

This is a **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not a validated universal algorithm.

## 21. Important non-equivalences

1. Intrinsic feedback ≠ information only from “inside the body.”
2. Augmented feedback ≠ KR only.
3. KR ≠ KP.
4. Descriptive KP ≠ prescriptive KP.
5. KP ≠ universally superior to KR.
6. Immediate feedback ≠ universally better learning.
7. Delayed feedback ≠ universally better learning.
8. Concurrent feedback ≠ terminal feedback.
9. Better concurrent practice performance ≠ better retention.
10. High feedback frequency ≠ automatically harmful.
11. Reduced feedback frequency ≠ universally superior.
12. Fading ≠ universal optimum.
13. Bandwidth feedback ≠ one fixed error percentage.
14. Summary feedback ≠ one fixed summary length.
15. Self-controlled feedback ≠ universally superior.
16. Frequent feedback ≠ proven feedback dependency.
17. Acute RT velocity gain ≠ technique learning.
18. Visual feedback ≠ inherently superior across all tasks.
19. Feedback about every error ≠ necessarily better error detection.
20. Learner error estimation ≠ redundant when augmented feedback is available.

## 22. Measurement boundaries

### Acquisition
Performance during feedback-supported practice.

### Retention
Delayed performance, ideally without the augmented feedback being tested.

### Transfer
Performance in a changed/untrained condition.

### Feedback dependency
Worse independent performance when feedback is withdrawn, not merely frequent use.

### Error detection
Accuracy of learner's own performance/error estimate.

### Technical competency
Must be defined separately from external outcome.

### Acute RT output
Velocity/force/repetitions; not equivalent to motor learning.

## 24. Established findings

### HIGH

1. Intrinsic and augmented feedback are distinct sources.
2. KR and KP are different information-content categories.
3. Descriptive and prescriptive KP should not be conflated.
4. Acquisition under feedback is not equivalent to learning.
5. Feedback modality, content, timing and frequency are separate dimensions.
6. Reduced feedback frequency is not a robust universal superiority rule.
7. Feedback dependency must be tested by withdrawal/retention/transfer.
8. Learner error estimation is a relevant part of feedback processing.
9. Acute RT feedback effects do not establish durable technique learning.

### MODERATE-HIGH

10. Prescriptive KP or KR + prescriptive KP can benefit novices more than descriptive KP.
11. Concurrent feedback can improve practice performance but sometimes impair no-feedback retention.
12. Faded feedback can improve retention/transfer in some tasks.
13. Self-controlled feedback can improve retention/transfer, but publication-bias/heterogeneity concerns remain.
14. Visual feedback can support squat technique acquisition/retention in novices.

### MODERATE / TASK-DEPENDENT

15. Delayed post-response feedback may promote intrinsic error processing.
16. Bandwidth feedback can support error detection/retention.
17. Summary feedback can reduce guidance dependence.
18. High feedback frequency may be appropriate for complex or performance-oriented tasks.
