## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a defensible individual-differences framework for motor-learning/coaching decisions without:

- treating training age as a biological trait;
- assuming chronological age determines motor-learning capacity;
- treating “biological age” as interchangeable with chronological age;
- assuming prior experience always facilitates a new skill;
- treating handedness/dominance as a general motor-capacity hierarchy;
- assigning instructional styles from cognitive typologies;
- assuming working-memory or attention measures determine one optimal learning method;
- assuming ADHD-specific coaching can be inferred from generic attention findings;
- treating fatigue sensitivity as a fixed person trait without repeated measurement;
- assigning a preferred cue style once and permanently;
- interpreting group moderators as deterministic individual prescriptions.

### Primary downstream context

Healthy adult novice/intermediate resistance trainees learning/refining strength exercises, with later individualization based on measured response.

### Critical outcomes

- acquisition rate;
- retention;
- transfer;
- cue response;
- cognitive-load tolerance;
- performance under fatigue;
- task-specific dominance/asymmetry;
- robustness across contexts.

## 2. Individual differences are real, but typologies are risky

Motor performance and learning show substantial between-person variation.

But variation can arise from:

- prior experience;
- current task familiarity;
- cognitive capacity;
- age;
- current fatigue;
- attention state;
- morphology;
- motivation;
- measurement error;
- random within-person variability.

Therefore:

**between-person difference ≠ stable trait by default.**

Project rule:

**use group-level moderators to generate hypotheses; use repeated task-specific response to make individual decisions.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 3. Training age

“Training age” is useful descriptively but is a crude proxy.

It can mean:

- years since beginning training;
- years of structured practice;
- years in one sport;
- years using one exercise;
- accumulated deliberate practice;
- performance caliber.

These are not equivalent.

The 2026 expertise-stratified attentional-focus network meta-analysis (PMID **42419609**):

- included 87 studies;
- reclassified expertise into performance/training tiers;
- focus-condition rankings differed across tiers;
- distal external focus ranked favorably in lower tiers;
- holistic focus ranked highest in higher tiers;
- conclusions remained sensitive to small-study effects/reporting bias.

This supports:

**expertise can moderate instructional effects.**

It does not establish:

- one cue rule for every novice;
- one cue rule for every expert;
- years trained as the causal moderator.

The 2025 RT adaptation review (PMID **40551899**) likewise notes training-history differences in neuromuscular adaptation, but also major methodological gaps and limited elite-athlete data.

Canonical rule:

**replace “training age” with the most task-relevant experience/proficiency measure available whenever possible.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 4. Training age ≠ skill level

A lifter can have:

- many years of general gym experience;
- little experience with a specific lift;
- high strength but poor technical familiarity;
- extensive exposure but low-quality practice.

Therefore:

`years trained`
≠
`exercise-specific expertise`.

For NEU decisions, more relevant descriptors include:

- exposure to the exact task;
- current performance consistency;
- retention/transfer history;
- ability to self-detect errors;
- cue dependence;
- performance caliber.

Status: **PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**.

## 5. Biological age

The source map uses “biological age,” but current motor-learning studies frequently operationalize **chronological age groups**, not a validated biological-age index.

This distinction is important.

### Healthy aging evidence

2024 review PMID **39486523**:

- older adults generally show slower/less accurate baseline motor performance;
- capacity for motor skill improvement remains;
- neural mechanisms may differ from young adults.

2025 motor-adaptation study PMID **39792202**:

- young n=28, older n=20;
- both groups adapted similarly;
- implicit adaptation and spontaneous recovery were similar.

2026 systematic experimental paper PMID **42157004**:

- ageing had dissociable effects:
  - impaired discovery/caching of explicit strategies;
  - enhanced implicit recalibration in the studied adaptation paradigms.

2026 procedural-learning study PMID **42886636**:

- young n=18, older n=19;
- both groups learned and retained;
- older group showed stronger next-day retention in that serial-reaction paradigm.

Thus:

**older age does not imply globally reduced motor-learning capacity; different learning components can change in different directions.**

Confidence: **HIGH against global age-deficit rule**.

---

## 6. Biological age ≠ chronological age

Actual biological-age constructs may use:

- maturation stage;
- organ/system biomarkers;
- brain-age estimates;
- physiological frailty/capacity.

They are not interchangeable with calendar age.

2026 youth pilot PMID **42043085**:

- biological age indexed by years from peak height velocity;
- gross motor skill differed across maturational categories;
- authors explicitly judged results underpowered/inconclusive and noted practice/exposure may matter more.

2026 large imaging study PMID **42147461** linked biological brain-aging markers to cognitive/motor performance, but this is observational association, not motor-learning prescription.

Canonical rule:

**do not infer an individual’s learning capacity from chronological age or an unspecified “biological age” label. Name the age construct and outcome.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 7. Previous motor experience

Previous motor experience can:

- facilitate acquisition;
- transfer partial structure;
- bias strategy;
- create interference;
- create expectations about task dynamics.

Transfer depends strongly on similarity between previous and current task structure.

Classic but still relevant task-space evidence (PMID **24920632**) showed:

- prior learning facilitated a criterion task when task dimensions overlapped;
- non-overlapping prior task structure produced prolonged interference.

2025 transfer study PMID **40811438** shows that motor learning can transfer across hands with re-expression of learning-related higher-order network activity.

2025 switching study PMID **41213806** showed newly learned motor policies can interfere transiently during switching through perseveration of the prior policy.

Canonical rule:

**previous experience is useful only insofar as its learned structure overlaps appropriately with the new task.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 8. Prior experience ≠ always positive transfer

Prior experience can create:

- positive transfer;
- negative transfer/interference;
- no meaningful transfer.

Therefore:

**“experienced athlete” is not enough to predict how fast a new exercise will be learned.**

Relevant questions:

1. What was practiced?
2. Which task constraints overlap?
3. Which sensory/action mappings overlap?
4. Does the prior solution fit or conflict with the new goal?

Status: **CORE PRACTICAL BOUNDARY**.

## 9. Handedness / dominance

Hand preference and skilled dominance should not be collapsed.

The 2026 PNAS study PMID **42378273** provides a major current update:

- little/no dominant advantage in ordinary reaching;
- no special dominant advantage under a simple inertial wrist-weight challenge;
- large dominant advantage emerged in unfamiliar complex tool-trajectory control;
- dominance disappeared when participants used a novel effector (elbow);
- practice with either elbow produced symmetric improvement.

Authors conclude that skilled dominance is predominantly:

**learned, task-specific trajectory control riding on top of an initial preference**, rather than one general dominant-hemisphere motor-control superiority.

Canonical rule:

**dominant limb ≠ universally more trainable or globally better controlled.**

Confidence: **HIGH for task-specificity of skilled dominance**.

---

## 10. Dominance and transfer can be asymmetric

Although dominance is not a universal capacity hierarchy, interlimb transfer may be asymmetric in specific tasks.

2025 pistol-shooting study PMID **41192342**:

- right-handed participants;
- dominant→nondominant transfer occurred immediately;
- nondominant→dominant did not under the initial protocol;
- additional exposure altered the asymmetry.

2024–2025 upper/lower-limb RCTs (PMIDs **38768164**, **41262191**) also show cross-limb transfer is possible but task dependent.

Therefore:

**handedness/dominance is a task/history modifier, not a universal prescription to always train one side first.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 12. Cognitive load tolerance

“Cognitive load tolerance” is not one directly measured stable trait.

Relevant capacities include:

- working-memory capacity;
- motor working memory;
- attentional control;
- processing speed;
- strategy use.

2025 motor-working-memory study PMID **41196177** found:

- effector-independent motor working memory correlated selectively with explicit visuomotor learning;
- inferred effector-specific motor working memory correlated with implicit learning.

2026 novice hockey study PMID **41996616**:

- n=40 novices;
- fluid/crystallized intelligence, working memory, psychomotor ability and practice attempts predicted different phases/components of learning;
- working-memory capacity predicted post-coaching improvement.

Earlier instruction-load evidence (PMID **28878701**, upstream NEU-16):

- multiple explicit rules interacted negatively with lower working-memory capacity.

Canonical rule:

**cognitive capacity can moderate instruction/learning, but it does not prescribe one fixed instructional style.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 13. Cognitive capacity measures are not coaching diagnoses

Correlations between WM/cognition and motor learning do not justify:

- “low-WM learner” permanent labels;
- automatically using analogy;
- avoiding all explicit instruction;
- assigning a fixed cue count.

Reasons:

- task specificity;
- measurement noise;
- state effects;
- different cognitive components support different learning phases;
- behavioral response to the actual instruction remains the outcome of interest.

Project rule:

**adapt instruction based on observed overload/performance response; use cognitive testing only if it has a validated downstream decision.**

Confidence: **HIGH against typology**.

## 14. Attention profile

Attention is multidimensional.

The 2024 attention-control meta-analysis PMID **38769271** supports stable between-person variation in broad attention-control abilities.

2026 latent-variable study PMID **41114956** found:

- attention control had a direct relation with learning;
- indirect effects also ran through strategy use and self-efficacy;
- attention intensity/consistency related to learning indirectly through broader factors.

Thus:

**attention profile is not one scalar “good/bad attention” trait.**

Relevant dimensions can include:

- control;
- distractibility/capture;
- sustained consistency;
- conflict resolution;
- strategic allocation.

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 15. Attention profile ≠ attentional-focus prescription

A person with stronger/weaker general attention control does not thereby have a proven optimal:

- internal cue;
- external cue;
- distal cue;
- analogy cue;
- no-cue strategy.

NEU-14/16 remain binding.

The 2026 expertise-stratified NMA (PMID **42419609**) shows cue effects vary by expertise, but also large classification/reporting-bias sensitivity.

Canonical rule:

**general attention capacity and task-specific cue response are separate variables.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 17. Fatigue sensitivity

Fatigue effects on motor learning are heterogeneous.

2026 systematic review PMID **42480681**:

- 38 articles / 43 experiments;
- acquisition:
  - 65.1% detrimental;
  - 9.3% facilitatory;
  - 16.3% neutral;
  - 9.3% mixed/inconclusive;
- retention varied by fatigue modality/task/testing context;
- participant-level moderators were rarely tested.

This proves:

**fatigue does not uniformly impair learning.**

It does not establish stable “fatigue-sensitive learner” phenotypes.

Confidence: **HIGH against universal fatigue effect**.

---

## 18. Fatigue resilience is not automatically a stable individual trait

2026 study PMID **42811472**:

- n=60;
- fatigue vs control before visuomotor tracking practice;
- both groups learned and retained;
- no reliable group difference;
- exploratory individual-difference networks suggested associations;
- confirmatory moderation models provided limited evidence for systematic moderators.

This is particularly relevant to NEU-21:

**individual-looking differences in fatigue response can appear in exploratory analyses without proving stable moderator traits.**

Project rule:

**measure fatigue response repeatedly in the actual task/context before labeling an individual fatigue sensitive/resilient.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

---

## 19. Resistance-training fatigability varies with protocol

Resistance-exercise data reinforce that fatigue sensitivity is contextual.

2025 scoping review PMID **40112869**:

- 34 studies in resistance-trained participants;
- sex differences in fatigue were generally minor-to-none overall;
- some conditions favored lower fatigability in females;
- protocol, exercise complexity, rest, relative strength and technical proficiency influenced findings.

2026 bench-press study PMID **41527566**:

- women completed more repetitions to failure under that protocol;
- recovery of estimated 1RM strength did not differ significantly;
- difference appeared related partly to between-set recovery.

Therefore:

**fatigue response depends on the protocol and cannot be summarized by one stable person score.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 20. Preferred / efficient cueing style

The source map itself says:

> “проверять практически, не назначать по типологии.”

Current evidence strongly supports this boundary.

PMID **29617214**:

- children improved under both internal and external focus;
- task-specific preference correlated with differential immediate performance (r≈0.56);
- preference was one moderator, not a universal cue identity.

PMID **42911075** (2026):

- n=60;
- choosing an external-focus cue increased perceived autonomy;
- did not improve jump height;
- different cue formulations produced different performance;
- participants frequently reinterpreted/modified the cue.

PMID **42419609**:

- cue effects varied by expertise strata;
- ranking changed after instruction reclassification;
- small-study/reporting-bias concerns remained.

Canonical rule:

**cue efficiency must be tested against the task outcome; preference is useful information but not proof of durable superiority.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 21. Preferred cue ≠ stable learner type

Reject labels such as:

- “internal-focus person”;
- “external-focus person”;
- “analogy learner”;
- “visual learner”;
- “one-cue learner.”

Reasons:

- task goal changes;
- expertise changes;
- wording matters;
- fatigue/pressure changes;
- the learner can reinterpret cues;
- acute preference may not predict retention.

Project rule:

**individualize cueing dynamically, not by permanent category.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 22. Training history can change what cue/instruction works

The expertise NMA provides one current example of a broader principle:

**the same nominal instruction can have different effects at different skill levels.**

This is compatible with:

- novice need for more task representation;
- expert proceduralization;
- different action-effect associations built through practice.

But it does not justify:

- “novices need X” as a universal rule;
- “experts need Y” as a universal rule.

Status: **MODERATE-HIGH practical modifier**.

## 23. Individual difference vs state difference

This distinction is closure-critical.

### Trait-like candidate
Relatively stable over repeated observations.

Examples:

- age;
- accumulated task experience;
- hand preference.

### State/context variable
Can change over hours/days/tasks.

Examples:

- fatigue;
- arousal;
- attention state;
- motivation;
- sleep loss.

### Mixed / task-specific
Can show both stable and contextual variation.

Examples:

- cue preference;
- cognitive-load tolerance;
- perceived effort;
- task-specific dominance.

Canonical rule:

**do not convert a one-session state response into a stable learner trait.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

---

## 24. Individual response ≠ treatment-effect heterogeneity proof

A participant responding differently from the group mean does not prove:

- stable personalized treatment effect;
- reproducible responder status;
- causal moderator.

Measurement noise and within-person variability matter.

This is especially relevant for:

- fatigue response;
- cue response;
- attentional response.

Project rule:

**personalization requires repeatability or a strong mechanistic/behavioral test, not one observed deviation.**

Status: **METHODOLOGICAL BOUNDARY inherited from RES/TST logic**.

---

## 25. Practical individualization synthesis

A defensible future coaching heuristic is:

1. describe task-specific training history, not just years trained;
2. measure current skill level/consistency;
3. treat age as a modifier, not a capacity verdict;
4. ask which prior skills share relevant task structure;
5. do not assume dominant side is globally superior;
6. reduce/reshape instruction when observable cognitive overload appears;
7. distinguish broad attention capacity from task-specific attentional strategy;
8. leave ADHD-specific design to Area 12;
9. measure fatigue response in the actual protocol across repeated exposures;
10. test cue variants against performance/retention rather than assign a permanent cue type;
11. update the model as expertise and context change.

Status:

**PRACTICAL_HEURISTIC**, not a learner-typing system.

## 27. Measurement boundaries

### Training age
Exposure duration/history; must specify task and training type.

### Expertise
Task-specific proficiency/performance level.

### Biological age
Must specify construct/measure; not a synonym for chronological age.

### Previous motor experience
Prior task/control policies potentially relevant to new task.

### Dominance
Preference/learned skill asymmetry; task dependent.

### Cognitive capacity
Specific construct such as WM/attention control, not generic “smart learner.”

### Attention profile
Pattern across attention-control dimensions, not one scalar.

### Fatigue sensitivity
Repeated task-specific performance/learning response under fatigue.

### Cue preference
Subjective or behavioral preference for a cue format.

### Cue efficiency
Observed task outcome/retention under a cue.

## 28. Direct resistance-training boundary

Direct individual-difference evidence in compound-lift motor learning remains limited.

Current direct/near-direct evidence supports:

- expertise modifying cue/focus effects;
- strength-training history modifying neuromuscular adaptations;
- resistance-training fatigability depending on sex/protocol/technical proficiency;
- cue semantics affecting trained lifters;
- dominant/non-dominant differences being task/practice specific.

It does **not** establish:

- a validated “lifter learning profile”;
- cue type from personality/cognition;
- one training-age threshold;
- one fatigue-sensitive phenotype;
- ADHD-specific technique-learning rules;
- biological-age motor-learning prescription.

Exact individual decision rules belong IND/COA after TST/PSY/MEC dependencies.
