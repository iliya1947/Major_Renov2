## 1. Purpose / Question Card

### Decision

Build a sufficiently accurate motor-learning framework for later exercise-technique assessment, coaching, testing and programming without:

- inferring learning from within-session performance alone;
- treating acquisition as synonymous with durable learning;
- calling immediate post-practice improvement “retention”;
- treating retention, transfer and generalization as interchangeable;
- assuming a retained skill transfers broadly to new tasks/load/context;
- treating automaticity as all-or-none or as “no attention required”;
- treating fast relearning as proof that performance was fully retained;
- assuming rested retention implies robustness under fatigue or pressure;
- assuming practice under fatigue/pressure automatically produces resilience;
- treating exercise technique as verbal-rule recall instead of a practiced sensorimotor skill;
- treating one observed movement pattern as the only valid learned technique.

### Question type

Motor-learning construct + measurement + transfer evidence, with direct resistance-exercise evidence where available.

### Population/context

Primary downstream target:

- healthy adults;
- novice/intermediate resistance trainees;
- exercise-technique learning.

Supporting evidence:

- laboratory motor learning;
- sport skill learning;
- sensorimotor adaptation;
- locomotor learning.

Transfer from laboratory paradigms to complex strength exercise is labeled explicitly.

### Critical outcomes

- acquisition performance;
- delayed retention;
- transfer;
- generalization;
- relearning/savings;
- automaticity;
- performance under fatigue;
- performance under psychological pressure;
- retained exercise technique.

### Closure criterion for this PASS

The pass must preserve the learning-performance distinction, separate retention/transfer/generalization/relearning, keep automaticity gradual, define robustness as state/context dependent, and establish exercise technique as a practiced motor skill without prematurely prescribing coaching methods owned by later NEU topics.

## 3. Core construct — motor learning is inferred, not directly observed

For project use:

> **Motor performance** = observable task output at a given moment.

> **Motor learning** = relatively durable change in capability for skilled performance attributable to practice/experience, inferred from later behavior rather than directly observed during practice.

This distinction is foundational.

Within-session performance can change because of:

- genuine learning;
- warm-up;
- fatigue;
- motivation/arousal;
- augmented feedback;
- task familiarity;
- transient strategy;
- temporary guidance/support.

Therefore:

`better during practice`
does not automatically imply
`better retained skill`.

The classic focused review (PMID 22142953) directly shows that delayed retention/transfer is a better indicator of learning than end-of-practice performance.

Current evidence still demonstrates the same dissociation. In the 2026 random-vs-blocked practice study (PMID 42691817):

- blocked practice produced better end-of-practice performance;
- random practice produced better 24-h retention.

The 2026 AI-feedback meta-analysis (PMID 43016567) likewise explicitly cautions that immediate performance improvement cannot establish durable learning when retention/transfer are unmeasured.

Canonical rule:

**learning must be inferred from durability and/or transfer, not from acquisition performance alone.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 4. Acquisition

Acquisition is the practice phase in which:

- performance is initially expressed;
- task errors may decline;
- speed/accuracy/coordination may improve;
- task strategies are explored/refined.

Acquisition data are important because they show:

- responsiveness to practice;
- trajectory of performance change;
- fatigue/feedback effects;
- how quickly a task can be performed better under current conditions.

But acquisition does not establish durable learning by itself.

A practice condition can:

- improve acquisition and retention;
- improve acquisition but not retention;
- worsen acquisition but improve retention;
- produce no clear long-term effect.

Canonical rule:

**acquisition is the practice-phase performance history, not a synonym for learning.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 5. Retention

Retention asks whether a practiced capability remains after a period without substantial practice.

A defensible retention test should specify:

- delay length;
- whether feedback/instruction is removed;
- whether warm-up/reminder trials occur;
- task/test conditions;
- speed-accuracy trade-off;
- whether the state differs from acquisition.

Important boundary:

`immediate post-test`
is not equivalent to
`delayed retention`.

There is no single universal retention interval that proves learning for all tasks.

The 2025 procedural-skill meta-analysis (PMID 40455501; 1,344 effect sizes from 457 reports) shows that:

- skill decay increases with nonuse;
- decay differs by performance dimension and task;
- task complexity, intermittent use and instructions can moderate decay;
- retention must be separated from relearning.

Its exact decay estimates should not be converted into a universal motor-skill half-life because the evidence spans heterogeneous procedural skills.

Canonical rule:

**retention is outcome-, task-, delay- and test-condition-specific; one retained variable does not prove all aspects of skill are retained.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 6. Transfer

Transfer asks how training affects performance outside the exact practiced condition.

Possible transfer dimensions include:

- different task variant;
- different load;
- different speed;
- different limb;
- different environment;
- different equipment;
- different target;
- different sport/exercise context.

Transfer is expected to depend on overlap between:

- perceptual information;
- movement solution;
- task goal;
- dynamics;
- context.

The 2024 bimanual transfer study (PMID 38081160) and 2026 execution-learning study (PMID 42607302) show that transfer can be substantial in some directions while remaining constrained by task similarity or movement direction.

In strength training, PMID 40314751 provides direct high-level evidence for specificity:

- dynamic strength gains were substantially larger in the trained dynamic task;
- transfer to non-trained isometric strength was smaller.

This is not pure motor-skill evidence because muscular adaptation also contributes, but it confirms that strength expression is task specific rather than fully general.

Canonical rule:

**transfer must be tested in the target condition; it cannot be assumed from successful retention of the trained task.**

Confidence: **HIGH for specificity principle / MODERATE-HIGH for exact transfer magnitude across resistance exercises.**

## 7. Generalization

“Generalization” and “transfer” overlap in motor-learning literature and are not used identically across all subfields.

Project operational distinction:

- **generalization** = expression of learning across untrained values/contexts within a related task space;
- **transfer** = broader performance benefit in a different task, effector, environment or target condition.

This is an operational project convention, not a claim that all papers use the terms this way.

Recent evidence demonstrates that generalization depends on:

- movement direction;
- task similarity;
- movement goals;
- subjective/context similarity;
- training duration;
- learning type.

PMID 42799321 shows that motor generalization can depend not only on kinematic similarity but also on inferred goals and subjective contextual similarity.

PMID 43003929 shows that extensive long-term practice can increase retention while **narrowing angular generalization**, demonstrating that more durable memory does not necessarily imply broader generalization.

Canonical rule:

**retention strength and generalization breadth are different properties.**

Confidence: **HIGH**.

## 8. Automaticity

Automaticity is not treated as an all-or-none stage where conscious control disappears.

The 2025 multilayer model (PMID 41386165) treats automaticity as:

- gradual;
- partial;
- increasing independence from higher-level control;
- associated with reduced control demands.

Useful probes can include:

- dual-task cost;
- attentional-demand manipulations;
- consistency under distraction;
- speed/accuracy under reduced deliberate monitoring.

But these measures are not perfect or interchangeable.

PMID 41844636 is an important corrective: sequence learning improved task performance, yet dual-task demands did not simply disappear and could increase for the trained sequence under some conditions.

Therefore:

**good skill performance ≠ complete automaticity**.

And:

**automaticity ≠ zero attention**.

Canonical rule:

**automaticity is a graded reduction/reorganization of control demand, not a binary endpoint.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH**.

## 9. Relearning / savings

Relearning asks how rapidly a previously practiced skill is reacquired after performance has declined or conditions have changed.

The key behavioral construct is often **savings**:

> faster or more efficient relearning on re-exposure than during initial learning.

Savings is distinct from retention.

A person can show:

- incomplete overt retention;
- yet faster reacquisition.

PMID 41805913 found that human motor savings after sensorimotor adaptation was associated with reinstatement of previously formed cortical activity structure, supporting the existence of latent retained information even when overt behavior changes.

However exact neural mechanism is unresolved.

PMID 42126916 demonstrates a plausible computational route for savings without explicit contextual cues, but it is an RNN model and must not be treated as direct human neural proof.

Canonical rule:

**relearning/savings is evidence of retained learning history, but it is not equivalent to full retention and does not identify one unique neural-memory mechanism.**

Confidence: **HIGH for behavioral savings / MODERATE for exact neural mechanism.**

## 10. Skill robustness under fatigue

Skill robustness asks whether acceptable performance remains when the internal state changes.

Fatigue is one such state change.

The 2026 systematic review (PMID 42480681; 43 experiments) found:

- 65.1% of acquisition experiments showed detrimental fatigue effects;
- some showed neutral, mixed or even facilitatory effects;
- retention findings varied by fatigue modality, task and testing state;
- local neuromuscular fatigue appeared more consistently disruptive than cardiovascular fatigue.

Therefore:

`fatigue during practice`
does not imply
`learning is always worse`.

And:

`rested retention`
does not establish
`fatigued-state robustness`.

PMID 42811472 found that cardiovascular-fatigue practice did not reliably impair learning but also did not create a clear group-level fatigue-resilience advantage.

Canonical rule:

**fatigue robustness must be tested under the fatigue state of interest; it cannot be inferred from rested acquisition or retention.**

Confidence: **HIGH for state dependence / MODERATE for best fatigue-exposure strategy.**

## 11. Skill robustness under psychological stress / pressure

Psychological pressure can:

- facilitate performance;
- impair performance;
- alter attention/control strategy;
- produce choking in susceptible contexts.

The 2026 ESSA position statement (PMID 42271117) explicitly treats pressure effects as context and person dependent rather than uniformly harmful.

The 2026 intervention meta-analysis (PMID 41951087) found that pressure-countering interventions improved several sport-specific performance outcomes, with larger/more consistent effects in closed-skill contexts.

The 2024 mild-anxiety training experiment (PMID 38376935) supports the possibility that practice under manageable pressure can improve later pressure performance in some tasks.

But this does not establish:

- one universal pressure-training dose;
- that every learner should practice under high anxiety;
- that pressure robustness follows automatically from ordinary retention.

Canonical rule:

**robustness under pressure is a separate performance property that must be evaluated in the relevant pressure context.**

Confidence: **MODERATE-HIGH for context dependence / MODERATE for general pressure-training prescription.**

## 12. Technique as a motor skill

Exercise technique is not only declarative knowledge about rules.

Knowing that a movement “should” involve:

- a certain bar path;
- a certain joint position;
- a certain stance;
- a certain sequence

does not guarantee the person can reliably produce the movement under load, fatigue or altered conditions.

Technique is better modeled as a learned sensorimotor capability involving:

- perception of body/load state;
- task goal;
- coordination;
- feedforward prediction;
- feedback correction;
- force control;
- repeated practice;
- retained execution;
- task-specific adaptation.

The 2023 paper `Strength training as a dynamical model of motor learning` (PMID 37270792) explicitly frames strength training through motor-learning dynamics and separates transient performance effects from learning-related changes.

Direct squat evidence is especially relevant.

PMID 37916599 randomized healthy novices to visual, tactile or no feedback during squat learning and measured acquisition plus delayed retention. Visual feedback produced retained changes in several squat-performance variables at 3 days to 1 week.

This is direct evidence that at least some exercise-technique components can be:

- acquired;
- retained;
- modified by feedback.

The 2023 resistance-training feedback meta-analysis (PMID 37410360) also found evidence that feedback affects acute performance and chronic technical competency, while simultaneously demonstrating why acute feedback-enhanced output cannot automatically be labeled learning.

Canonical rule:

**exercise technique is a practiced motor skill expressed under task constraints, not merely a memorized checklist of verbal rules.**

Confidence: **HIGH for skill component / MODERATE-HIGH for direct complex-RT technique evidence.**

## 13. Technique skill does not imply one universal movement pattern

This topic must remain consistent with NEU-09.

A learned exercise skill can be stable without every repetition being visually identical.

Because motor control permits multiple task-successful solutions:

- technique can improve through more reliable task control;
- coordination can reorganize with load;
- individual anthropometry can alter joint-level solution;
- acceptable variability can remain.

Therefore:

`learning technique`
does not mean
`converging on one universal kinematic template`.

Detailed acceptable-technique criteria remain TEC/MEC.

Canonical rule:

**technique learning is learning to control the task within acceptable constraints, not necessarily reproducing one exact geometry.**

## 14. Acquisition, retention and transfer can dissociate

A practical interpretation matrix:

### Good acquisition + good retention
Likely durable learning under similar conditions.

### Good acquisition + poor retention
Possible temporary performance support/guidance rather than durable learning.

### Worse acquisition + better retention
Possible desirable difficulty / contextual-interference-type pattern in some tasks.

### Good retention + poor transfer
Skill is durable but narrow.

### Poor overt retention + fast relearning
Latent learning history / savings may remain.

### Good rested retention + poor fatigued/pressure performance
Skill retained but not robust to that state.

Project rule:

**motor learning is multidimensional; no single acquisition score captures durability, transfer, automaticity and robustness.**

## 15. Measurement boundaries

### Acquisition score
Measures practice-phase performance, not durable learning by itself.

### Immediate post-test
May still contain short-term performance/support effects.

### Retention test
Requires explicit delay and test conditions.

### Transfer test
Requires an untrained target condition and should name exactly what changed.

### Generalization curve
Shows expression across related task/context dimensions, not broad real-world transfer by default.

### Dual-task cost
Can probe automaticity/attentional demand but is not a universal automaticity score.

### Savings
Measures reacquisition advantage, not full retention.

### Performance under fatigue
Measures state-dependent robustness, not the same thing as rested learning.

### Performance under pressure
Measures pressure robustness and can be altered by psychological/contextual factors without erasing underlying skill.

### Technical rating
Can reflect a meaningful skill outcome, but validity depends on defined task criteria and measurement reliability.

## 17. Practical downstream rules

1. **Do not judge learning from the best set/session during practice.**
2. **Use delayed retention when the question is whether technique was learned.**
3. **Use transfer tests when the target differs in load, speed, environment, equipment or movement.**
4. **Name the exact transfer/generalization dimension rather than saying “it transferred”.**
5. **Do not infer complete automaticity from smooth-looking technique.**
6. **After a break, assess both current performance and relearning speed instead of assuming the skill is erased.**
7. **If fatigue robustness matters, test technique/performance under the relevant fatigue state.**
8. **If pressure robustness matters, test under realistic pressure rather than inferring it from calm practice.**
9. **Teach exercise technique through practice and task interaction, not verbal rules alone.**
10. **Do not require exact repetition identity when task-relevant control remains acceptable.**
11. **Keep coaching-method choices in NEU-12…18; NEU-11 establishes what counts as learning evidence.**
