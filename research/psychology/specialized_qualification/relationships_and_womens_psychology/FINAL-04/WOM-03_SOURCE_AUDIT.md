## 2. Corrections and tests

R1 — Distinguish prediction target and timing in preference-matching. Main worldwide sample uses participant-perceived partner attributes and romantic evaluations, not partner's independent reports or actual reciprocal decisions; normative matching beta .37 alone and .34 with distinctive matching; distinctive .19 alone and .11 jointly; average attribute interaction .04. These are different estimands and not probability of choosing a person. Authors estimate only 2–4% of relationship variance potentially explained by corrected matching; this is their model-based interpretation, not prospective individualized accuracy or squared beta automatically. Separate US N1585 two-survey sample 709 steadily partnered,687 steadily single,189 newly partnered; T1 ideals, T2 attributes and evaluation, mean104 days. Table4 corrected matching .24/.01/.24, raw .50/.18/.39; omnibus F10.78. The newly partnered subset is selected by relationship formation, not all possible dates or alternatives. No validated model predicting who will partner, causal effect of matching, or guarantee that a person will select an ideal-matching partner. Premeeting vs initial-impression prediction distinct: Baxter supports initial observed unique liking as predictor of later outcomes, not anticipation of unique liking from personality before meeting. This is compatible with earlier FINAL-04 Eastwick/Finkel evidence. Root cause: previous wording on prediction/formation lacked explicit distinction of target and selection. Retest direct methods/table and sum 709+687+189=1585. Preserve modest matching association; do not replace it with universal irrelevance of stated preferences.

R2 — Sample denominators and heterogeneity in romantic outcomes. Hoan & MacDonald text reports N5941, listed groups 2890 men+2831 women+29 nonbinary=5750 (difference191); nominal Table1 sample Ns sum5962 (21 more than stated overall). Table1 gender categories are also not complete for some nominal samples. Exact missingness, exclusions and discrepancies cannot be reconstructed from inspected text. Results show different outcome-specific t-test dfs: 5862 status,5893 life,3003 sexual satisfaction,4803 desire; do not impose single denominator. Report d=.30/.21 as published, not rederived or prevalence. Exploratory paragraph refers to Sample2/3 while reporting results labelled3/4; unresolved, no strong replicated status interaction claim. The authors' within-women comparison also finds partner-desire differences across demographic contexts; no individual inference from categories. For Bode romantic love selection, mean recalled timing difference .94 months, common modal onset zero, sex incremental adjusted R2 for intensity 1.06%. Neither mean nor selected currently-in-love cohort identifies an individual's love trajectory. Cluster paper's 809 and sex comparison's808 from Romantic Love Survey 2022 are shared source lineage, not independent proof of latent types. Further E6/E7 potential shared data: Hoan Table1 dating-motivation samples 489/811; AMRPS Study1 N1280 in prior note may derive from overlapping recruitment but exact lineage not confirmed, so no double-counting as independent until checked. E9 pairfam source appears different; no record-level dependency determination. Root cause: aggregate description lacked denominator inconsistencies, study label ambiguity and target selection. Retest primary results and arithmetic; general motive, specific attraction, chosen commitment and behavior remain distinct.

R3 — General mean, intraindividual variation, instability and measurement frequency. Three-study desire paper short-term combined N11282 in abstract versus Study2 6656 and Study3 4667=11323 in methods/results (difference41). Potentially different eligible analytic sets, but no reconciliation verified. Use explicit per-study and per-model N; Study3 excluded two participants from variability metrics (255 vs253), not necessarily all other analyses. Study3 beta=.17, CI[-.09,.43] for net variability; instability .19 CI[-.06,.45]; inertia .02 CI[-.04,.08]. None significant, not demonstrated equivalence. Study2 partner-specific vs Study3 general desire questions, intervals and context differ, long-term 3 waves cannot estimate day-to-day fluctuation. Higher mean generalized sex drive g=.69 or modeled bias adjustment .54 does not entail greater or lower within-person variability, partner-specific desire or an individual trait. The debate on sociocultural explanation and response bias explicitly does not determine innate vs social causal shares. Chivers concordance correlations are between distinct measurements in controlled settings, not categorical agreement or a detector of internal states; response-model endorsements are questionnaire classifications, not universal temporal pathways. Root cause: missing numerical inconsistency and precision on eligibility. Retest original abstract, Study2/3 methods/results and arithmetic. No universal female variability/response-sequence claim.

R4 — General desire vs relationship-directed desire and actual predictive validity. Estonian Biobank final count discrepancy already noted; fresh check confirms 46879+20456=67335 vs text67334; reported model F(51,67283) suggests N67335 in Model1 if 51 predictors plus intercept, consistent with subgroup sum, but does not resolve participant flow. Model1 excludes satisfaction to include singles, adjusted R2 .283; Model2 partnered subset includes satisfaction, adjusted R2 .282. Not a same-sample incremental contribution of satisfaction, not held-out prediction; do not label unexplained fraction evidence of a specific hidden cause. General desire uses two questionnaire items, not partner-specific desire. Legal/recorded sex, self-report, language restriction and cohort selection limit transfer. Cross-sectional recent childbirth association cannot substitute for postpartum within-person measurement. Cheon & Kim complete dyads 501/289, individual 1058/655, ratios .577 and .619, descriptive sample ratios rather than eligible-person retention rates. Longitudinal interpretation limited by transition-to-marriage selection, attrition, concurrent vs change and unverified full methods. No proof that general desire inherently harms relationships or that partner-specific desire implies exclusivity or chosen commitment. Prekatsounaki's randomized assignments must not be confused with verified intervention contrast; full assignment results still unverified. Household labor surveys and follow-up heterogeneity suggest contextual associations, not a general causal prescription. Root cause: original notes correctly separate desire types but understate model comparability/retention. Retest direct model descriptions, indexed abstract and arithmetic.

R5 — Time scale, direction comparison and causal claims. Park Table3 actor sexual satisfaction -> subsequent own relationship satisfaction b=.08 CI[.029,.123] p=.002 vs reverse .02 CI[-.013,.055] p=.225; direct equality test p=.046. Partner .05 CI[.001,.095] p=.045 becomes .04 CI[-.012,.086] p=.143 controlling frequency. Change of statistical significance is not proof of mediation; no identified causal pathway via frequency. Table4 frequency outcome .05 CI[.007,.095] p=.024 vs reverse .02 CI[-.014,.063] p=.219; differing p values alone do not establish significant difference (prior note already cautions, no fresh direct contrast verified). Sample excludes 67 same-gender couples and seven mixed-gender couples with no baseline partnered sexual activity: 2111-7=2104; 22.4% attrition,4.1% dissolution, FIML under missingness assumptions, no public CREATE data permission. Annual conditional lag associations not causal. Zhao 12-day study reports bidirectional associations stronger under high stress, not proof of stress mechanism or an invariant feedback loop. Distinct timescales, samples, outcomes and models preclude treating these as directly contradicting common estimands or asserting empirically proved timescale explanation. Scoping review includes Dewitte and overlapping samples, not extra replication. Root cause: explicit limitation of conditioning/mediation and comparison across timescales needed. Retest direct Table3/4, sample description and original Zhao abstract.

Correct primary meta-analysis DOI https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035438 (PMID24564172; 134 effects from 38 published/12 unpublished studies; indexed original abstract). Incorrect DOI is Harris, Pashler & Mickes' critical comment on analytic flexibility, PMID25180803, not original supportive meta-analysis. Correct main report bibliographic link and preserve explicitly identified critique. Additional conflicting meta-analysis Wood et al. https://doi.org/10.1177/1754073914523073 located via original publisher index; no full-text estimate reverified in this pass. The earlier FINAL-04 review already contrasted Stern et al. 2021 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33385373/ N257, four sessions, 1028 assessments, weak general male-body attraction evidence, no compelling robust preference shift and no systematic steroid-level associations; freshly confirmed from indexed abstract. Additional independent longitudinal test Jones et al. 2018 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29708849/ (N584, repeated saliva assays and face preference ratings, indexed original abstract) found no compelling hormone-linked facial masculinity preference change. Different outcomes, overlapping historical literature and unknown cohort dependencies mean no quantitative pooling. These results narrow a universal specific mate-preference shift claim, not evidence of absence of all cyclical changes in desire, mood, attraction, or every subgroup. Separate actual hormonal measures, cyclic timing, generalized desire, stimulus evaluation, trait preference, partner-specific desire and actual mutual choice. Do not infer a specific person's cycle, feelings or willingness from such findings. Detailed physiological and assay audit belongs in WOM-06. Root cause: incorrect DOI in earlier attestation, and insufficient explicit competing-meta-analysis/independent repeated-assay comparison. ## 4. Revised operational method
Separate general relationship wish and general sexual motivation, stated preference, distinctiveness relative to population norms, appraisal of actual person, actor and partner tendencies, unique interaction, experienced affection/love/attachment, partner-directed desire, subjective and physiological arousal, satisfaction, intent, commitment, opportunity and mutual choice. Record which channel, actor, target and time window support each proposition. Model normative desirability and individual matching separately; do not reverse inference from choices to a unique motive or from a reported preference to partner selection. Use real evidence of prospective outcomes and comparator only where available; no statistical model validated for sparse personal records. Compare analogous outcome measures and timescales, distinguish actor/partner/shared effects, conditional association, selection and causal identification. Never infer consent from desire, physiological response, affection, prior activity or relationship status. Respect freely given, specific and revocable agreement; do not treat disinterest or refusal as concealed desire. No manipulative guidance or covert testing. No clinical or hormone-specific conclusion beyond evidence; WOM-05/06 handle transitions and physiological mechanisms.
