# DEC-01 — Judgment and decision foundations: normative/descriptive models, risk and uncertainty, heuristics, framing, reference dependence, calibration, base rates, choice architecture, and inference limits

Status: **CLOSED_FOR_PRACTICE v1.0 — 18/18 PASS**

Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**

## Purpose

Establish the foundational decision-science architecture required before studying forecasting, advice, multi-attribute choice, debiasing, and high-stakes decision support.

The core problem is not:

> Which cognitive biases does a person have?

The core problem is:

> What normative standard applies, how is the decision problem represented, what descriptive process generated the response, what environmental/resource constraints matter, and what conclusion is actually warranted?

This block therefore separates:

1. normative decision standards;
2. descriptive choice behavior;
3. risk versus uncertainty;
4. expected value versus expected utility;
5. prospect-theory-style reference dependence;
6. framing effects;
7. heuristics versus biases;
8. base-rate use;
9. probability communication;
10. confidence/calibration;
11. decision quality versus outcome quality;
12. choice architecture/defaults;
13. bounded/resource/ecological rationality;
14. dual-process language and its limits.

Canonical rule:

`decision problem -> normative target -> information/probability representation -> reference/context -> descriptive response -> resource/environment constraints -> confidence/calibration -> alternatives -> narrow decision inference`.

Never:

`norm violation -> irrational person`

or:

`named bias -> mechanism`.

# II. Decision science requires normative and descriptive levels

## 12. Descriptive question

> What do people actually judge or choose?

Examples:

- choose gamble A versus B;
- estimate probability;
- accept default;
- seek advice;
- express confidence.

## 13. Normative question

> Under what standard should the judgment or choice be evaluated?

Possible standards include:

- expected value;
- expected utility;
- probability theory;
- Bayesian coherence;
- calibration;
- dominance;
- consistency/transitivity;
- decision cost/benefit under constraints;
- ecological/resource rationality.

## 14. Normative standard must be specified before "bias"

Hahn's 2026 review of human rationality emphasizes that descriptive rationality research cannot be interpreted without normative considerations.

Therefore:

`response differs from textbook answer -> bias`

is unsafe until the relevant norm is established.

## 15. Normative pluralism is real

Different tasks can legitimately invoke different normative frameworks.

For example:

- maximizing long-run monetary return;
- maximizing subjective welfare;
- minimizing catastrophic downside;
- preserving option value;
- satisfying constraints.

These are not always equivalent.

## 16. Rationality is relative to objective and information set

A decision can be coherent for one goal but poor for another.

Therefore:

`same choice -> same quality for every decision-maker`

is invalid.

# III. Decision quality versus outcome quality

## 17. Good decision can have bad outcome

Under uncertainty, a high-quality decision can still lose.

Example structure:

- option A has better expected payoff;
- rare bad outcome occurs.

The outcome does not retroactively invalidate the decision rule.

## 18. Bad decision can have good outcome

A poor process can get lucky.

Therefore:

`good result -> good reasoning`

is invalid.

## 19. Outcome bias must not replace process evaluation

Decision evaluation should ask:

- information available ex ante;
- probability estimates;
- utilities/costs;
- decision rule;
- constraints.

## 20. Ex ante and ex post are different viewpoints

Post-outcome knowledge can create hindsight distortions.

DEC-02 will deepen decision-quality and forecasting evaluation.

# IV. Risk versus uncertainty

## 21. Risk

A useful operational definition:

outcomes and their probabilities are specified or sufficiently estimable.

## 22. Uncertainty / ambiguity

Probabilities may be:

- unknown;
- imprecise;
- contested;
- model-dependent.

## 23. Risk and ambiguity should not be collapsed

A 20% known probability differs from:

> somewhere between 5% and 40%, model uncertain.

## 24. Expected-utility theory is central but not exhaustive

Gilboa's 2025 state-of-the-science review describes expected utility as the dominant framework under uncertainty while emphasizing:

- robust empirical violations;
- open normative questions;
- competing models.

Therefore expected utility should be treated as a benchmark/model, not unquestionable psychological law.

# V. Expected value and expected utility

## 25. Expected value

For monetary outcomes:

`EV = Σ p_i x_i`.

It treats monetary units linearly.

## 26. Expected utility

`EU = Σ p_i u(x_i)`.

The utility function represents subjective/value-relevant consequences under specified assumptions.

## 27. Risk aversion != loss aversion

Risk aversion can arise from curvature of utility over outcomes.

Loss aversion concerns asymmetric weighting relative to a reference point.

Do not conflate them.

## 28. Expected utility is normative under assumptions

Its force depends on:

- preference axioms;
- outcome representation;
- probability representation;
- stationarity/context assumptions.

Violations can motivate alternative models without automatically proving irrationality.

---

# VI. Prospect theory / cumulative prospect theory

## 29. Core descriptive ingredients

Prospect-theory-family models commonly include:

1. reference-dependent valuation;
2. different value curvature for gains/losses;
3. probability weighting;
4. possible loss/gain asymmetry.

## 30. Reference dependence

Outcome value depends partly on change relative to a reference point rather than only final wealth/state.

## 31. Probability weighting

Decision weights need not equal objective probabilities.

Low probabilities can sometimes receive disproportionate weight and high probabilities less than proportional weight under fitted models.

## 32. Prospect theory has strong empirical foundations

A multinational direct replication:

- N = 4,098;
- 19 countries;
- 13 languages;

replicated 94% of original items and 12 of 13 key theoretical contrasts, though with heterogeneity and attenuation.

## 33. Replication does not make prospect theory complete

Observed choices can also be explained by:

- alternative risk models;
- sampling/noise;
- resource-rational models;
- threshold/need effects;
- context-sensitive reference points.

## 34. Model fit != mechanism

A prospect-theory parameter is:

- a model parameter;
- under a specified task/model.

It is not automatically:

- stable trait;
- neural mechanism;
- personality feature.

---

# VII. Loss aversion requires special caution

## 35. Loss aversion is not equivalent to "people hate losing"

Technical loss aversion refers to asymmetric valuation of equivalent losses and gains relative to a reference point.

## 36. Loss aversion is currently debated

A 2024 meta-analysis of fitted cumulative prospect theory parameters reported an average lambda around:

- 1.31;
- 95% CI [1.10, 1.53];

with limited high-quality datasets and substantial estimation issues.

## 37. 2025 re-meta-analysis challenged robustness

A re-analysis of a larger meta-analytic dataset concluded that loss aversion was not robust in some cleaner symmetric/unordered risky-choice subsets.

## 38. Therefore "losses loom twice as large" is not a universal constant

The classic ~2x slogan should not be used as a generic person-level law.

## 39. Estimated lambda is model-dependent

Recent 2026 work shows estimates can shift substantially across model specifications even with unchanged observed choices.

Therefore:

`lambda > 1 -> person psychologically weights losses more`

requires model-identification evidence.

## 40. Loss aversion can be context-sensitive

Context and domain can alter observed gain/loss asymmetries.

Trait inference requires cross-context reliability.

---

# VIII. Framing

## 41. Framing effect

Equivalent or near-equivalent decision problems can evoke different choices when represented as:

- gains;
- losses;
- survival;
- mortality;
- other reference-relative descriptions.

## 42. Framing is robust but heterogeneous

A 2025 meta-analysis found significant risky-choice framing effects with moderation by:

- problem domain;
- age.

## 43. 2026 replication evidence supports persistence

A recent replication found risky-choice framing effects persisted even when option descriptions were matched and complete.

## 44. Framing effect != one mechanism

Possible contributors include:

- reference points;
- attention;
- representation;
- linguistic/pragmatic interpretation;
- probability weighting;
- task demand;
- risk sensitivity.

## 45. Framing is not proof of irrationality by itself

Need to establish:

1. descriptions are genuinely extensionally equivalent;
2. no relevant pragmatic information differs;
3. normative invariance is appropriate.

# IX. Reference point

## 46. Reference point is not one fixed baseline

Possible reference points:

- status quo;
- expectation;
- aspiration;
- recent outcome;
- social comparison;
- minimum acceptable threshold.

## 47. Reference points can vary across people and contexts

Therefore two identical outcomes can be coded differently.

## 48. Reference-point model parameters are not personality essence

Reference-point theory and recent individual-difference work suggest person/context heterogeneity.

Do not infer stable global risk style from one task.

---

# X. Heuristic != bias

## 49. Heuristic

A heuristic is a simplified decision/inference strategy.

It can reduce:

- search;
- computation;
- memory demand.

## 50. Bias

"Bias" usually denotes systematic deviation from a specified benchmark.

The term does not by itself identify the process.

## 51. One bias pattern can arise from multiple mechanisms

Current probability-judgment models include:

- heuristic accounts;
- noisy representation;
- sampling models;
- Bayesian sampler;
- quantum probability;
- averaging/weighting models.

Therefore:

`conjunction fallacy -> representativeness mechanism proven`

is unsafe.

## 52. Adaptive heuristics exist

A heuristic can outperform a more complex strategy when:

- information is limited;
- environment is structured;
- noise is high;
- computation is costly;
- overfitting is a risk.

## 53. Heuristic use is neither automatically good nor bad

Evaluation must include:

- environment;
- objective;
- resource constraints;
- error cost.

---

# XI. Bounded/resource/ecological rationality

## 54. Bounded rationality

Human decision-makers have finite:

- time;
- attention;
- memory;
- computation.

## 55. Resource rationality

A strategy may be near-optimal after including cognitive cost.

## 56. Ecological rationality

A strategy can be effective because it matches environmental structure.

## 57. 2025 computational work formalizes this perspective

Recent bounded-rationality models explain some apparent anomalies as outcomes of limited-resource optimization.

## 58. But resource rationality can become unfalsifiable

A valid account must specify:

- resource limitation;
- objective;
- environmental distribution;
- quantitative predictions.

Never:

`bias exists -> hidden resource optimum`

without testable structure.

# XII. Base rates and Bayesian reasoning

## 59. Base rate

Prior prevalence/probability before case-specific evidence.

## 60. Likelihood

How expected the observed evidence is under competing hypotheses.

## 61. Posterior

Probability after combining prior/base rate and evidence under the model.

## 62. Base-rate neglect is not all-or-none

A broad 2022 study found base-rate underweighting generalized beyond classic low-base-rate problems but with large individual heterogeneity.

## 63. Individual strategies can differ qualitatively

In that study, one group nearly ignored base rates while another incorporated them strongly.

Therefore:

`people ignore base rates`

is an overgeneralization.

## 64. One group-level average can hide strategy mixtures

Population mean underweighting can be produced by:

- everyone mildly underweighting;
- mixture of Bayesian and non-Bayesian strategies.

These imply different mechanisms.

## 65. Bayesian norm requires correct problem representation

Before labeling a response non-Bayesian, verify:

- sample space;
- conditional probabilities;
- denominator;
- prior;
- independence assumptions.

# XIII. Probability representation matters

## 66. Equivalent probabilities can feel different across formats

Examples:

- 1%;
- 1 in 100;
- 10 in 1,000;
- "rare";
- icon array.

## 67. Verbal probabilities are ambiguous

"Rare", "likely", "unlikely" vary between people and contexts.

## 68. Relative risk can exaggerate perceived impact

Example:

50% relative reduction can correspond to:

- 2% -> 1%.

Absolute risk is needed for interpretation.

## 69. Current systematic-review evidence supports several communication rules

The 2025 Making Numbers Meaningful synthesis found stronger support for:

- part-to-whole visuals/icon arrays;
- consistent denominators;

and cautions against:

- verbal probability terms;
- 1-in-X formats;
- relative-risk-only framing.

## 70. No probability format is universally optimal

The same review explicitly concludes that no single visual/numerical format is best for every decision context.

## 71. Probability communication is an intervention

Changing format can alter:

- comprehension;
- perception;
- behavior.

Therefore format is not neutral.

# XIV. Calibration

## 72. Calibration

A probability forecaster is calibrated if events assigned probability p occur about p proportion of the time across comparable forecasts.

## 73. Calibration != discrimination

A forecaster can:

- be calibrated but uninformative;
- discriminate well but be systematically overconfident.

## 74. Confidence != probability of correctness by definition

Subjective confidence is a metacognitive judgment.

It may or may not be calibrated.

## 75. Overconfidence has multiple meanings

Common distinct phenomena:

- overestimation: estimated performance > actual performance;
- overplacement: believing oneself better than others;
- overprecision: intervals too narrow/confidence too high.

Do not collapse them.

## 76. Confidence can be learned and shifted

2025 experimental evidence shows confidence judgments update with feedback even when objective performance is unchanged.

Therefore confidence is dynamic.

## 77. Confidence measures need reliability evidence

A large-scale 2025 study directly investigated within/between-person reliability of confidence measures.

Stable person-level "confidence tendency" claims require psychometric evidence.

## 78. Collective forecasts can be calibrated in real-world settings

A 2026 PNAS study analyzed approximately one million resolved forecasts across:

- four platforms;
- twelve domains;
- time horizons from hours to years.

Aggregate forecast probabilities were broadly calibrated, with domain-specific miscalibration.

## 79. Collective calibration != individual calibration

Aggregation can improve performance.

Do not infer that each contributor is well calibrated.

# XV. Accuracy, calibration, and proper scoring

## 80. Binary correctness wastes probability information

A 51% and 99% prediction can both be "correct" after the event.

Their quality should differ.

## 81. Proper scoring rules reward honest probability estimates

Examples include:

- Brier score;
- logarithmic score.

## 82. Calibration is not enough for forecast quality

A constant 50% prediction in a balanced event set can be calibrated but not sharp/informative.

Need multiple metrics.

## 83. 2026 prediction-model guidance similarly separates

- calibration;
- discrimination;
- overall accuracy;
- clinical utility.

The conceptual distinction generalizes to human forecasts.

# XVI. Anchoring

## 84. Anchoring effect

An initial numeric/value reference can pull subsequent estimates.

## 85. Famous demonstrations should still be replicated

A 2025 replication/extension reproduced the classic factorial-expression order anchoring effect in 253 U.S. adults.

## 86. Anchor effect != anchoring trait

Magnitude depends on:

- task;
- anchor relevance;
- knowledge;
- adjustment;
- incentives/context.

## 87. Anchor can be informative

If an anchor legitimately contains information, moving toward it is not necessarily bias.

# XVII. Availability and representativeness

## 88. Availability-like judgments

Ease of retrieval/salience can influence judged frequency/probability.

## 89. Representativeness-like judgments

Similarity to a prototype can influence category/probability judgments.

## 90. These are descriptive constructs, not diagnosis

They should not become:

- stable cognitive defects;
- universal mechanisms.

## 91. Salience can be rationally informative in some environments

Events are often memorable because they are:

- frequent;
- severe;
- relevant.

The key question is whether retrieval accessibility tracks target probability in the current environment.

# XVIII. Confirmation bias / motivated search

## 92. "Confirmation bias" is not one mechanism

Selective evidence use can reflect:

- hypothesis testing strategy;
- search cost;
- asymmetric priors;
- motivated cognition;
- source availability;
- stopping rule.

## 93. Evidence-seeking quality depends on information value

MET-02 controls:

- sampling;
- source quality;
- stopping;
- revision.

## 94. Seeking confirming evidence is not always irrational

If the test is highly diagnostic or cheaper, confirmation-focused search can be rational.

Need expected information value.

# XIX. Dual-process language

## 95. Fast/intuitive and slow/deliberative are useful descriptive contrasts

But they are not two literal brain modules.

## 96. Modern dual-process theory is more nuanced

De Neys' 2025 framework separates deliberation functions:

- response control;
- response generation;
- justification;
- regulation.

## 97. Errors can arise in both rapid and deliberative processing

Clinical-reasoning and general cognition reviews challenge:

`System 1 = bias, System 2 = correction`.

## 98. Deliberation can introduce errors

More thought can:

- rationalize;
- overfit;
- overweight misleading evidence;
- amplify motivated reasoning.

## 99. Conflict between intuitions can trigger deliberation

2026 experimental evidence supports the idea that conflict between competing intuitive responses can recruit additional processing in a base-rate paradigm.

## 100. Therefore

Use:

- rapid/intuitive process;
- deliberative process;
- conflict detection;
- response revision.

Avoid:

- "System 1 caused it";
- "System 2 fixed it"

unless directly operationalized.

# XX. Risk preference is not one coherent trait

## 101. "Risk tolerance" varies by measure

Risk preference can be measured through:

- self-report propensity;
- behavioral gamble tasks;
- domain-specific behavior;
- frequency measures.

## 102. Large 2025 IPD meta-analysis

Across:

- 358 risk-preference measures;
- 33 panels;
- 57 samples;
- 579,114 respondents;

temporal stability and convergent validity varied substantially.

## 103. Behavioral risk measures often show lower stability

Self-reported propensity/frequency measures showed higher stability than behavioral measures on average.

## 104. Cross-measure agreement is low

This questions the idea that all "risk preference" measures capture one underlying stable trait.

## 105. Domain matters

Investment risk, health risk, occupational risk, alcohol-related risk etc. need not be identical.

## 106. Therefore

`risky choice on task -> globally risk-seeking person`

is unsafe.

# XXI. Framing, defaults, and choice architecture

## 107. Choice architecture

The arrangement/presentation of choices can alter behavior without removing options.

Examples:

- defaults;
- ordering;
- salience;
- reminders;
- information format.

## 108. Default effect

Preselected options can increase selection probability.

## 109. 2025 controlled experiments

Two experiments, N = 317, found:

- 38–39% chose the highlighted default;
- random benchmark = 25%.

Low winning probability increased default reliance.

## 110. Default effect is context-sensitive

Framing affected defaults inconsistently across the two studies.

This is evidence against one universal default mechanism.

## 111. Nudge effectiveness is heterogeneous

A large 2022 meta-analysis found:

- 200+ studies;
- 450+ effects;
- N > 2.1 million;
- average d ≈ .45;

but:

- extreme heterogeneity;
- publication bias.

## 112. Later evidence is more cautious

2025/2026 reviews/meta-analyses show:

- small effects in some applied domains;
- strong heterogeneity;
- publication bias sensitivity;
- some defaults/availability interventions outperform others.

## 113. Choice architecture is not necessarily "better decision-making"

It changes behavior.

Whether behavior is better depends on:

- welfare criterion;
- preferences;
- informed consent/autonomy;
- actual downstream outcome.

## 114. BHC/ETH own intervention ethics

DEC identifies decision influence.

ETH later evaluates AI/policy manipulation and autonomy boundaries.

# XXVI. Bias labels are not stable person diagnoses

## 128. Most named biases are task-defined effects

Examples:

- framing;
- anchoring;
- base-rate neglect;
- default effect.

## 129. Cross-task stability often requires separate evidence

Risk-preference measurement research demonstrates substantial low convergence across measures.

## 130. Person-level inference requires psychometrics

Before saying:

> this person is highly loss averse / risk seeking / overconfident

- repeated measures;
- reliability;
- cross-context transfer;
- construct validity;

## 131. Model parameters require recovery

A fitted risk/loss parameter can be unstable because of:

- sparse choices;
- parameter trade-offs;
- task design;

# XXVII. Decision-support interventions

## 132. Debiasing is not simply telling people "don't be biased"

Possible interventions:

- change representation;
- provide base rates;
- use natural frequencies;
- structured analytic procedure;
- decision aid;
- feedback/calibration training;
- defaults/choice architecture.

## 133. Debiasing effects vary by person

A 2025 base-rate training study found distinct groups:

- consistently biased;
- improved;
- consistently correct.

Therefore one intervention does not help everyone equally.

## 134. Representation change can outperform exhortation

If error arises from denominator/probability-format difficulty, changing the representation may be more effective than asking for "more rational thinking."

## 135. Decision aid quality requires outcome-specific validation

A tool can improve:

- knowledge;
- calibration;
- choice consistency;

without improving:

- long-term welfare.

Different targets need different evidence.

# XXVIII. Advice and social information

## 136. People often under/overweight advice relative to quality

A 2025 meta-analysis included:

- 346 effect sizes;
- 129 datasets;
- N = 17,296.

## 137. Perceived advisor quality influenced advice weight

Average adjustment increased when advisor quality was described as higher.

## 138. Perceived quality can be wrong

Therefore:

`followed advice more -> better decision`

does not follow.

## 139. Advice aggregation can improve judgment under some conditions

Especially if sources are:

- independent;
- calibrated;
- diverse.

Correlated sources can create false confidence.

## High-confidence practical boundaries

1. descriptive and normative decision questions are distinct;

3. decision quality and realized outcome quality are distinct;

13. probability format changes comprehension/perception;
14. calibration and discrimination are distinct;

`decision target -> normative benchmark -> probabilities/information -> utilities/reference point -> descriptive choice -> framing/heuristic/context -> confidence/calibration -> resource/ecological alternatives -> repeated measurement -> narrow conclusion`.

## Source-boundary note

Expected utility, prospect theory, Bayesian reasoning, bounded/resource rationality, and ecological rationality are treated as distinct normative/descriptive/explanatory frameworks rather than a single hierarchy.

Prospect-theory replication evidence supports robust risky-choice patterns across countries, but does not establish a unique cognitive mechanism or stable person-level parameters.

Loss-aversion evidence is explicitly contested. The 2024 meta-analysis and 2025 re-meta-analysis reach materially different conclusions; DEC-01 therefore does not use a universal fixed loss-aversion coefficient.

Risk-preference meta-analysis shows substantial heterogeneity and low cross-measure convergence; one behavioral task is not treated as a stable global trait measure.

Choice-architecture meta-analyses show real average effects alongside extreme heterogeneity and publication-bias concerns; behavior change is not equated with welfare improvement.

Collective forecasting calibration is not treated as evidence that individual forecasters are calibrated.

No source is used to infer any individual's rationality, risk preference, loss aversion, overconfidence, financial behavior, or decision competence.
