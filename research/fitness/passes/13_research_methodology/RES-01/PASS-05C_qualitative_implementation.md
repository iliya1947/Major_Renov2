# Part A — Qualitative questions

## 1. Qualitative synthesis asks about meaning, experience and process

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Qualitative evidence synthesis is designed to understand phenomena such as:
- experiences;
- perceptions;
- beliefs;
- meanings;
- barriers/facilitators;
- processes;
- contextual influences.

It does not primarily estimate an average treatment effect.

Project consequence:
do not force a qualitative question into intervention-effect PICO.

---

## 2. JBI PICo

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

JBI uses:

1. **P — Population / participants**
2. **I — Phenomenon of Interest**
3. **Co — Context**

for qualitative/textual review questions and eligibility.

### Project consequence

For a qualitative question, define:

- whose perspective/experience?
- what phenomenon?
- in what context?

Example:
> How do adults restarting home resistance training experience barriers to maintaining the program during the first 12 weeks?

P:
adults restarting training.

I:
experience of barriers/facilitators to maintenance.

Co:
home resistance training during restart phase.

---

## 3. Phenomenon of interest is not necessarily an intervention

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

JBI explicitly allows phenomena such as:
- experiences;
- expectations;
- social behaviour;
- processes;
- opinions.

Project consequence:
the “I” in PICo must not be interpreted as intervention by habit.

---

## 4. Context is substantive, not decorative

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

JBI and Cochrane qualitative guidance emphasize context because meaning and experience may depend on:
- setting;
- culture/subculture;
- social conditions;
- service/practice environment;
- local implementation conditions.

Project consequence:
qualitative findings from:
- supervised rehabilitation;
- commercial gym;
- unsupervised home training
may answer different questions even when the nominal phenomenon is “adherence”.

---

## 5. Qualitative question can evolve, but refinement must remain transparent

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Cochrane-Campbell QES guidance emphasizes that developing a good qualitative review question can require:
- conceptual refinement;
- theory/logic models;
- stakeholder input;
- reflection on reviewer assumptions.

Project rule:
qualitative scope may become more precise as concepts become clearer, but changes must be logged just like quantitative scope changes.

---

## 6. Qualitative synthesis is not automatically a barrier checklist

### Established finding / project inference
**Confidence: HIGH/MODERATE**

A qualitative question may seek:
- description;
- explanation;
- interpretation;
- conceptual relationships.

Reducing all qualitative evidence to a list of “barriers and facilitators” can discard:
- meaning;
- interaction;
- sequence;
- context.

Project consequence:
only use barrier/facilitator coding when it matches the actual decision.

# Part B — Implementation questions

## 7. “Does the intervention work?” and “Can it be implemented?” are different questions

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Implementation science distinguishes:

1. **clinical/service/client outcomes** — what the intervention ultimately does;
2. **implementation outcomes** — how successfully an innovation/practice is taken up and delivered.

Proctor et al. explicitly separate implementation outcomes from service and client outcomes.

Project exercise translation:

### Training efficacy/effectiveness outcome
- strength;
- hypertrophy;
- endurance.

### Implementation outcome
- acceptability;
- adoption;
- feasibility;
- fidelity;
- sustainability.

A program can be physiologically effective yet poorly implementable.

---

## 8. Implementation questions are not one single question type

### Project synthesis grounded in implementation-science frameworks
**Confidence: HIGH**

At least three distinct classes must be separated:

### A. Determinant question
> What helps or hinders implementation?

Examples:
- barriers;
- facilitators;
- contextual determinants.

### B. Implementation-outcome question
> How well is the innovation being implemented?

Examples:
- acceptability;
- adoption;
- feasibility;
- fidelity;
- sustainability.

### C. Implementation-strategy effect question
> Does strategy X improve implementation outcome Y compared with strategy Z/usual implementation?

This third type is a causal intervention-effect question and can legitimately use:
- PICO/PICOT;
- estimand;
but the outcomes are implementation outcomes.

---

## 9. Proctor implementation outcomes

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Proctor et al. propose eight implementation outcomes:

1. acceptability;
2. adoption;
3. appropriateness;
4. feasibility;
5. fidelity;
6. implementation cost;
7. penetration;
8. sustainability.

### Critical project rule

Do not treat these labels as synonyms.

For example:
- “people liked the program” ≠ feasibility;
- “people started it” ≠ fidelity;
- “people completed it” may overlap with adherence/retention but does not automatically establish sustainability.

Operational definitions must be explicit.

---

## 10. Referent must be explicit

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Proctor emphasizes that implementation outcomes require a clear **referent**:
what exactly is being rated/evaluated?

Possible referents:
- the innovation/program;
- implementation strategy;
- implementation effort/system.

Project example:
“acceptability” could refer to:
- the workout itself;
- the tracking system;
- reminders;
- coaching;
- the entire delivery package.

Without a referent, the outcome is ambiguous.

---

## 11. Level of analysis must be explicit

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Implementation outcomes can exist at different levels:
- individual recipient;
- deliverer/coach;
- group/team;
- organization/system.

Exercise-project consequence:
“adoption” by a trainee and “adoption” by a gym/coach are not the same unit of analysis.

For a one-person program, many organizational levels may be irrelevant, but the field must still be named rather than silently ignored.

---

## 12. Determinants are not implementation outcomes

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

CFIR is primarily a determinant framework:
it organizes factors that can influence implementation across domains such as:
- innovation;
- outer setting;
- inner setting;
- individuals;
- implementation process.

A determinant such as:
- complexity;
- available resources;
- motivation
is not itself the same thing as an outcome such as:
- feasibility;
- fidelity;
- sustainability.

Project consequence:
do not report “complexity improved” as if it were automatically implementation success.

---

## 13. CFIR must be operationalized to the project

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Current CFIR guidance explicitly says the framework should be operationalized before use:
- define what each domain refers to in the project;
- replace broad construct language with project-specific language where needed;
- add salient constructs if necessary.

Project consequence:
do not dump all CFIR constructs into every training question.

Use only constructs relevant to the specific implementation problem.

---

## 14. Implementation phase matters

### Established finding / framework-consistent rule
**Confidence: HIGH/MODERATE**

Implementation questions differ by phase:

1. exploration;
2. preparation;
3. active implementation;
4. sustainment.

Different outcomes/determinants become salient at different stages.

Exercise example:
- before program start: acceptability/appropriateness;
- during first weeks: feasibility/fidelity;
- months later: sustainability.

Project consequence:
record implementation phase/time horizon.

# Part C — Question frameworks

## 15. Qualitative experience/process question

Use **PICo**:

1. Population;
2. Phenomenon of Interest;
3. Context.

Add when relevant:
- perspective/actor;
- time/phase.

---

## 16. Implementation determinant question

No single universal acronym was identified in the checked sources.

### Project framework: **ID-CAP**

For internal use only:

1. **I — Innovation/practice being implemented**
2. **D — Determinant question**
3. **C — Context/setting**
4. **A — Actors / relevant roles**
5. **P — Phase of implementation**

Then specify:
- barrier/facilitator domain;
- expected direction if causal inference is attempted.

This is explicitly project-specific.

CFIR may be used to organize determinants after the question is defined.

---

## 17. Implementation outcome question

### Project framework: **IO-CARP**

For internal use only:

1. **I — Innovation / referent**
2. **O — Implementation outcome**
3. **C — Context**
4. **A — Actor / unit of analysis**
5. **R — Reference/comparator or threshold**, if meaningful
6. **P — Phase/time**

Examples:
- Is a home workout plan acceptable to target users during onboarding?
- What proportion adopt the tracking procedure within week 1?
- Is prescribed effort delivered with adequate fidelity over 8 weeks?

This is a project engineering framework based on Proctor-style outcome distinctions.

---

## 18. Implementation-strategy effect question

If the question is:

> Does strategy A improve implementation outcome Y versus strategy B?

Use ordinary intervention methodology:

- PICO/PICOT;
- estimand;
- implementation outcome as O;
- implementation context/actor clearly specified.

Example:
> Do automated reminders vs no reminders increase workout completion fidelity over 12 weeks?

This is a causal effect question about an implementation strategy.

---

## 19. Qualitative implementation question can still use PICo

Example:
> How do trainees describe reasons that reminders become irritating or helpful during home training?

Use PICo because the target is:
- experience/meaning/process,
not a numeric implementation effect.

CFIR can later be used as an analytic framework if justified, but it should not replace the review question.

---

## 20. Implementation failure vs intervention failure

### Established finding
**Confidence: HIGH**

Proctor's framework is explicitly motivated by the need to distinguish:
- an intervention that is ineffective;
from
- an effective intervention that was implemented poorly.

Project consequence:
when a training program “doesn't work”, later analysis must ask separately:

1. Was the intended physiological stimulus ineffective?
2. Was the program not performed as intended?
3. Was it not adopted/sustained?
4. Was measurement inadequate?

This distinction is foundational for eventual personalized training.

---

## 21. Practical rules produced by PASS-05C

1. Use PICo for qualitative experience/meaning/process questions.
2. Treat context as part of the substantive question.
3. Do not interpret the “I” in PICo as intervention by default.
4. Do not reduce all qualitative evidence to barrier/facilitator lists.
5. Separate implementation questions into:
   - determinants;
   - implementation outcomes;
   - effects of implementation strategies.
6. Keep implementation outcomes separate from clinical/training outcomes.
7. For implementation outcomes, define:
   - referent;
   - actor/unit of analysis;
   - phase/time;
   - context.
8. CFIR is a determinant framework, not an outcome taxonomy and not a universal question template.
9. Operationalize CFIR rather than applying every construct mechanically.
10. Strategy-effect questions return to PICO/PICOT + estimand.
11. Qualitative implementation questions can remain PICo.
12. Distinguish intervention failure from implementation failure.
13. ID-CAP and IO-CARP are project-specific internal frameworks, not external standards.
