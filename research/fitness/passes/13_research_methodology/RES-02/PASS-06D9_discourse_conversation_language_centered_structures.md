# 2. Canonical language-centered principle

Many qualitative approaches treat language primarily as a route to:

- experience;
- perception;
- meaning;
- memory;
- views.

Language-centered approaches instead ask how language itself operates.

Core question:

**What does this talk/text/discourse do, how is it organized, what realities or identities does it construct, and what social actions or power relations does it enable?**

### Critical rule

Do not read language automatically as a transparent window into an inner mental state.

A speaker saying:

"I am not making excuses, but..."

can be analyzed not only for the belief being expressed, but also for:

- accountability management;
- stake;
- identity work;
- action orientation;
- anticipation of criticism.

---

# 3. Discourse analysis is a family, not one method

The label discourse analysis covers materially different traditions.

For RES-02, major families include:

1. Discursive Psychology (DP);
2. Critical Discursive Psychology (CDP);
3. Foucauldian Discourse Analysis (FDA);
4. Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA), including Fairclough-type approaches;
5. Conversation Analysis (CA) as a distinct but related interaction-centered tradition;
6. other linguistic/semiotic discourse traditions.

### Project rule

Never store only:

design = discourse analysis.

Record exact approach and inferential target.

---

# 4. Conversation Analysis: canonical target

CA studies the organization of social action in talk-in-interaction.

It asks:

- how turns are allocated?;
- how actions are recognized?;
- how sequences unfold?;
- how misunderstandings are repaired?;
- how participants display understanding?;
- how institutional roles are enacted moment by moment?

The unit is not simply:

what topics people talked about.

It is:

how action is accomplished sequentially in interaction.

---

# 5. Naturally occurring interaction

Canonical CA strongly favors recorded interaction that would have occurred without the researcher eliciting it for the study.

Examples:

- coach-athlete feedback session;
- clinician-patient consultation;
- team meeting;
- training-floor instruction;
- peer coaching;
- rehabilitation session.

Why?

Because the analytic target is participants' own real-time interactional organization.

### Critical rule

Naturally occurring does not mean researcher-free or context-free.

Recording, consent and researcher presence can still affect the setting.

But the interaction itself is not primarily produced as an answer to a research interview.

---

# 6. Interview data boundary in CA

Research interviews can themselves be analyzed interactionally.

But then the object is:

the interview interaction.

The researcher cannot treat the interviewer as an invisible neutral prompt.

Questions shape:

- answer format;
- topic;
- accountability;
- turn allocation;
- sequence.

### Rule

Interview-as-data and interview-as-transparent-content are different analytic uses.

---

# 7. Turn-taking

Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson showed that conversation is systematically organized through locally managed turn-taking.

Relevant phenomena:

- turn construction;
- transition-relevance places;
- self-selection;
- next-speaker selection;
- overlap;
- gap;
- interruption;
- continuation.

### Fitness example

In coach feedback:

- who gets to speak?;
- who selects the next speaker?;
- does coach question format restrict the athlete's response?;
- can athlete initiate topics?;
- who closes the sequence?

These are interactional structures, not simply "communication style".

---

# 8. Sequence organization

Actions occur in sequential contexts.

Examples:

- question → answer;
- invitation → acceptance/refusal;
- assessment → agreement/disagreement;
- instruction → compliance/resistance;
- correction → acknowledgment.

A turn gets much of its meaning from:

- what came before;
- what comes next;
- what response it makes relevant.

### Critical rule

Extracting one sentence from its sequence can destroy the action being analyzed.

---

# 9. Repair

Repair concerns trouble in:

- speaking;
- hearing;
- understanding.

CA distinguishes:

- repair initiation;
- repair solution/correction;
- self- vs other-initiation;
- self- vs other-repair.

### Fitness example

Coach:
"Keep your knee out."

Athlete:
"Out, like toward the rack?"

The second turn displays a possible understanding problem.

How repair unfolds can reveal:

- instructional clarity;
- expertise asymmetry;
- embodied reference;
- interactional authority.

---

# 10. Action formation and action ascription

CA asks how a turn is built and understood as an action such as:

- request;
- instruction;
- challenge;
- assessment;
- complaint;
- invitation;
- reassurance;
- correction.

### Critical rule

Sentence form and social action are not identical.

A declarative can function as an instruction.

A question can function as criticism.

Analysis must use sequential evidence for how participants themselves treated the action.

---

# 11. Participant orientation

A central CA discipline is to avoid importing analyst categories unless participants demonstrably orient to them.

Example:

Rather than assuming:

coach dominates because coach has formal power,

CA asks whether dominance/control is displayed through:

- turn allocation;
- topic control;
- sequence closure;
- interruption;
- question-answer architecture;
- asymmetrical rights to evaluate.

### Rule

Analyst interpretation should be grounded in interactional evidence.

---

# 12. CA transcription requirements

Ordinary verbatim transcripts often remove interactional features that CA treats as data.

Jeffersonian-style transcription preserves details such as:

- timed pauses;
- micropause;
- overlap;
- latching;
- elongation;
- stress;
- volume;
- pitch shifts;
- cut-offs;
- laughter;
- in/out-breaths;
- speech rate.

### Critical rule

For CA, transcription detail is not cosmetic.

If the claim depends on:

- overlap;
- timing;
- repair;
- turn completion;

those features must be recoverable from recording/transcription.

---

# 13. Audio vs video

Audio can support many CA questions.

Video becomes important when action depends on:

- gaze;
- gesture;
- body orientation;
- equipment;
- pointing;
- embodied demonstration;
- movement timing.

### Fitness relevance

Exercise coaching is often multimodal.

An instruction such as:

"there"

may only be intelligible with:

- gesture;
- gaze;
- barbell position;
- athlete movement.

### Rule

Language-centered does not mean speech-only when the interaction is embodied.

---

# 14. CA sampling and generalization

CA often builds collections of recurrent interactional phenomena.

Logic:

single instance
→ identify candidate practice
→ search additional cases
→ compare variants/deviant instances
→ specify interactional organization.

This differs from probability sampling.

### Critical rule

Frequency alone is not the main CA validity criterion.

The goal is to demonstrate a systematic practice and how participants orient to it.

---

# 15. Discursive Psychology (DP): canonical target

DP treats psychological matters as things people construct and manage in discourse.

Rather than treating talk as a direct report of:

- memory;
- belief;
- attitude;
- motive;
- emotion;

DP asks how these categories are built and used in interaction.

Core principles include:

1. discourse is constructed;
2. discourse is constructive;
3. discourse is action-oriented;
4. discourse is situated.

---

# 16. DP and social action

DP asks:

What is the speaker accomplishing with this version?

Examples:

- justifying;
- blaming;
- excusing;
- resisting blame;
- establishing credibility;
- presenting an account as factual;
- managing stake/interest;
- constructing identity.

### Fitness example

Athlete says:

"I am not lazy, I just don't train when I know recovery is bad."

A descriptive study may code:

recovery barrier.

DP may examine:

how the speaker protects a disciplined-athlete identity while accounting for non-training.

Both analyses can be legitimate, but they answer different questions.

---

# 17. DP and naturalistic data

Contemporary DP strongly values records of actual interaction because these expose action in context.

Interview data can be used, but open-ended interviews introduce researcher-designed topics and accountability structures.

### Project rule

For DP, record:

- naturalistic vs elicited data;
- interactional context;
- researcher/interviewer contribution;
- sequential analysis depth.

---

# 18. Interpretative repertoires

Potter/Wetherell-derived discourse analysis introduced interpretative repertoires.

These are relatively recognizable, culturally available ways of describing or framing an issue.

Example fitness repertoires:

- training as discipline;
- exercise as health duty;
- rest as weakness;
- body as project;
- genetics as limit;
- pain as proof of effort.

### Critical rule

Repertoire ≠ theme.

A repertoire is a culturally available way of constructing an object that can be flexibly deployed for action.

---

# 19. Subject positions

Discourses/repertoires make certain identities and rights available.

Examples:

- serious lifter;
- lazy beginner;
- disciplined athlete;
- fragile patient;
- expert coach;
- non-compliant client.

Subject positions can carry:

- rights;
- duties;
- expectations;
- moral accountability.

### Rule

Identity in discourse analysis is not necessarily treated as a fixed inner trait.

It can be interactionally/discursively produced.

---

# 20. Ideological dilemmas

Critical discursive work often examines tensions among culturally available common-sense positions.

Fitness examples:

- push through discomfort vs listen to your body;
- discipline vs self-care;
- autonomy vs obey the coach;
- body acceptance vs body optimization.

Speakers can move between apparently contradictory repertoires without this being treated simply as inconsistency/error.

---

# 21. Critical Discursive Psychology (CDP)

CDP combines:

- close attention to situated language/action;
- broader attention to cultural discourse, subject positions and power.

It often uses:

- interpretative repertoires;
- subject positions;
- ideological dilemmas;
- discursive devices;
- contextual power analysis.

### Critical rule

CDP should not be collapsed into either:

pure micro-CA
or
pure macro-Foucauldian discourse analysis.

Its value is the micro–macro linkage.

---

# 22. Foucauldian Discourse Analysis (FDA)

FDA asks how discourses make particular objects, subjects and forms of conduct thinkable and governable.

Typical analytic targets:

- discursive constructions;
- available discourses;
- subject positions;
- practices enabled/limited;
- power;
- normalization;
- discipline;
- governmentality;
- technologies of self;
- resistance.

### Example

"Obesity" or "fitness" can be analyzed as discursively constructed objects linked to:

- responsibility;
- morality;
- surveillance;
- expertise;
- self-management.

---

# 23. FDA and power

Power is not only treated as something one actor possesses and imposes.

Foucauldian approaches often treat power as:

- productive;
- relational;
- diffuse;
- embedded in practices and knowledge.

### Fitness example

A fitness discourse can produce the "responsible healthy subject" who monitors:

- weight;
- steps;
- calories;
- training consistency.

The analysis asks how these norms make certain identities/actions normal and others deviant.

---

# 24. Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA)

CDA is a family explicitly concerned with language, society, ideology, inequality and power.

Fairclough-type CDA treats discourse through linked levels:

1. text;
2. discursive practice;
3. social practice.

Analysis may examine:

- vocabulary;
- grammar;
- modality;
- agency;
- nominalization;
- intertextuality;
- genre;
- production/consumption;
- institutional/social context.

### Critical rule

CDA is not just identifying negative words or politically charged themes.

Textual features must be connected to discourse/social practice and power.

---

# 25. FDA vs Fairclough-style CDA

They overlap in attention to discourse and power but differ in analytic traditions.

## FDA

Typically foregrounds:

- what objects can be spoken of;
- subject positions;
- normalization;
- power/knowledge;
- historical/discursive conditions.

May have no single standardized linguistic procedure.

## Fairclough-style CDA

More explicitly integrates:

- detailed linguistic/textual analysis;
- discursive practice;
- broader social practice.

### Project rule

Preserve the exact discourse tradition rather than coding all critical discourse work as one method.

---

# 26. CA vs discourse analysis

## CA

Primary target:
- sequential organization of interaction.

Strongest evidence:
- recordings of actual interaction;
- fine transcription;
- participant orientation;
- collections of interactional practices.

## DP

Primary target:
- discourse as situated social action, including psychological constructions.

## FDA/CDA

Primary target:
- wider discourses, subjectivity, ideology, institutions and power.

### Critical rule

Micro and macro discourse approaches answer different scientific questions.

---

# 27. Interview-analysis boundary

The same interview can be analyzed in different ways.

## QD

What barriers did participant report?

## Phenomenology

What is the lived meaning of the experience?

## Narrative

How is experience organized into a story?

## DP

What social action does this account accomplish?

## FDA

What discourse makes this identity/action thinkable?

## CDA

How does the text reproduce or resist wider power/ideological relations?

### Rule

Data source does not determine methodology.

---

# 28. Naturally occurring vs elicited data

## Naturally occurring

Would exist independently of the research prompt.

Examples:
- coaching session;
- team meeting;
- consultation;
- online forum conversation.

## Elicited

Created through:
- research interview;
- focus group;
- prompted written response.

### Critical rule

Elicited data are not invalid for discourse research.

But the researcher must not pretend the interviewer/question has no constitutive role.

---

# 29. Documents and visual/semiotic data

Discourse analysis can use more than spoken language.

Sources can include:

- policy;
- training manuals;
- websites;
- advertisements;
- social media;
- posters;
- gym graffiti;
- signage;
- videos;
- images;
- institutional documents.

### Fitness example

CrossFit signage can be analyzed for how masculinity and legitimate bodies are constructed.

Gym graffiti can be analyzed as part of the discursive construction of fitness.

---

# 30. What language-centered evidence can establish

Depending on approach, it can strongly support claims about:

- interactional practices;
- turn/sequence organization;
- how actions are accomplished;
- accountability;
- identity construction;
- interpretative repertoires;
- subject positions;
- cultural common sense;
- discursive normalization;
- institutional language practices;
- ideology/power relations in discourse;
- resistance/counter-discourse.

---

# 31. What it cannot establish by itself

Language-centered qualitative evidence alone does not establish:

- prevalence;
- incidence;
- population effect size;
- intervention efficacy;
- biological mechanism;
- diagnostic accuracy;
- prediction performance.

### Additional critical boundary

Discourse analysis cannot automatically infer:

> speaker's true private belief.

Because the methodology often treats talk as situated action rather than transparent cognition.

---

# 32. CA claim boundary

CA can show:

- how an interactional practice works;
- how participants orient to it;
- recurrent sequential patterns.

It does not automatically show:

- why a person psychologically behaved that way;
- what they privately believed;
- population frequency outside the sampled interactional corpus.

---

# 33. CDA/FDA claim boundary

CDA/FDA can show how discourse:

- constructs objects/subjects;
- makes practices legitimate;
- relates to ideology/power;
- enables/constrains subject positions.

It does not automatically prove:

- how every individual experiences that discourse;
- actual behavioral effect size;
- historical causation without appropriate evidence.

# 35. Sport exemplar: coach-athlete feedback

Groom, Cushion and Nelson recorded six video-based performance-feedback sessions in elite youth soccer across a 10-month season.

Conversation-analytic work showed interactional control through:

- asymmetrical turn allocation;
- unequal speaking opportunities;
- topic control;
- question-based speaker selection.

Methodological lesson:

"coach power" was not inferred only from formal job title.

It was demonstrated through sequential organization of interaction.

---

# 36. Sport exemplar: coaching as action-in-context

Conversation-analysis work in sports coaching argues that coaching should be studied as situated action rather than reconstructed only through later interview accounts.

Project value:

For questions about:

- instruction;
- correction;
- feedback;
- athlete response;
- decision-making in interaction;

recorded practice can answer things retrospective self-report cannot.

# 37. Gym discourse exemplar

Ethno-discursive work in gym settings has examined how men negotiate dominant masculinity discourses.

This illustrates a layered architecture:

- ethnographic/contextual observation;
- discourse analysis;
- critical discursive psychology.

### Rule

Multiple methodologies can be combined, but each layer must remain explicit.

# 39. Do-not-confuse matrix

| Do not confuse | Why |
|---|---|
| language data vs discourse methodology | many qualitative designs use language |
| interview vs discourse analysis | interview is only a data-generation format |
| discourse analysis vs one method | multiple incompatible traditions exist |
| CA vs generic discourse analysis | CA has a distinct sequential interactional architecture |
| topic coding vs CA | CA studies action organization, not just topics |
| verbatim transcript vs CA-ready transcript | timing/overlap/prosody may be essential |
| single quote vs interactional action | sequence context is required |
| researcher category vs participant orientation | CA requires evidence participants orient to the relevance |
| DP vs attitude measurement | DP analyzes construction/action, not hidden stable attitude |
| repertoire vs theme | repertoire is a culturally available way of constructing an object |
| subject position vs personality trait | discursive identity position is contextually available/enacted |
| FDA vs Fairclough CDA | related but distinct critical traditions |
| CDA vs negative-language coding | language must be tied to social practice/power |
| power analysis vs proof of behavioral effect | discourse power is not an effect-size estimate |
| narrated/private belief vs discursive action | talk need not transparently reveal cognition |
| naturally occurring vs researcher-free | recording/research context can still matter |
| elicited data vs invalid discourse data | valid if researcher role is analytically acknowledged |
| discourse prevalence vs population prevalence | qualitative corpus does not support statistical frequency |
| coach authority vs demonstrated interactional control | formal role and interactional practice are distinct |
| language-centered explanation vs causal intervention effect | different inferential targets |

# 40. Confidence

## HIGH

Well-supported:

- discourse analysis is a family rather than one method;
- CA targets sequential organization of talk-in-interaction;
- turn-taking, sequence organization, repair and action formation are core CA domains;
- CA strongly favors detailed recordings/transcripts and participant-orientation evidence;
- DP treats discourse as constructed, constructive, action-oriented and situated;
- interpretative repertoires and subject positions belong to discursive traditions rather than ordinary thematic coding;
- FDA/CDA foreground discourse, subjectivity/power/ideology at broader levels;
- Fairclough-type CDA links text, discursive practice and social practice;
- language-centered qualitative evidence does not itself estimate prevalence or causal effect.

## MODERATE-HIGH

Project routing choices:

- unified CA/DP/CDP/FDA/CDA decision architecture;
- exact threshold for labeling interview-based work as DP rather than broader discourse analysis;
- standardized extraction router;
- boundary between CDP and other synthetic micro–macro discourse approaches.

Reason:

the discourse-analysis field is theoretically plural and terminology overlaps substantially across disciplines.

2. Discourse analysis is a family and must be subtyped.
