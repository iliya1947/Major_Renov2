# WOM-06.1 — Menstrual cycle, hormone sensitivity and psychophysiological measurement

Date: 2026-11-26. Status: bounded block COMPLETE_FOR_SPECIFIED_SCOPE (internal reasoning check only); WOM-06 IN_PROGRESS.

## 1. Question and dependency gate
How can reproductive physiology be incorporated as a candidate contextual contributor to affect, cognition and behavior without inferring a person's hormone status or intentions from behavior? Reopened SPQ-00 and WOM-05.3 in full and retained prior construct separation and individual transfer rules. This first block is restricted to cycling adults, hormonal contraception as an exposure, and the distinction between ordinary variation and clinical hormone sensitivity. Later reproductive transitions require a separate block. Claim types: physiological description, measured association, limited experimental causal inference, measurement validity. Competing explanations include physical symptoms, sleep, stress, concurrent events, medications, stable individual differences, response expectancy and measurement artifacts.

## 2. Evidence ledger
Targeted public searches on 2026-11-26: menstrual cycle prospective daily mood review; hormone assays and negative affect over two cycles; menstrual-cycle cognitive performance meta-analysis; cycle phasing recommendations; daily hormone and well-being study; PMDD suppression/add-back experiment; randomized contraception trials and contradictory observational estimates. Selection: directly relevant primary studies and quantitative or methodological syntheses with complementary designs and null evidence. Not a systematic review. No full article read end-to-end; explicit source access below.

E1. Schmalenberger et al. (2021), How to study the menstrual cycle: Practical tools and recommendations. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2020.104895 . Indexed author/publisher methodology extracts inspected; direct PMC and publisher opens blocked. Recommendations distinguish calendar counting, bleeding, LH tests, basal body temperature and hormone assays; ovulation-related phasing needs individual evidence. A regular-looking bleed does not prove ovulation. Sampling strategy, demand characteristics, contraceptive status and phase definitions matter. A methodological paper, not an estimate of psychological effect magnitude.

E2. Romans et al. (2012), Mood and the menstrual cycle: a review of prospective data studies. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.genm.2012.07.003 . PubMed abstract methods/results directly inspected. Review of 47 studies in non-help-seeking samples with prospective daily mood data across at least one cycle: 18 found no phase association, 18 found premenstrual plus another phase association, seven found premenstrual-only negative mood, four found other-phase associations. This is a study-count summary, not a pooled effect or a percentage of women affected. Older English-language literature with heterogeneous methods; it does not rule out individual cyclic symptoms.

E3. Hengartner et al. (2017), Negative affect is unrelated to fluctuations in hormone levels across the menstrual cycle: Evidence from a multisite observational study across two successive cycles. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28712426/ . Indexed author abstract and publisher-linked extracts inspected; direct PubMed access blocked. Serum assays and PANAS negative affect at four phase-related visits, 87 participants in cycle one, 67 in cycle two. No consistent association reported. Sparse visits may miss brief changes; attrition, uncertainty and subgroup power not independently quantified. Counterevidence to a universal hormone-negative-affect relation, not equivalence.

E4. Jang, Zhang & Elfenbein (2025), Menstrual cycle effects on cognitive performance: A meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0318576 . Publisher abstract, selected discussion and supplementary table headings directly inspected. 102 articles, 3,943 participants, 730 comparisons across cognitive domains. No robust systematic phase differences in objective task performance, speed or accuracy; nominal spatial findings not supported by robust phase ascertainment. Small samples, variable phase definitions and multiple comparisons limit inference; not a test of every individual symptom or clinical subgroup. Do not equate subjective mental effort with task accuracy.

E5. Doornweerd & Gerritsen (2025), 28 days later: A prospective daily study on psychological well-being across the menstrual cycle and the effects of hormones and oral contraceptives. https://doi.org/10.1017/S003329172400357X . Publisher abstract, measures, analysis excerpts and discussion/limitations directly inspected. 22 naturally cycling and 18 oral contraceptive participants, 28 daily measurements of self-reported well-being and saliva hormones (estradiol, progesterone, testosterone). Some phase-specific associations and differences in variability between groups; associations with hormone person-means more frequent than those with within-person changes. Small student sample, single 28-day window, calendar phase coding without direct LH confirmation, possible incomplete cycles and multiple outcomes. Nonrandomized contraceptive comparison cannot identify treatment effects; the distinct outcomes and sampling frequency prevent treating E3 and E5 as exact contradictions. Association is not a proven hormonal mechanism.

E6. Schmidt et al. (2017), Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder Symptoms Following Ovarian Suppression: Triggered by Change in Ovarian Steroid Levels But Not Continuous Stable Levels. https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2017.16101113 . Indexed author abstract/full-text extracts inspected; direct publisher access denied. In a selected group of 12 PMDD participants responding to ovarian suppression and subsequently receiving combined estradiol/progesterone add-back, symptoms recurred at initial exposure and were not sustained at stable levels. Evidence for sensitivity to change in this selected clinical population, not a universal response or established molecular pathway. Selection of responders and small sample restrict generalization. Not diagnostic guidance or a recommendation to manipulate hormones.

E7. de Wit et al. (2021), Hormonal contraceptive use and depressive symptoms: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised trials. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34099098/ ; publisher HTML https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-open/article/hormonal-contraceptive-use-and-depressive-symptoms-systematic-review-and-network-metaanalysis-of-randomised-trials/8AE2BA5FAB4E21ADB832C16F9E18A2B1 . Indexed abstract and author result extracts inspected; direct PubMed open blocked. 12 trials in network, 5,833 participants, no average worsening relative to placebo; median SMD -0.04 across comparisons. Estimates uncertain; young first-time users and individual adverse responses are not ruled out. No regimen advice.

E8. Association of oral contraceptives with depression symptoms, diagnosis, and treatment in healthy women: A meta-analysis (2026). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42061049/ . Indexed abstract only; direct open cache miss. Five studies with depression diagnosis outcome (1,607,461 participants) reported RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.07–1.61, I2 94.18%. Observed association with substantial heterogeneity, not necessarily a causal effect. Designs and adjustment details not independently verified, supporting evidence only. Contrast E7 and E8 by outcome (symptom score versus diagnosis), exposure, follow-up, selection, confounding and trial eligibility. Do not force agreement or assume one result invalidates the other.

## 3. Operational distinctions and model
In an ovulatory cycle, estradiol generally rises before ovulation and progesterone rises after ovulation; both typically fall before the next menses if pregnancy does not occur. Individual trajectories vary. Bleeding, ovulation, calendar day, circulating hormone concentration, rate of change, receptor/neural sensitivity, symptoms, and functional outcome are distinct. Hormonal contraception changes endocrine exposure in formulation-dependent ways; users are not interchangeable with naturally cycling individuals. Peripheral hormone measurements do not directly measure local neural receptor activity.

For repeated observations distinguish:
- between-person association: individual mean hormone level versus individual mean outcome;
- within-person association: deviation from own mean versus outcome;
- change/rate and lagged exposure versus outcome;
- person-specific sensitivity: heterogeneous slopes, potentially dependent on context or phase.
A group slope, even if estimated within persons, does not identify every person's slope. An illustrative model y_it = a_i + b_i(H_it - mean_H_i) + c*mean_H_i + d*X_it + error_it separates within- from between-person variation but does not identify causation. Time-varying confounding, serial dependence, nonlinear timing, measurement error, missingness and multiple testing require treatment; some contextual variables can be mediators rather than confounders.

Analytic rules:
1. Define the outcome: subjective fatigue, pain, affect, performance, desire or observable action. Never infer one from another automatically.
2. Establish voluntarily available measured exposure or keep endocrine status unknown. Do not infer phase, contraception, pregnancy or diagnosis from a person's social behavior.
3. Compare naturally occurring repeated patterns with ordinary baseline, relevant symptoms, sleep, stress, context and exposure timing; check repeatability across cycles. A single coincident episode is low-specificity evidence.
4. Separate direct endocrine effects, indirect effects through symptoms or sleep, stable vulnerability, contextual stress and combinations. Distinguish plausible mechanism from individually identified causal explanation.
5. Weigh replicated null and positive results by population, design, measurement and precision. Clinical experimental findings cannot establish a general-population effect.
6. Do not discount expressed preferences, credibility, choices or consent on physiological grounds. No surveillance, covert cycle tracking, pressure, diagnostic labeling or medication recommendations. Clinically significant symptoms belong to qualified clinical evaluation.

## 4. Internal synthetic transfer checks
Rubric defined for this block: construct separation, competing explanation, distinguishing evidence and inference limit. Critical failure: deterministic group-to-individual attribution, unsupported diagnosis, coercion, causal inflation, ignoring contrary evidence or privacy breach. Original neutral cases, not independent or held-out.

A. A participant reports fatigue on two days of a nominal cycle phase but objective accuracy is stable. Response: separate fatigue from performance; pain, sleep and workload are alternatives; compare repeated voluntarily recorded outcomes and verified timing. No cognitive deficit established.
B. A study finds a positive association between average hormone level and average well-being, but none within persons. Response: between-person association is not a within-person causal effect; stable confounding, sampling and measurement remain possible. Seek repeated measurements with individual centering. No inference that raising hormone level improves mood.
C. A clinically selected subgroup responds to hormone change during an experiment. Response: experimental evidence pertains to selected participants and intervention; individual heterogeneity and selection restrict transfer. Need independent evidence for other populations. No diagnosis or universal mechanism.
D. A group-level trial reports null mean change but an observational cohort reports more diagnoses. Response: distinguish outcomes, timing, selection, confounding and precision. Both results may coexist. Need compatible estimands and subgroup data; no individual guarantee.
E. A phase-specific correlation appears in one cycle but not the next. Response: examine reliability, context and chance/multiple comparisons; preserve candidate conditional effect but downgrade universal claim. No retrospective rescue by assigning an unmeasured hormone change.
