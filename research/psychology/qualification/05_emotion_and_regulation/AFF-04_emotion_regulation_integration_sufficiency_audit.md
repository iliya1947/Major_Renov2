# AFF-04 — Emotion and regulation integration/sufficiency audit: generation, appraisal, expression, regulation, dynamics, and measurement

Status: **COMPLETE_FOR_CURRENT_SCOPE — 20/20 PASS**
**Domain decision: AFF sequence COMPLETE_FOR_CURRENT_SCOPE (AFF-01…04).**
Evidence cutoff: **2026-11-19**.
Scope: public affective-science evidence and generic inference methodology only; no individualized psychological material.

## 1. Purpose and inputs

This block audits the practical sufficiency and internal consistency of:

1. **AFF-01** — affect, emotion, mood, valence/arousal, appraisal, expression, perception, interoception, and measurement;
2. **AFF-02** — emotion-regulation architecture, strategies, context, flexibility, implementation, and outcome validity;
3. **AFF-03** — temporal dynamics, inertia, variability, recovery, mixed states, interpersonal regulation, synchrony, and ecological measurement.

The question is not whether affective science is complete. The question is whether the current foundation prevents the major inference errors that would otherwise contaminate psychological reasoning.

## 2. Integrated architecture

A generic affective episode can be analyzed through distinct layers:

\`event/context -> appraisal/meaning -> affective experience + bodily/action/expression changes -> regulation attempts -> temporal evolution -> interpersonal/contextual feedback\`

This is a **reasoning scaffold**, not a mandatory serial biological pathway.

Each arrow can be:
- bidirectional;
- moderated by prior learning, goals, culture, physiology, social context, and time;
- measured with different channels;
- altered by regulation or new events.

No single layer can stand for the whole system.

## 3. Core construct map

| Construct | Primary question | Common overreach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core affect | How pleasant/unpleasant and activated is the state? | Valence/arousal = complete emotion |
| Emotion category | What recurring affective meaning/action pattern is being labeled? | Category = fixed biological fingerprint |
| Appraisal | What meaning does the situation have relative to goals, agency, certainty, control, norms? | Appraisal correlation = unique cause |
| Feeling | What is consciously experienced/reported? | Report = whole emotion |
| Expression | What facial/vocal/postural behavior occurs? | Expression = direct inner-state readout |
| Physiology | What bodily response changes occur? | Physiology = unique emotion detector |
| Regulation | What process attempts to alter emotion/its consequences? | Strategy label = adaptive value |
| Dynamics | How does affect change over time? | One temporal coefficient = trait |
| Interpersonal regulation | How do people influence affect through interaction? | Synchrony = beneficial co-regulation |

## 4. Emotion is multicomponent, not a single latent meter

AFF-01 established that subjective experience, appraisal, physiology, expression, behavior, and observer inference can partially covary without being interchangeable.

A valid conclusion should state the measured component.

Examples:
- "reported negative affect decreased";
- "skin conductance increased";
- "observers labeled the face as anger";
- "negative-affect inertia was higher at the sampled lag".

Avoid the unsupported compression:
**"the emotion increased/decreased"**
when only one channel was measured.

## 5. Dimensional versus categorical emotion structure

AFF-01 found no need to choose a universal winner between:
- dimensional models;
- discrete/category models;
- appraisal/component-process models;
- constructionist/high-dimensional models.

Current methods review emphasizes that inferred structure depends on:
- stimulus sampling;
- rating task;
- language/labels;
- algorithm;
- dimensionality criterion;
- whether experience, expression, concepts, or perception are studied.

Therefore:

**A dataset supporting valence/arousal does not prove emotion categories are meaningless. A categorical classification result does not prove invariant natural kinds.**

## 6. Appraisal evidence: strong information, nonunique mapping

The 2024 meta-analysis of appraisal–emotion relations synthesized:
- 2,634 effect sizes;
- 309 studies;
- 47 appraisal dimensions;
- 63 emotions;
- 853 appraisal–emotion relationships.

Most previously hypothesized relationships were supported, but many unpredicted relationships also appeared.

Interpretation:
- appraisals carry substantial explanatory information;
- mappings are probabilistic and overlapping;
- appraisal theories are supported in broad structure, not as one-to-one deterministic decoders.

No contradiction with dimensional models follows.

## 7. Event is not emotion

The same event can produce different emotional outcomes because of:
- goal relevance;
- expectation;
- agency/blame;
- perceived control;
- prior learning;
- culture;
- social meaning;
- current physiology/state.

Conversely, different situations can yield similar emotion labels.

Therefore:
**event category -> emotion**
is an invalid unique inference unless appraisal/context evidence is available.

## 8. Arousal boundary

Current NIMH RDoC defines arousal as a continuum of sensitivity/activation and states that arousal is distinct from motivation and valence while capable of covarying with them.

AFF-01 further established that subjective, autonomic, motor, and neural arousal measures can diverge.

Thus:
- high heart rate ≠ high subjective arousal by definition;
- high arousal ≠ fear;
- low arousal ≠ no emotion;
- arousal ≠ motivation.

## 9. Experience, expression, and perception

AFF-01 separated:
1. first-person experience;
2. facial/vocal/postural expression;
3. observer emotion inference;
4. eliciting context.

The 2025 face–context meta-analysis found large effects of both faces and visual context on emotion labeling.

This blocks a direct-readout model:
**face -> objectively known internal emotion**.

Expression remains useful evidence but is probabilistic and context-dependent.

## 10. Physiology: component evidence, not fingerprint

Autonomic and other physiological measures can identify dimensions of activation, orienting, threat/reward engagement, or response tendency.

But AFF-01 found no basis for a universal one-to-one autonomic fingerprint for common emotion categories.

The correct inference form is:

**physiological measure + baseline + context + convergent channels -> constrained probabilistic interpretation**

not:

**physiological measure -> emotion category**.

## 11. Emotion generation versus regulation

AFF-02 resolved a common category error:

Affective change does not automatically imply intentional regulation.

Emotion can change through:
- new information;
- habituation;
- learning;
- fatigue;
- event termination;
- spontaneous recovery;
- social input;
- regulation.

To infer regulation, identify:
- regulation goal;
- selected strategy;
- implementation;
- temporal relation;
- target outcome.

## 12. Regulation taxonomy is mechanism-first

AFF-02 uses the process model's broad families:
1. situation selection;
2. situation modification;
3. attentional deployment;
4. cognitive change;
5. response modulation.

The extended regulation cycle additionally separates:
- identification;
- strategy selection;
- implementation;
- monitoring.

This prevents "strategy frequency" from being treated as a complete measure of regulation competence.

## 13. Strategy labels do not have fixed adaptive signs

A core result of AFF-02:

**reappraisal ≠ always adaptive; suppression ≠ always maladaptive; avoidance ≠ always pathological; distraction ≠ denial; acceptance ≠ passivity.**

Strategy effects depend on:
- goal;
- intensity;
- controllability;
- culture;
- timing;
- cognitive resources;
- social setting;
- outcome channel;
- time horizon.

A strategy can improve subjective affect while worsening memory or social communication, or vice versa.

## 14. Daily-life regulation substantially depends on situation difficulty

The 2026 coordinated analysis of 12 ambulatory datasets included:
- 1,511 participants;
- 161,513 measurement occasions.

Acceptance, reappraisal, and social sharing were favorable on average, but substantial between-person variability occurred for every strategy.

Most importantly, approximately **75–80% of the variance in estimated strategy effectiveness was explained by intensity of the state being regulated**.

Therefore raw strategy–outcome association is heavily confounded by situation difficulty.

## 15. Reverse causality in regulation research

High-intensity episodes may trigger:
- rumination;
- suppression;
- polyregulation;
- strategy switching.

The same high-intensity states are also harder to regulate.

Thus:
**strategy used -> later distress**
does not uniquely imply strategy caused distress.

Naturalistic regulation models should include prior affect, intensity, selection propensity, concurrent strategies, and relevant context.

## 16. Cognitive control is a contributor, not the definition of emotion regulation

The 2026 meta-analysis of 215 studies found small associations between updating/inhibition and some emotion-regulation measures.

This is consistent with cognitive-control contribution without supporting identity:

**emotion regulation ≠ executive function**.

Reappraisal, acceptance, social regulation, situation change, and expression involve processes beyond generic laboratory control scores.

## 17. Regulatory flexibility needs validated fit, not strategy count

AFF-02 separates:
- repertoire;
- switching;
- expressive flexibility;
- strategy–situation fit;
- regulation ability.

A 2026 meta-analysis of expressive flexibility found small associations with some mental-health outcomes, but substantial heterogeneity, prediction intervals including zero, and predominantly cross-sectional evidence.

Therefore:
**more switching / more strategies / more expressive flexibility = healthier**
is not a valid universal rule.

## 18. Temporal dynamics are descriptive properties

AFF-03 distinguishes:
- mean affect;
- variability;
- instability;
- inertia;
- reactivity;
- recovery;
- recurrence;
- transition;
- synchrony.

These parameters describe different properties of a time series.

Do not substitute one for another.

## 19. Emotional inertia is not emotional stability

Affective inertia is typically modeled as temporal carry-over at a chosen lag.

The 2026 methodological review identifies major problems with:
- inertia–stability conflation;
- negative/oscillatory dynamics;
- ordinal/bounded scales;
- temporal resolution;
- timescale mismatch;
- cross-level inference;
- stationarity assumptions.

Therefore an autoregressive coefficient has no context-free psychological meaning.

## 20. Sampling design is part of the construct

The same affective process can produce different estimates under:
- 5-minute sampling;
- 2-hour sampling;
- once-daily reports.

Thus dynamic claims must name the timescale.

"High negative-affect inertia" is incomplete without:
- sampling interval;
- duration;
- measurement scale;
- model;
- context.

## 21. Recovery is not identical to inertia

Recovery requires:
- a defined perturbation;
- a reference/baseline;
- post-event trajectory.

Autocorrelation can reflect persistence without identifying a specific event.

Event exposure, reactivity, and recovery must be separated before explaining sustained negative affect.

## 22. State-dependent/asymmetric dynamics

AFF-03 incorporated 2026 evidence that persistence can differ depending on whether affect is above or below a person's usual level.

This blocks a strong symmetry assumption.

One linear autoregressive coefficient may average:
- persistence of beneficial states;
- slow recovery from adverse states.

Dynamic models should follow the theory and data rather than forcing one parameter.

## 23. Mixed emotions are not PA–NA correlation

Mixed emotion requires co-occurring positive and negative components.

A correlation between positive and negative affect across time is not direct evidence of simultaneous mixed emotion.

Also:
- simultaneous mixed affect;
- rapid alternation;
- ambivalence;
- emotion complexity

are distinct constructs.

## 24. Mixed emotion has no invariant adaptive sign

AFF-03 incorporated a large 2025 EMA study showing associations of mixed emotions with later outcomes varied by stress context, outcome, and time horizon.

Therefore mixed emotion cannot be universally labeled:
- mature/resilient;
- conflicted/pathological.

Context is required.

## 25. Synchrony is not co-regulation

Two people's affective or physiological signals can covary because of:
- shared event exposure;
- common environment;
- mutual influence;
- stable similarity;
- measurement artifact.

Co-regulation additionally requires a regulatory influence process and functional target.

The 2026 review of interpersonal physiological synchrony notes that psychological meaning remains ambiguous across a heterogeneous literature.

Thus:
**synchrony -> successful regulation**
is invalid.

## 26. Interpersonal regulation must specify direction and goal

For interpersonal emotion regulation identify:
- regulator;
- target;
- strategy;
- direction;
- regulation goal;
- outcome;
- shared context.

Social sharing, reassurance, validation, advice, humor, co-rumination, emotional contagion, and synchrony are not interchangeable.

SOC/REL will deepen these mechanisms.

## 27. Measurement hierarchy

For any affective claim, select the measurement target:

| Target | Preferential evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Conscious feeling | momentary/validated self-report |
| Appraisal | direct appraisal ratings/manipulations |
| Expression | behavioral/facial/vocal coding |
| Physiology | channel-specific physiological measure |
| Strategy use | momentary report/observed instruction or behavior |
| Strategy ability | experimentally supported implementation |
| Strategy effectiveness | target outcome change with valid comparator |
| Dynamics | adequate repeated measurement + temporal model |
| Interpersonal influence | dyadic temporal design + directionality |
| Emotion perception | observer inference task |

No row automatically substitutes for another.

## 28. Multimethod convergence is useful; disagreement is informative

If self-report, behavior, and physiology agree, confidence can increase.

If they disagree, do not average them into a fictional single "emotion intensity" without theory.

Disagreement can reveal:
- channel-specific regulation;
- measurement noise;
- timing differences;
- distinct components.

Cross-channel divergence is data.

## 29. Reliability, validity, and transport remain separate

A reliable emotion questionnaire may not:
- track momentary experience;
- predict physiology;
- generalize across cultures;
- identify causal mechanisms.

A highly accurate classifier may exploit:
- context;
- identity;
- stimulus artifacts;
- label conventions.

A sophisticated time-series model may still model the wrong construct.

**Measurement sophistication cannot replace construct validity.**

## 30. Group evidence versus individual mechanism

Population-level findings can generate hypotheses, not unique individual explanations.

For person-level inference require:
- relevant observation;
- repeated/contextual evidence;
- alternative mechanisms;
- measurement validity;
- uncertainty.

Do not infer stable emotional style from:
- one facial expression;
- one questionnaire;
- one dynamic estimate;
- one lab regulation trial.

## 32. Red-team inference cases

**Case A — high heart rate + negative report.**
Could reflect anxiety, anger, exertion, orienting, pain, medication, or other causes. Conclusion: elevated physiological activation plus reported negative state; category unresolved.

**Case B — smiling during an unpleasant situation.**
Could reflect masking, politeness, mixed affect, regulation, social signaling, or genuine positive affect. Conclusion: smile alone cannot establish internal valence.

**Case C — frequent reappraisal + poor mood.**
Could reflect high exposure/intensity causing more regulation attempts. Conclusion: frequency is not effectiveness.

**Case D — lower distress after suppression.**
Suppression may have reduced subjective distress in that context; memory/social/physiological outcomes remain separate.

**Case E — high day-to-day inertia.**
Check mean affect, event exposure, sampling interval, innovation variance, state dependence, and stationarity before inferring rigidity.

**Case F — high affect variability.**
Could reflect adaptive context tracking, major environmental variation, or dysregulation. Variability alone is descriptive.

**Case G — positive/negative affect both elevated.**
Could be mixed emotion if simultaneous measurement supports co-occurrence; a correlation across days is insufficient.

**Case H — partners show physiological synchrony.**
Could reflect common stimulus or mutual coupling; co-regulation requires directional/process evidence.

**Case I — emotion classifier 90% accurate.**
Need external dataset, participant-independent validation, context-only baseline, and label validity before claiming emotion decoding.

## 33. Cross-domain boundaries

1. **LEA:** learned cue/outcome relations, extinction, habit and predictive updating.
2. **MOT:** value, wanting, effort, goal pursuit, action allocation.
3. **EXE/MET:** control implementation, monitoring, confidence.
4. **AFF:** emotion generation, appraisal, expression, regulation, temporal affect dynamics.
5. **STR:** stressor exposure, allostasis, coping, resilience/adaptation.
6. **SOC/REL:** interpersonal perception, support, attachment, conflict, co-regulation.
7. **PER:** stable individual differences and traits.
8. **DEV:** developmental changes in affective systems/regulation.
9. **NEU:** neural implementation and causal circuitry.
10. **PSYCHOMETRICS:** individual score validity and measurement invariance.
11. **CLN/BHC:** psychopathology and intervention efficacy.

This routing prevents AFF from absorbing every psychological process involving feelings.

## 34. Why STR is the next dependency

AFF can describe emotional responses to threat, frustration, loss, and regulatory attempts.

It does not yet sufficiently distinguish:
- **stressor** from **stress response**;
- acute from chronic stress;
- threat appraisal from physiological stress systems;
- homeostasis from allostasis/allostatic load;
- coping from emotion regulation;
- resilience from absence of distress;
- adaptation from recovery;
- stress exposure from trauma.

These are distinct scientific dependencies and belong to STR.

## 35. RDoC boundary to STR

Current NIMH RDoC distributes relevant functions across:
- Negative Valence Systems;
- Arousal/Regulatory Systems;
- Positive Valence Systems;
- Cognitive Systems;
- Social Processes.

RDoC explicitly recognizes interaction among domains and multiple units of analysis.

Therefore the next STR block should not define "stress" as simply high arousal, negative emotion, or anxiety.

## 36. Integrated Emotion Inference Gate — 48 questions

### A. Construct
1. What is the exact target: affect, emotion, mood, feeling, appraisal, expression, regulation, or dynamic property?
2. Is it first-person experience or observer inference?
3. What is the timescale?
4. Is the target dimensional, categorical, or both?
5. Is arousal channel specified?
6. Is the event/context known?

### B. Generation and appraisal
7. What goal/relevance appraisal is involved?
8. Are certainty and expectedness distinguished?
9. Are agency/blame and control distinguished?
10. Is appraisal measured or retrospectively inferred?
11. Could different appraisals explain the same event?
12. Could motivation/value account for part of the pattern?

### C. Components
13. Is subjective experience directly measured?
14. Is physiology channel-specific?
15. Is expression separated from experience?
16. Is observer perception separated from expression production?
17. Are culture/context/display rules relevant?
18. Could non-emotional causes explain the measured signal?

### D. Regulation
19. Is regulation intentional/goal-directed or merely inferred from change?
20. What is the regulation goal?
21. Which process stage: identification, selection, implementation, monitoring?
22. What exact strategy/tactic is used?
23. Is implementation fidelity known?
24. Is strategy frequency separated from ability?
25. Is effectiveness tied to a named outcome?
26. Is immediate relief separated from long-term consequences?
27. Are intensity and controllability measured?
28. Are concurrent strategies/polyregulation considered?
29. Could reverse causality explain strategy–outcome association?

### E. Dynamics
30. Is mean affect modeled?
31. Is variability separated from instability?
32. Is inertia separated from recovery?
33. Is a perturbation/event identified?
34. Does sampling frequency match the process?
35. Are irregular intervals and missingness handled?
36. Are stationarity/distribution assumptions plausible?
37. Is state-dependent/nonlinear dynamics relevant?
38. Are within-person and between-person effects separated?
39. Is mixed co-occurrence separated from rapid alternation?

### F. Interpersonal
40. Is synchrony being substituted for co-regulation?
41. Is common event exposure controlled?
42. Is directionality identifiable?
43. Are sharing and co-rumination separated?
44. Are regulator/target goals measured?

### G. Validity
45. Does the measurement method validly represent the exact construct?
46. Is a population result being assigned to an individual?
47. What competing model/process remains viable?
48. What is the narrowest claim justified by the design?

## 38. Evidence confidence map

### High-confidence practical boundaries

- emotion is multicomponent;
- arousal is distinct from valence and motivation;
- experience, expression, physiology, and perception are noninterchangeable;
- appraisal systematically relates to emotion differentiation;
- strategy value depends on target/context/outcome;
- strategy frequency is not effectiveness;
- inertia is not stability;
- synchrony is not co-regulation;
- temporal and cross-level measurement assumptions matter.

### Supported but context-sensitive

- reappraisal and acceptance can reduce negative experience;
- suppression can alter experience in some contexts while affecting other outcomes differently;
- emotion-regulation strategies can contribute to affective persistence;
- flexible expression/strategy use may relate to functioning;
- mixed emotions may have context-dependent consequences;
- interpersonal regulation can support or worsen outcomes depending on process.

### Not established as universal

- one emotion taxonomy is uniquely correct at all levels;
- one physiological/facial signal identifies a specific emotion;
- one strategy is always adaptive;
- one dynamic metric diagnoses emotional health;
- high synchrony means better relationships;
- daily-life correlations establish causal regulation mechanisms.

## Canonical operating rule

**Define affective target and timescale -> identify appraisal/context -> separate experience/expression/physiology/perception -> identify regulation goal/process and implementation -> measure relevant outcome channels -> model temporal dynamics at the correct resolution -> distinguish interpersonal influence from synchrony -> test validity and rival models -> make only the narrowest supported inference.**
