## 3. Thermoregulation: core model

Exercise converts most metabolic energy into heat rather than external mechanical work.

A bounded heat-balance model is:

`stored heat = metabolic heat production - external work ± radiation ± convection ± conduction - evaporation`.

Relevant environmental variables include:

- air temperature;
- radiant heat;
- humidity/water-vapor pressure;
- airflow;
- clothing;
- water exposure;
- solar load.

Relevant person/task variables include:

- metabolic rate;
- body size/composition;
- acclimation state;
- hydration status;
- clothing/equipment;
- exercise duration/intensity.

In hot conditions, dry heat exchange can shrink or reverse as ambient/skin temperature gradients narrow.

At sufficiently high environmental heat load, evaporation becomes the dominant available heat-loss pathway.

Project rule:

**ambient temperature alone is not a complete measure of thermal stress.**

---

## 4. Sweating and evaporative cooling

Sweating is a thermoeffector.

But:

`sweat produced` ≠ `sweat evaporated`.

Effective evaporative heat loss depends on:

- skin-to-air water-vapor pressure gradient;
- humidity;
- airflow;
- clothing;
- exposed skin area;
- sweat distribution/dripping;
- local skin temperature.

The 2025 sweat-evaporation review confirms that environmental humidity, airflow and clothing materially determine whether sweat actually evaporates.

The 2025 cycling study at 33°C showed that increasing humidity progressively:

- reduced maximum environmental evaporative capacity;
- reduced sweating efficiency;
- increased skin/core thermal strain;
- reduced self-paced power at high/very-high humidity.

Project rule:

**high sweat rate can coexist with poor cooling when evaporation is constrained.**

Sweat rate is therefore not a stand-alone heat-tolerance score.

## 5. Heat acclimation

Repeated heat exposure can produce physiological adaptations.

The 2025 quantitative review across 211 papers found average heat-acclimation effects including:

- lower resting and end-exercise heart rate;
- lower resting and end-exercise core temperature;
- plasma-volume expansion;
- higher whole-body sweat rate;
- improved exercise capacity/performance in heat.

The 2026 comparative meta-analysis found exercise in heat produced greater improvements than thermoneutral exercise for several heat-response outcomes and aerobic performance in heat.

However adaptations depend on:

- number of exposures;
- duration per exposure;
- environmental temperature/humidity;
- controlled-hyperthermia vs constant-work approach;
- training status;
- prior climate exposure.

A 2026 small RCT also showed measurable cardiovascular/thermoregulatory adaptations after 5 days, demonstrating that some adaptations can begin quickly.

Project rule:

**heat acclimation is a dose-dependent adaptation process, not a single fixed protocol.**

Boundary:

- early partial adaptation ≠ complete acclimation;
- sweat-rate increase is one adaptation, not the whole phenotype;
- protocol-specific averages should not be turned into a universal individual prescription here.

Detailed heat-preparation programming remains GPP/PRO.

## 6. Cold response

Acute cold exposure prioritizes core-temperature defense.

Major responses include:

- cutaneous vasoconstriction;
- reduced peripheral skin blood flow;
- behavioral thermoregulation;
- increased metabolic heat production;
- shivering when required;
- sympathetic activation.

This protects core temperature at a cost:

- lower peripheral skin/muscle/nerve temperature;
- poorer manual dexterity;
- reduced local strength/power;
- increased thermal discomfort;
- increased energetic cost when shivering occurs.

The 2025 cold-air study found 8°C exposure reduced skin blood flow/temperature and impaired dexterity/strength; eight repeated exposures did not reliably reverse those impairments.

The 2025 0°C/10°C study similarly found dose-related handgrip/dexterity impairment, partially reversed by brief rewarming.

Cold-performance effect is therefore not uniform.

It depends on:

- actual skin/muscle/core cooling;
- wind/wetness;
- clothing;
- duration;
- exercise-generated heat;
- task type;
- exposed body region.

Project rule:

**cold stress can preserve core temperature while degrading peripheral neuromuscular performance.**

Cold-air exercise must not be equated with cold-water immersion, which produces much faster heat transfer.

## 7. Fluid balance

Exercise fluid balance is dynamic.

Body water is influenced by:

- sweat loss;
- respiratory water loss;
- drinking;
- urine;
- substrate-associated water;
- environmental conditions.

In heat, sweating can produce large fluid losses.

Dehydration can increase physiological strain through combinations of:

- lower plasma volume;
- greater cardiovascular strain;
- higher core temperature for a given workload in some contexts;
- reduced sweat output at larger deficits;
- higher perceived effort.

But current evidence does not support a deterministic universal performance threshold.

The 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of oral hydration during ≥60 min endurance exercise found:

- no clear time-trial/fixed-duration performance benefit of hydration vs minimal/no fluid in the small available evidence;
- time-to-exhaustion estimates favored hydration but were imprecise;
- lower average core temperature and HR with hydration, with high heterogeneity;
- no clear performance superiority of programmed/individualized over self-regulated drinking;
- **very low certainty** for critical/important estimates.

Therefore:

**hydration can reduce thermophysiological strain, but exact performance benefit and optimal dosing are context dependent.**

Additional boundary:

**adequate hydration cannot fully prevent heat accumulation when metabolic/environmental heat load exceeds evaporative capacity.**

Overdrinking and exercise-associated hyponatremia are MED/NUT safety issues rather than reasons to prescribe “as much water as possible.”

## 8. Cardiovascular drift

During prolonged constant-work exercise, especially in heat:

- heart rate tends to rise progressively;
- stroke volume tends to fall;
- cardiac output may be maintained initially or become compromised under greater heat/dehydration strain;
- relative physiological intensity rises despite unchanged external workload.

Heat can amplify this through:

- hyperthermia;
- high skin blood flow;
- reduced central blood volume;
- reduced ventricular filling time as HR rises;
- dehydration/plasma-volume loss.

Human heat studies show substantial HR rise and stroke-volume decline across prolonged exercise, associated with reduced VO2max.

Project rule:

**cardiovascular drift is an integrated prolonged-exercise response, not a direct dehydration meter.**

Practical implication:

A fixed HR target in prolonged hot exercise can represent a changing absolute workload, and a fixed absolute workload can represent rising relative intensity.

Detailed prescription remains PRO/GPP/TST.

## 9. Heat, humidity and performance

Heat raises physiological cost through:

- greater skin blood flow requirement;
- greater sweat requirement;
- higher thermal discomfort;
- greater cardiovascular strain;
- progressive hyperthermia when heat loss is insufficient.

Humidity specifically reduces evaporative capacity.

The 2025 trained-cyclist study showed:

- very-high humidity markedly reduced evaporative capacity and sweating efficiency;
- peak core temperature increased;
- self-paced power output fell.

The 2026 network meta-analysis similarly found acute heat exposure increased HR/core/skin temperature and impaired exercise performance across pooled studies.

Project rule:

**humidity is a performance-relevant heat-stress variable because it changes evaporation, not merely comfort.**

Performance effects depend on:

- event duration/intensity;
- self-paced vs fixed workload;
- acclimation;
- clothing;
- airflow;
- body size;
- hydration;
- starting temperature.

No single temperature/humidity cutoff is used here as a universal performance boundary.

## 10. Heat-illness boundary

Normal exercise heat strain can include:

- sweating;
- high HR;
- high skin temperature;
- thermal discomfort;
- progressive core-temperature increase.

But exertional heat illness is not simply “more normal heat strain.”

Current ACSM/WMS guidance treats exertional heat stroke as a **medical emergency** requiring rapid recognition and cooling.

PHY-20 therefore does not diagnose or manage:

- exertional heat exhaustion;
- exertional heat injury;
- exertional heat stroke;
- exercise-associated hyponatremia;
- heat-related renal injury;
- medication-related heat intolerance.

Project rule:

**suspected exertional heat illness crosses immediately into MED.**

Hydration is a risk modifier, not a guarantee against heat illness.

## 12. Established project-level model

### Heat

`metabolic heat production + environmental heat load`
`→ skin blood flow + sweating`
`→ evaporation-dependent heat loss`.

If:

`required evaporation > environmental evaporative capacity`

then:

`body heat storage ↑`
`→ thermal + cardiovascular strain ↑`
`→ sustainable performance ↓`.

### Hydration

`sweat loss - fluid replacement`
modifies:

`plasma volume + cardiovascular strain + thermoregulation`

but hydration does not remove the environmental evaporative ceiling.

### Cold

`cold exposure`
`→ vasoconstriction + thermogenesis`
`→ core protection`
while potentially causing
`peripheral cooling → neuromuscular/manual-performance impairment`.

## 13. Training-relevant implications

1. **Do not use air temperature alone to judge heat stress.**
2. **Do not equate sweating with cooling; evaporation is the relevant heat-loss step.**
3. **Account for humidity, airflow, solar/radiant load and clothing.**
4. **Heat acclimation can materially reduce heat strain, but response is protocol/person dependent.**
5. **Do not use one fixed number of heat sessions as proof of complete acclimation.**
6. **Do not assume hydration prevents heat accumulation when evaporation is limited.**
7. **Do not treat a fixed percentage body-mass loss as a deterministic performance threshold across all exercise.**
8. **Do not prescribe overdrinking; fluid strategy belongs to NUT/GPP/MED context.**
9. **Interpret rising HR during prolonged hot exercise partly through cardiovascular drift.**
10. **Cold can impair peripheral force/dexterity even if core temperature is defended.**
11. **Heat-illness symptoms are MED territory, not normal-training tolerance.**

## 15. Confidence / unresolved issues

### HIGH

- thermoregulation depends on heat production and heat exchange, not air temperature alone;
- sweating only cools effectively when sweat evaporates;
- humidity reduces evaporative capacity;
- heat acclimation lowers thermal/cardiovascular strain on average;
- cold causes cutaneous vasoconstriction and can impair peripheral manual/neuromuscular performance;
- cardiovascular drift involves rising HR and falling stroke volume during prolonged exercise;
- heat/humidity can impair endurance/self-paced performance;
- exertional heat stroke is a medical emergency.

### MODERATE-HIGH

- plasma-volume expansion and higher sweat rate are common heat-acclimation adaptations;
- dehydration often increases thermophysiological strain;
- heat acclimation improves heat performance on average;
- short repeated cold-air exposure does not reliably improve peripheral function.

### MODERATE / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

- exact performance effect of a given fluid strategy;
- exact dehydration threshold at which one individual loses performance;
- optimal sodium/fluid dose;
- exact number/duration of exposures needed for complete heat acclimation;
- magnitude of cardiovascular drift for a specific person/workload;
- cold-performance response for a specific task/environment.
