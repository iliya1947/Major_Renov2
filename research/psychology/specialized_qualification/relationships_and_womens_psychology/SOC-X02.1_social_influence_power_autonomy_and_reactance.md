E1. Bond (2005), Group Size and Conformity. https://doi.org/10.1177/1368430205056464 . Direct publisher abstract: meta-analysis of 125 Asch-type studies. Association of majority size with conformity differs by public/private responding and experimental paradigm. Normative and informational processes are proposed explanations, not uniquely identified motives. Exact pooled coefficients and uncertainty not checked. No general rule for interpersonal compliance.

E2. Quantifying compliance and acceptance through public and private social conformity (2018). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6204883/ . Direct selected methods, results and discussion. Within-participant color-matching task; 22 young adult women recruited, 21 retained. Both public and private response shifts reported; public shifts greater than private. Authors operationalize private shift as informational influence and additional public shift as normative influence. Difference between estimated magnitudes of these components was not statistically significant (reported p=.303). Small homogeneous task-development sample; interpretation depends on assumption that private responding removes normative influence. Results text gives some inconsistent degrees of freedom relative to retained sample; no recalculation of effects. Private response is not proof of durable internalization or autonomy. Do not generalize to a person's intimate choices.

E3. Rains (2013), The Nature of Psychological Reactance Revisited: a Meta-Analytic Review. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2012.01443.x . Direct publisher abstract. Twenty studies, reported N=4,942. Among tested path models, latent reactance represented by anger and counterargument indicators fit best. Model fit is not unique causal identification; no universal diagnostic marker or individual inference. Coefficients and competing model details not checked.

E4. Li & Shi (2026 issue, online 2025), Message effects on psychological reactance: meta-analyses. https://doi.org/10.1093/hcr/hqaf016 ; https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/message-effects-on-psychological-reactance-meta-analyses/ . Complete institutional abstract and selected publisher background/limitations inspected. 28 articles, 33 studies, 146 effect sizes (2005–2024). Greater freedom-threatening language associated with increased anger (r=.21), negative cognitions (.17) and reactance (.20). Gain/loss framing showed no statistically significant differences for these outcomes (r=-.03, -.01, -.02 respectively). Counterevidence to treating all framing changes as interchangeable; null findings are not equivalence. Communication modality and repetition moderated findings. No full confidence intervals, dependence or bias analysis audited. Health/prosocial message effects are not estimates for personal relationships; no instructions to suppress resistance.

E5. Words that trigger: a meta-analysis of threatening language, reactance, and persuasion in health (2025). https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqaf004 . Complete publisher abstract directly inspected. 35 studies, combined N=10,658; synthesis broadly consistent with reactance theory but substantial heterogeneity and reported moderators. Exact effects and subgroup tests not verified. Potential primary-study overlap with E4 and E3; not independent pooled replication. Distinguish perceived threat, reactance measures and persuasion outcomes. No causal chain inferred from pooled association alone.

E6. Don & Hammond (2017), Social Support in Intimate Relationships: The Role of Relationship Autonomy. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167217705119 . Complete publisher abstract directly inspected. Couples (reported N=80) studied in support interactions. Greater autonomous motivation of support seekers was associated with more direct/positive support seeking and partner support; autonomy of both roles also predicted subjective evaluations. Observational mediation/prediction does not establish causal effect; N unit, exact coding, coefficients and uncertainty not independently checked beyond abstract. Support seeking and free choice can coexist, but receiving support is not evidence of romantic commitment.

E7. Why are we together? A dyadic longitudinal investigation of relationship motivation, goal progress, and adjustment (2019 online). https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12503 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31420868/ . Indexed original abstract: prospective design, 153 heterosexual couples; own autonomous relationship motivation positively associated with own relationship and self-oriented goal progress. Direct PubMed CAPTCHA and Wiley 403. Timing, full partner effects, confounders and uncertainty not verified; no claim of causal change or universal autonomy benefit.

E8. Körner & Schütz (2021), Power in romantic relationships: How positional and experienced power are associated with relationship quality. https://doi.org/10.1177/02654075211017670 ; https://extern.fis.uni-bamberg.de/en/entities/publication/53351964-36b7-43f7-8bf6-8fa6c8825668/ . ## 3. Construct map and alternatives
Social influence describes changes in judgments or conduct associated with others. Informational influence concerns the informational relevance of others' views; normative influence concerns expectations and social consequences. These can coexist with prior preferences, role duties, opportunity and independently reached conclusions. Public agreement, private belief, action, enduring internalization and voluntary endorsement are different outcomes. A refusal does not diagnose reactance. Anger, counterarguments, perceived threat, restoration of choice and actual safety are distinct.

Persuasion can offer reasons and accurate information while leaving choice intact. Autonomy concerns self-endorsed action and meaningful ability to refuse; it is not isolation, refusal of all help or absence of interdependence. Separate perceived autonomy, actual alternatives, resource constraints, positional power, perceived influence, desired influence, consent, preference and actual conduct. Agreement under credible threat cannot be assumed freely given. Even polite wording cannot make a coercive situation voluntary.

Candidate explanations for agreement: independent preference, genuine belief revision, shared norms, ordinary coordination, social desirability, practical constraint, pressure, fear or coexistence. Candidate explanations for disagreement: different values, accurate criticism, lack of information, infeasible request, boundary, threat appraisal or changed preference. Neither response identifies a unique motivation. A person's freely expressed choice is actionable without requiring a theory of hidden reasons.

## 4. Operational reasoning
1. Define actual decision, participants, observable act, context, time and evidence channel; distinguish factual information from inference.
2. Identify available alternatives and material consequences of refusal; separate requests and boundaries from threats or retaliation. Do not label disagreement or persuasion inherently coercive.
3. Compare genuinely distinct explanations, including ordinary preferences and constraints. Separate public expression from private endorsement; do not infer endorsement from compliance or resistance from a refusal alone.
4. Use freely volunteered statements, legitimate observations and relevant agreements to revise claims. No deceptive tests, pressure escalation, manufactured social consensus, surveillance or exploiting dependence.
5. Check study population, task, construct, comparator, selection and uncertainty. Experimental effects on group judgments or health-message ratings do not establish individual romantic choices. Meta-analytic model fit is not proof of a causal mechanism.
6. Preserve meaningful refusal, informed choice and safety independent of whether someone agrees. If credible coercion or threats are present, consider safety and appropriate support rather than diagnosing or optimizing compliance.
7. Record what remains unknown and what evidence would alter the inference. No numerical susceptibility scores, personalized influence recipes or tactics to overcome resistance.

## 5. Internal synthetic transfer review
Rubric set for review: construct separation, alternative explanation, discriminating evidence, inference boundary. Critical failures: unsupported motive, causal inflation, deterministic individual transfer, ignoring counterevidence, coercion or privacy breach. Six original neutral examples, not held-out.
A. A committee member votes with the group but later expresses a different opinion. Response: public action and endorsement differ; coordination or social pressure may explain. Voluntary account and actual decision conditions clarify; no certain private motive.
B. A person declines a recommendation with a reasoned objection. Response: refusal and reactance differ; disagreement or inapplicability may suffice. Consider reasons and relevant facts; do not treat refusal as a barrier to overcome.
C. A worker agrees to a schedule after a threat of unjust punishment. Response: agreement and meaningful freedom differ; actual consequences and alternatives matter. Do not infer consent or recommend pressure.
D. A study reports language-related reactance effects but null framing comparisons. Response: distinct manipulations and estimands; heterogeneity and precision matter. Inspect compatible comparisons; no universal causal pathway or equivalence claim.
E. A person freely requests assistance for a chosen goal. Response: reliance and autonomy compatible; personal preference or practical need may explain. Voluntary goals and ability to decline are relevant; no inferred dependency or romance.
F. Two collaborators have unequal resources but both report influence. Response: positional power, experienced power and freedom to refuse differ. Actual decision practices and alternatives matter; no conclusion of equal safety from self-report alone.
